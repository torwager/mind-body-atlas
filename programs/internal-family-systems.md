## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Multiplicity of mind.** The psyche is normally made up of sub-personalities ("parts") organised around a core **Self**. The Self is undamaged and has qualities summarised as the "8 Cs": calm, curiosity, compassion, clarity, confidence, courage, creativity, connectedness.
- **Trauma and burdens.** Adverse experiences leave parts carrying "burdens" (extreme beliefs, emotions and sensations). Vulnerable **exiles** hold pain and shame. Protective parts keep them out of awareness in two ways:
  - **Managers** act pre-emptively (control, perfectionism, criticism, over-functioning).
  - **Firefighters** react when exiled pain breaks through (substance use, bingeing, dissociation, self-harm).
- **Symptoms as protective strategies.** Psychiatric symptoms are reframed as parts' protective activity or as burdens rather than as disorders. Applied to the body, IFS authors propose that protective parts can produce or amplify physical symptoms such as pain, fatigue, gut symptoms and inflammation, or that symptoms carry burdens linked to unresolved trauma and toxic stress.
  - Schwartz encourages "befriending" symptoms as parts with a protective intent.
  - The RA trial authors framed stress and emotion as modulating pain and disease activity.
- **Neurobiology.** No formal neural model is offered. Secondary writing loosely invokes trauma/stress physiology (*The Body Keeps the Score*), memory reconsolidation and the default-mode network, and these claims are not tested.
- Overall, it is a **trauma/emotion-processing and self-relationship model**, not a pain-neuroscience model. It overlaps with TMS/PPD thinking in treating symptoms as protecting against unbearable emotion.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Transdiagnostic, with a single model for all presentations.** The same parts/Self map and the same unburdening process are applied to depression, PTSD, addiction, eating disorders, couples conflict and physical illness. The only differences are in emphasis:
- Specialised Level 2 trainings and books cover trauma, addiction, couples, children and health.
- The 2026 *IFS Health Therapy* book addresses autoimmune, metabolic and digestive conditions through the same lens.

IFS does not distinguish separate mechanisms for named functional syndromes such as ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, IBS or POTS. It does not adopt a central-sensitisation or neuroplastic-pain framework of its own, although TMS/PPD clinicians often combine IFS with those frameworks.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
**Core therapeutic sequence (individual therapy, usually weekly, open-ended; research protocols ran 16 x 90 min sessions):**
1. **Unblending from protectors**, using the "6 Fs": *find* the part (in body or mind), *focus* on it, *flesh it out*, ask how the client *feels toward* it, *befriend* it, and learn what it *fears* would happen if it stopped its role.
2. **Accessing Self energy**, checked by asking "How do you feel toward this part?" until the answer reflects Self qualities.
3. **Gaining permission** from protectors to approach the exile.
4. **Healing the exile:** witnessing its story, "do-over"/re-parenting, retrieval from the past scene, **unburdening** (releasing burdens through imagery of elements such as light, water, fire or wind), then inviting in desired qualities.
5. **Integration:** protectors are invited to take new roles, and polarised parts are negotiated with.

**Self-help and self-care:**
- "Parts check-ins" and daily inner dialogue.
- Somatic "where do you feel it in your body" tracking.
- Journaling to parts.
- Guided meditations from Schwartz (*No Bad Parts* exercises; Sounds True audio).
- IFS Online Circle (9-module course, US$497; up to 25.25 CE credits in the 2023–24 run; Spring and Fall 2026 cohorts offered).

**Applied to chronic symptoms:** treat pain, fatigue or a symptom as a part, ask what it is protecting, and unburden associated exiles. This is done alongside medical care. In the RA trial, IFS was added to usual rheumatology care.

**Variants:**
- **PARTS** (Program for Alleviating and Resolving Trauma and Stress; Schuman-Olivier/Cambridge Health Alliance): a 16-week online group programme plus individual sessions.
- **IFS-informed psychedelic/ketamine-assisted therapy.**
- **Intensives and retreats** (multi-day; about US$7,000–8,500 for some branded retreats).
- **Embodied IFS** (Susan McConnell, 2020), a body-focused branch.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Structural family therapy and systems theory** (Minuchin, Bateson): IFS applies family-systems concepts (roles, alliances, polarisation) to the inner world.
- **Psychodynamic "multiple selves" lineage:**
  - Freud's id/ego/superego
  - Jung's complexes and active imagination
  - Assagioli's psychosynthesis "subpersonalities"
  - Transactional Analysis ego states
  - Satir's "Parts Party"
  - Gestalt empty-chair work
- **Contemplative traditions:** a Buddhist-like witnessing awareness ("Self" as non-judgemental presence), and later explicitly spiritual framings (*The Spirituality of IFS* course).
- **Allied trauma movement:** van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014) promoted IFS strongly.
- **Lineage into mind-body pain care:** used within TMS/PPD practice as an emotion-processing tool, functionally overlapping with EAET and ISTDP.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **SAMHSA NREPP (2015):** rated "effective" for general functioning and wellbeing, and "promising" for:
  - phobia/panic/GAD
  - depression
  - physical health conditions and symptoms
  - personal resilience/self-concept

  The rating rested mainly on the RA trial. NREPP was discontinued in 2018.
- **Clinical uptake:**
  - 45,764 Psychology Today profiles mentioned IFS (April 2024).
  - Used in residential eating-disorder and trauma programmes.
  - The PARTS programme was developed at Cambridge Health Alliance (a Harvard affiliate) with NIH/foundation-supported studies.
- **Guidelines:** IFS is not recommended in NICE, APA or VA/DoD PTSD/depression guidelines, and it does not appear in chronic-pain guidelines.
- **Insurance:** IFS has no specific coverage. It is reimbursable only as generic psychotherapy by licensed providers. Out-of-network superbills are common.

## 6. Evidence base
Trials of IFS itself (all small; most involve the developer or IFS-affiliated investigators, and the Foundation for Self Leadership funded several):

| Study | Design | N | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shadick NA, Sowell NF, … Schwartz RC. *J Rheumatol* 2013;40(11):1831-41. doi:10.3899/jrheum.121465 (NCT00869349) | Proof-of-concept RCT. IFS (9 months; group plus individual sessions) vs mailed education, with usual RA care in both arms. | 79 (39 vs 40) | **Post-treatment:** better overall pain (p=0.04) and physical function (p=0.04). **At 1 year:** better self-rated joint pain, depression and self-compassion. **No sustained improvement** at 1 year in anxiety, self-efficacy or disease activity. Schwartz was a co-author, and the control was weak. |
| Haddock SA, Weiler LM, Trump LJ, Henry KL. *J Marital Fam Ther* 2017;43(1):131-44 (epub 2016). doi:10.1111/jmft.12184 | Pilot RCT. IFS vs treatment-as-usual (CBT/IPT) for depressed college women. | 37 randomised (17 vs 15 analysed) | Depression fell in both arms, with no significant difference between groups in the size or rate of change. |
| Hodgdon HB, Anderson FG, Southwell E, Hrubec W, Schwartz R. *J Aggress Maltreat Trauma* 2022;31(1):22-43. doi:10.1080/10926771.2021.2013375 | Uncontrolled pilot; 16 x 90-min individual IFS sessions. | 17 (13 PTSD-diagnosed completers) | Large pre-post drops in PTSD and depression, plus dissociation and somatisation. At 1-month follow-up 12 of 13 completers (92%) no longer met PTSD criteria. No control arm; developer co-authored. Published online 2021. |
| Comeau et al. *Psychol Trauma* 2024;16(Suppl 3):S636-40 (PMID 38934934) | Single-arm feasibility study of PARTS (16 x 90-min online groups plus 8 individual sessions). | 15 | 73% attended at least 12 groups, and 92% of responders would recommend it. PTSD severity fell (d = -0.7 at week 16, -0.9 at week 24). |
| Ally et al. *Front Psychiatry* 2025;16. doi:10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1544435 | Single-arm pilot of online group IFS for PTSD plus substance use disorder. | 10 (70% retained) | PARTS-SUD: 12 weekly groups plus 6 individual sessions. PTSD (PCL-5) fell by about 1.7 points per week, and 54% reached the minimally important difference. |
| Joss D, Comeau A, … Sweezy M, Schuman-Olivier Z. *Psychol Trauma* 2026 (online 29 Jan 2026). doi:10.1037/tra0002089 (PMID 41609644) | RCT. PARTS vs Nature-Based Stress Reduction (matched active control): 16 weekly groups plus 8 individual sessions. | 60 | Both arms showed large CAPS-5 reductions with **no significant difference between groups**. PARTS had better attendance and satisfaction. A secondary analysis (Joss et al., *Sci Rep* 2026; PMID 42471454) attributes part of the shared benefit in both arms to reduced sense of isolation. Funded by the Foundation for Self Leadership. |
| Habibi N, Basharpoor S, et al. *Child Abuse Negl* 2026;178:108140. doi:10.1016/j.chiabu.2026.108140 (PMID 42241954) | Randomised pre-post-follow-up group study (described as quasi-experimental). IFS-based group vs control for trauma-exposed adolescents in out-of-home care, Iran. | 50 (25 vs 25) | Reductions in post-traumatic cognitions (e.g., negative self-cognitions, eta-squared 0.40) and fear of compassion, maintained at 2 months. Independent of the IFS Institute; control condition not described in the abstract. |

**Reviews:** Buys (2025), *Clinical Psychologist* (scoping review; doi:10.1080/13284207.2025.2533127), found 27 studies: 17 case studies, 5 quasi-experimental studies, 3 qualitative studies and 2 RCTs. It called IFS "promising" (citing chronic pain, depression and PTSD) but said the research "remains limited in scope".

**Gaps:**
- No trials in fibromyalgia, ME/CFS, IBS, headache, FND, long COVID or other functional/overlapping pain conditions.
- No independent replication.
- No dismantling of components.
- Harms are not systematically assessed.
- RA is an inflammatory disease, so the trial shows adjunctive benefit for pain and mood, not modification of disease.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1980s–1990s:** niche within family therapy. Guilford textbook in 1995; *Introduction to the IFS Model* in 2001; Center for Self Leadership founded in 2000.
- **2013–2015:** RA RCT (2013); NREPP listing (2015); *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014; a long-running NYT bestseller) gave IFS mainstream visibility, and IFS promoters often cite Pixar's *Inside Out* (2015).
- **2017 to 2020:** IFS Institute training rose from about 500 to about 1,600 trainees per year. In 2020–21 came a pandemic-era telehealth boom and Schwartz's appearance on Tim Ferriss's podcast. ***No Bad Parts* (Sounds True, 2021)** was a bestseller with an audiobook featuring Alanis Morissette.
- **2023–2025:**
  - Nearly 4,000 trainees per year, a waitlist of about 20,000, full-time staff growing from 12 to 35 and trainers from 30 to more than 100 (The Nation, 2025).
  - In 2024: 45,764 Psychology Today profiles mentioning IFS, about 3 million TikTok posts, and about 75k Instagram posts tagged #internalfamilysystems.
  - Viral "parts work" on social media.
- **2025–2026:** critical coverage (The Nation, April 2025; *The Cut*, October 2025), the first group RCT (2026) and the *IFS Health Therapy* book (2026). Popularity remains very high, with a slight plateau or backlash signal.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Evidence vs claims.** "Evidence-based" marketing far outstrips a small evidence base (Brownstone et al., *Psychotherapy Bulletin* 2024; The Nation 2025).
- **Safety concerns.** Encouraging people to experience themselves as split into parts may destabilise clients with psychosis or dissociative disorders. Rapid "unburdening" may risk iatrogenic or false memories.
- **Castlewood allegations.** Rachel Corbett, "The Truth About IFS, the Therapy That Can Break You," *The Cut* (New York Magazine), 30 October 2025. It reports that Richard Schwartz was hired in the early 2000s by Castlewood Treatment Center co-founder Mark Schwartz (no relation, a former IFS trainee) as a consultant to treat patients and train staff. Four former patients (Lisa Nasseff, first in 2011; Leslie Thompson; Colette Travers; Brooke Taylor) sued Castlewood and Mark Schwartz alleging implanted false memories of abuse; the suits were settled, and Missouri censured Mark Schwartz's licence. A former research director is quoted saying Richard Schwartz "knew fully what was going on". Richard Schwartz was not a defendant. The IFS Institute responded on 4 November 2025: Castlewood was run by Mark Schwartz, Richard Schwartz was never a therapist for Castlewood clients and was never named in any lawsuit, and IFS does not accept recovered memories as fact without corroboration.
- **Spiritual drift.** Later spiritual content (e.g., "unattached burdens," likened to exorcism) has drawn criticism.
- **Blame concerns.** Critics say attributing autoimmune or chronic symptoms to protective parts risks implying that patients cause their own illness.
- **Commercialisation.** Critics point to costly tiered training, mandatory recertification, expensive retreats and a proliferation of unregulated "IFS-informed" coaches.

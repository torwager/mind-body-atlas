## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Disconnection from self due to trauma.** The site's core claim is that the main source of suffering is disconnection from one's "authentic self," usually caused by childhood and intergenerational trauma. Maté defines trauma as the internal wound (what happened *inside* you), not the event itself, and includes "small-t" trauma such as emotional misattunement by stressed parents.
- **Symptoms as adaptations.** Addictions, anxiety, depression, conflicts and relationship patterns are described as coping strategies that once helped the child survive but now cause harm. The "essence" is said to be undamaged.
- **Illness pathway (*When the Body Says No*, 2003; *The Myth of Normal*, 2022).** Chronic hidden stress comes from emotional repression, compulsive caretaking, inability to say "no," suppressed anger and an exaggerated sense of responsibility. This stress dysregulates the HPA axis (cortisol), the autonomic nervous system and immune function (a psychoneuroimmunology framing), raising the risk of autoimmune disease, cancer, neurodegenerative disease (ALS, MS) and functional disorders. *The Myth of Normal* adds a sociocultural layer: a "toxic culture" of materialism, isolation and disconnection is said to make chronic illness and mental-health epidemics "normal."
- **Addiction model (*In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts*, 2008).** "Not why the addiction, but why the pain." Early adversity shapes the development of dopamine, opioid and stress-regulation circuits, and substances or behaviours become self-soothing.
- Mechanisms are mostly asserted through case narratives and citations of ACE, attachment and stress-physiology literature. They are not specified as testable models.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Essentially one, applied transdiagnostically.** Trauma-induced disconnection and chronic emotional suppression underlie addiction, mental illness and physical disease alike. *When the Body Says No* gives disease-specific case chapters (e.g., ALS patients as "nice," self-effacing; breast cancer and repressed anger; RA/lupus and self-denial), but these are variations on the same repression → stress-physiology → disease theme, not separate mechanisms. CI as a therapy is not disorder-focused: the same inquiry process is used whatever the presenting problem. The site does not single out ME/CFS, POTS, MCS and similar conditions as targets.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
**The CI session (clinician-delivered).** The therapist uses attuned, compassionate questioning to reflect the client's experience back to them. Starting from a current trigger or symptom, the therapist follows body sensations, emotions, beliefs and the "story," then traces these to early memories and implicit beliefs (e.g., "I'm not wanted"). The aim is to reveal the unconscious dynamics and their origins, and to restore compassion toward the adaptive self. Elements:
- therapist presence, safety and attunement
- noticing body states and tone of voice
- naming triggers, shame and pain
- inquiring into childhood attachment and adaptation
- examining memories, stories and beliefs
- will, compassion, forgiveness
- relationship and possibility

These are the 8 module topics of the course "CI Map."

**Training structure:**
- **Professional Training:** 1 year, 330 hrs per compassionateinquiry.com (drgabormate.com says 240 hrs) at 8–10 hrs/week. Content runs as 8 two-week modules, repeated across three 16-week levels. 27 facilitated bi-weekly 1.5-hr groups (12–18 people), with pods of ~6 and dyad/triad practice. Trainees must be in ≥12 sessions/yr of personal therapy. Prerequisites: ≥200 hrs of allied-health training plus ≥50 hrs of counselling skills. Completion requires attending ≥22 of the 27 groups.
- **Mentorship:** 6 months, required for the "CI Practitioner" title; candidates need regulatory registration, ≥3 clients/week and liability insurance.
- **Short Course:** "over 30 hours" of material: a self-paced recorded Vancouver workshop (~9 hrs, approx.), 8 narrated module summaries and 10 filmed demonstration sessions.

**Self-care advice from the books.** The "7 A's of healing" from *When the Body Says No*: acceptance, awareness, anger (healthy expression), autonomy, attachment, assertion, affirmation. Other advice:
- learning to say no
- noticing compulsive self-suppression
- self-compassion and emotional expression
- authentic connection and community
- mindful self-inquiry
- addressing social context

Maté has also publicly endorsed supervised psychedelic-assisted (ayahuasca) work, but that is not part of CI training.

**Adjunct (Sat Dharam Kaur's practice):** Kundalini yoga, breathwork and meditation alongside CI.

## 4. Roots and influences
- Psychodynamic/attachment theory: Bowlby, Winnicott, Alice Miller; Gordon Neufeld's attachment model (co-author of *Hold on to Your Kids*, 2004).
- Psychosomatic medicine and psychoneuroimmunology: Selye's stress theory; ACE research (Felitti, Anda); Lydia Temoshok's "Type C" cancer personality.
- Body-oriented trauma therapy: Peter Levine's Somatic Experiencing, Bessel van der Kolk; resemblance to Hakomi-style mindful inquiry (reviewer's interpretation, not a claim by CI).
- Buddhist mindfulness/compassion (Maté cites Buddhist teachings and A.H. Almaas's Diamond Approach; the "essence" language comes from Almaas).
- Resembles Internal Family Systems "parts" adaptation framing (reviewer's interpretation, not a claim by CI).
- Maté's own experience as a Holocaust-era infant survivor in Budapest, and his ADHD diagnosis.
- Through co-director Kaur: Kundalini yoga and naturopathy.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Training uptake:** taken mainly by licensed clinicians (psychotherapists, counsellors, physicians, addiction workers), "thousands in 80+ countries." CE credit: 10 CE (CCPA, Canada) and 30 CE via the Spiritual Competency Academy (a US CE provider) for the Short Course.
- **Clinical standing:** not recommended in any national guideline (NICE, APA, CMS). Maté's ideas influenced the trauma-informed care and harm-reduction movements (e.g., Vancouver's Insite) and are widely read by physicians. *The Myth of Normal* was reviewed in *BJGP* (2024) and *Family Medicine* (2024).
- **Insurance:** no CPT code or payer recognition for "CI." Reimbursement happens only when a licensed provider bills generic psychotherapy codes. Circles and coaching-style practitioners are private pay.

## 6. Evidence base
- **Branded program:** searched 2026-10-12:
  - PubMed "compassionate inquiry": 7 hits (re-run in fact-check, same count). None is about Maté's method; most are International Sex Survey papers and one oncology paper.
  - Europe PMC "compassionate inquiry" AND Maté: only two book reviews of *The Myth of Normal* (Milton, *BJGP* 2024;74(739):83; Fogleman, *Fam Med* 2024;56(1):58–59; both verified).
  - ClinicalTrials.gov: 0 registered studies.

  There are **no peer-reviewed trials, controlled or uncontrolled, of Compassionate Inquiry**. The site makes no research claims and offers only testimonials.
- **Indirect literature Maté cites** is correlational. It shows that childhood adversity predicts later disease, e.g.:
  - Felitti et al. 1998, *Am J Prev Med* (ACE Study)
  - Dube et al. 2009, *Psychosom Med* (ACEs and autoimmune hospitalisation)
  - stress/HPA–immune work

  This does not establish that emotional repression *causes* specific diseases, or that CI reverses them. The personality–disease links in *When the Body Says No* (e.g., ALS, cancer) rest on case anecdotes and older, contested "Type C" studies. Large prospective cohorts have generally found weak or null personality–cancer associations.
- **Related generic techniques** have trial support but are distinct: Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy, ISTDP, compassion-focused therapy and trauma-focused therapies.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **Book sales:**
  - *When the Body Says No* (2003) is a long-running bestseller, translated widely.
  - *In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts* (2008) won the Hubert Evans Prize (2009).
  - *The Myth of Normal* (Sept 2022, with Daniel Maté) was an international bestseller and won the 2023 Vine Award.
- **Film:** *The Wisdom of Trauma* (2021, Science and Nonduality) was free-streamed with ">10 million viewers in 230 countries/territories" (film site).
- **Media:** a podcast staple (e.g., Joe Rogan, Diary of a CEO, Tim Ferriss; approx.) with YouTube talks in the millions of views. The March 2023 livestream with Prince Harry, where Maté publicly "diagnosed" ADD/ADHD, PTSD, anxiety and depression, was a global media spike.
- **CI training:** described as studied by "thousands" in 80+ countries, with three cohorts a year (Feb/May/Sept; verified on drgabormate.com). An annual conference was claimed but not verified.
- **Trajectory (approx.):**
  - 2003–2008: book-driven niche.
  - 2008–2015: rising Canadian profile through addiction work.
  - 2016–2020: structured online training expands.
  - 2021–2023: peak from the film, *The Myth of Normal* and the Harry interview.
  - 2024–2026: sustained high plateau.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Reductionism:** Nick Haslam (University of Melbourne) calls the near-singular emphasis on trauma as the cause of ill health unbalanced. Stanton Peele calls Maté's account of addiction reductionist. James Coyne (U. Pennsylvania) said Maté "urges us to abandon what has evolved to be evidence-based solutions to health and social problems" (quoted on Wikipedia); Coyne has long criticised psychosocial-causation claims about cancer generally. Vincenzo Di Nicola notes that parts of the work are not evidence-based.
- **Patient blame:** framing ALS, cancer or autoimmune disease as linked to "niceness" or repressed anger risks implying patients caused their illness.
- **ADHD as trauma/environment** (*Scattered Minds*) conflicts with the strong genetic evidence on ADHD.
- **Prince Harry interview (2023):** criticised by clinicians for public diagnosis without clinical assessment.
- **No outcome data** for a costly year-long training marketed internationally; certification is self-conferred by the organisation, with no external accreditation of the method itself.
- **Psychedelic advocacy** (ayahuasca) is a separate but often-associated controversy.

## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)

- **Psychological inflexibility, not pain per se, drives suffering and disability.** Pain is treated as real and often not fully removable. Disability and distress come mainly from a person's *struggle* with pain:
  - experiential avoidance (efforts to control, suppress or escape pain, thoughts and feelings);
  - cognitive fusion (taking pain-related thoughts such as "I'll damage myself" literally);
  - narrowing of behaviour away from valued activities.
- **The "hexaflex" model** has six interrelated processes:
  - acceptance vs. experiential avoidance;
  - cognitive defusion vs. fusion;
  - present-moment awareness vs. rigid attention to past and future;
  - self-as-context vs. attachment to a conceptualised self ("I am a pain patient");
  - values vs. lack of values clarity;
  - committed action vs. inaction, impulsivity or avoidance.
- **Theoretical base: Relational Frame Theory (RFT)**, a post-Skinnerian behavioural account of language and cognition. Verbal and relational learning lets aversive private events (pain sensations, memories, predictions) take control of behaviour. Control-based coping (the "agenda of control") is held to be self-amplifying: the more one fights pain, the more life contracts around it.
- **Little neurobiological commitment.** Unlike PRT, DNRS or ANS Rewire, ACT makes no claim that the pain is "brain-generated" or reversible by retraining a danger signal, and it does not target central sensitisation directly. Its outcome target is functioning and valued living. Reduced pain intensity is a welcome by-product, not the goal. Some ACT-pain researchers link the model to fear-avoidance and attention/interoception literatures. Mediation studies (Wicksell 2013; Simister 2018; Trompetter 2015) report changes in psychological inflexibility, pain acceptance and catastrophizing as mediators. EFFIGACT (Luciano 2014) did *not* find acceptance to mediate most outcomes.

## 2. One mechanism or many?

**Transdiagnostic / not disorder-focused.**
- The same six processes are applied to every chronic pain condition. They are also applied to depression, anxiety, tinnitus, psychosis, addiction and more. ACT explicitly rejects syndrome-specific mechanisms as the treatment target.
- **No aetiological claims.** It makes no claim about what *causes* fibromyalgia, back pain or headache. Medical causes are left to medicine; ACT addresses the behavioural/psychological layer common to all.
- **Disorder-specific tailoring is in content only.** Protocols adapt exposure targets, metaphors and activity goals to fibromyalgia, paediatric pain (with parent modules), headache and other conditions.
- **Not extended to ME/CFS, MCS or EHS by the core ACT community.** The core pain literature makes no claims about those conditions, unlike unified "limbic/ANS" programmes. Separate trials of ACT exist for IBS, tinnitus and other conditions, but they are framed as coping and flexibility interventions, not cures.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies

**Typical clinician protocol:** 6–12 weekly sessions, individual or group (60–120 min), or 2–4-week interdisciplinary programmes (Bath model) alongside physiotherapy and occupational therapy. Common components:

1. **Creative hopelessness.** Review the costs and workability of past pain-control attempts (medication escalation, rest, avoidance, doctor-shopping).
2. **Acceptance/willingness exercises.** Make room for pain sensations without struggle. Metaphors include "tug-of-war with a monster," "passengers on the bus" and "quicksand."
3. **Cognitive defusion.** Notice thoughts as thoughts ("I'm having the thought that…"), repeat words until meaning fades, give thoughts names. Change the *function* of thoughts rather than their content (contrast with CBT restructuring).
4. **Mindfulness / present-moment practice.** Brief body-scan and breath exercises; observing pain sensations with curiosity.
5. **Self-as-context.** "Observer self" exercises (chessboard metaphor).
6. **Values clarification.** Domains such as work, family, health and leisure; values compass or bull's-eye worksheets.
7. **Committed action.** Graded, values-linked behavioural activation and exposure to avoided activities. Goal setting; handling setbacks.
8. Homework, practice logs, and sometimes a relapse-prevention booklet.

**Digital formats:**
- Internet ACT (e.g., Buhrman 2013, 7 weeks; Trompetter 2015, ~9–12 weeks; ACTonPain, Lin 2017, 7 modules ± coach guidance).
- **Stanza:** a 12-week self-guided smartphone programme of daily lessons, exercises and symptom tracking, about 15 minutes a day. It requires a prescription and is offered through clinical partners such as Swing Care (a virtual practice), Southwest Medical and UPMC, in 8 US states as of Oct 2026.

**Self-help books:**
- Dahl & Lundgren, *Living Beyond Your Pain* (2006)
- Vowles & Sorrell, *Life with Chronic Pain: An Acceptance-Based Approach* (therapist guide and patient workbook, 2007; freely distributed)
- Harris, *The Happiness Trap* (general, not pain-specific)

## 4. Roots and influences

- **Behaviourism / radical behaviourism (Skinner)** and functional contextualism, via Relational Frame Theory (Hayes, Barnes-Holmes & Roche 2001).
- **CBT (Beck/Ellis):** ACT is a "third-wave" CBT, reacting against first-order cognitive restructuring.
- **Buddhist and Zen-derived mindfulness and acceptance**, mediated through MBSR/MBCT and DBT; the "letting go" and "observer self" ideas.
- **Exposure therapy / fear-avoidance models of pain** (Lethem; Vlaeyen & Linton 2000).
- **Fordyce's operant behavioural model of chronic pain** (1970s) and interdisciplinary pain rehabilitation.
- Humanistic/existential psychology (values, meaning) and Gestalt-style experiential exercises.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance

- **NICE NG193 (April 2021)**, recommendation 1.2.3: consider ACT or CBT for pain for people aged 16+ with chronic primary pain, delivered by appropriately trained professionals. NICE found insufficient evidence to favour one over the other. ACT and CBT were the only psychological therapies recommended. NICE had previously named ACT for tinnitus (NG155, 2020, as one option for group-based psychological intervention; verified).
- **APA Division 12 (Society of Clinical Psychology)** rated ACT for chronic pain "Strong" under its original EST criteria; its page now lists the treatment as "pending re-evaluation" under the 2015 criteria, so "Strong Research Support" should not be cited as a current rating.
- **Specialty services:** the core model in several NHS specialist pain services (e.g., the Bath Centre for Pain Services, originally at the Royal National Hospital for Rheumatic Diseases and since 2015 part of the Royal United Hospitals Bath; INPUT at Guy's and St Thomas'; current service status not re-verified).
- **US VA:** VA trials (Wetherell 2011; Herbert 2017) and VA-developed ACT pain manuals (not re-verified).
- **Insurance:** standard psychotherapy and health-behaviour CPT codes cover ACT delivered by licensed clinicians in the US. There is no separate "ACT" code.
- **Stanza:** the first FDA De Novo-authorised prescription digital therapeutic for fibromyalgia symptoms (DEN220083, granted 9 May 2023, Class II, 21 CFR 882.5804; verified on openFDA). The authorisation came before the phase 3 PROSPER-FM results were published, so it rested mainly on the phase 2 SMART-FM data. As of Oct 2026 the Swing website says a prescription is required and that Stanza is offered through clinical partners (Swing Care, Southwest Medical in Nevada, UPMC in Pennsylvania) in 8 US states. The trial publication does not state payer coverage. CMS created digital mental health treatment (DMHT) device codes in 2025, but whether they apply to Stanza is not verified.

## 6. Evidence base

This section lists *generic ACT-for-pain* trials. Only PROSPER-FM and SMART-FM test the branded Stanza app.

| Study | Design | N | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vowles & McCracken 2008, *J Consult Clin Psychol* 76:397 | Uncontrolled effectiveness study, interdisciplinary ACT programme (Bath) | 171 | Medium or larger improvements in pain, depression, disability, medical visits; 75% reliably improved in ≥1 domain. 3-yr follow-up in Vowles et al. 2011, *BRT* 49:748. |
| Wicksell et al. 2009, *Pain* 141:248 | RCT, ~10-session exposure/acceptance-based ACT vs. multidisciplinary treatment incl. amitriptyline, paediatric longstanding pain | 32 | ACT better on functioning, pain intensity and discomfort when follow-ups were included (ITT). Comparisons were complicated by longer MDT treatment. |
| Wetherell et al. 2011, *Pain* 152:2098 | RCT, 8-session group ACT vs. CBT (VA/primary care) | 114 | Both improved; no between-group difference; higher satisfaction with ACT. |
| Wicksell et al. 2013, *Eur J Pain* 17:599 | RCT, 12-session group ACT vs. waitlist, fibromyalgia | 40 | ACT better on function, FM impact, depression and anxiety; mediated by psychological inflexibility. |
| Thorsell et al. 2011, *Clin J Pain* 27:716 | RCT, self-help ACT vs. applied relaxation (both with phone support) | 90 | ACT better on acceptance, function and pain intensity; both improved on depression and anxiety. |
| Kemani et al. 2015, *Clin J Pain* 31:1004 | RCT, 12-session group ACT vs. applied relaxation (active comparator), longstanding pain | 60 | Both improved. ACT reduced disability faster, but AR caught up by 6 months. Only ~20% showed clinically significant change. ACT was more cost-effective at post-treatment and 3 months but not at 6 months. |
| Buhrman et al. 2013, *Behav Res Ther* 51:307 | RCT, guided internet ACT vs. online forum | 76 | Improved acceptance and distress; maintained at 6 months. |
| Luciano et al. 2014, *Pain* 155:693 (EFFIGACT) | RCT, group ACT vs. pregabalin+duloxetine vs. waitlist, fibromyalgia (Spain) | 156 | ACT superior to both on FIQ; medium effects held at 6 months. Acceptance mostly did not mediate. Cost-utility favourable (Luciano 2017, *J Pain*). |
| Trompetter et al. 2015, *J Behav Med* 38:66 | RCT, internet ACT vs. expressive writing vs. waitlist | 238 | At 3-month follow-up, d=0.47 vs. expressive writing on interference, but not significant vs. waitlist (p=.11). Only 48% adhered. |
| Herbert et al. 2017, *J Pain* 18:200 | Noninferiority RCT, video-telehealth vs. in-person ACT, veterans | 128 | Telehealth noninferior on pain interference. |
| Lin et al. 2017, *Dtsch Arztebl Int* 114:681 (ACTonPain) | RCT, guided vs. unguided internet ACT vs. waitlist | 302 | Guided: d=0.58 on interference (NNT ≈3); unguided weaker. |
| Simister et al. 2018, *J Pain* 19:741 | RCT, online ACT + TAU vs. TAU, fibromyalgia | 67 | Large effect on FM impact (d 1.26 post, 1.59 at 3 months); mediated by acceptance. |
| Catella et al. 2024, *J Behav Med* 47:27 (SMART-FM, Stanza phase 2) | RCT, digital ACT vs. digital symptom tracking | 67 | PGIC improved 73% vs. 22%; FIQ-R d=0.44 (p=.07). Sponsor-run; retrospectively registered. |
| **Gendreau et al. 2024, *Lancet* 404:364 (PROSPER-FM, Stanza phase 3)** | Multicentre RCT (25 US sites), 12-week digital ACT vs. active symptom-tracking/education control | 275 (257 women) | PGIC responders 71% vs. 22% (difference 48.4%, p<.0001); no device-related adverse events. Funded by Swing Therapeutics; staff unmasked; patients told both arms might work. |

**Syntheses:**
- **Hughes et al. 2017, *Clin J Pain* 33:552** (11 RCTs; mostly vs. no treatment or TAU): medium–large effects on acceptance and flexibility; small–medium on functioning, anxiety and depression. Pain intensity and quality of life were not significant, effects were smaller at follow-up, and the authors warn that methodological limitations may have inflated effects.
- **Williams et al. 2020, Cochrane CD007407:** only 5 ACT studies (443 participants) met the inclusion criteria. There was no clear difference from active control at treatment end, and all ACT evidence was rated **very low quality**. CBT had the largest base but only very small effects versus active controls.
- **Ma, Yuen & Yang 2023, *Clin J Pain* 39:147** (21 RCTs, 1,962 participants): SMD −0.88 on pain-related functioning, −0.45 on pain intensity, −0.74 on depression.
- **Lai, …McCracken et al. 2023, *Behav Res Ther* 165:104308** (33 RCTs, 2,293 participants; trial sequential analysis): g≈0.44 for pain intensity and 0.59 for function post-treatment. Face-to-face beat internet delivery; effects were larger in headache and fibromyalgia and declined with longer follow-up.
- **Ye et al. 2024, *PLoS One*** (21 RCTs): medium effects on interference, functional impairment and depression; small on intensity.
- **Martinez-Calderon et al. 2024, *J Pain* 25:595:** an overview of 9 reviews (84 meta-analyses). It finds benefits on depression, anxiety, flexibility and functioning, but notes very high review overlap and low certainty.

**Gaps and caveats:**
- Many trials are small, compare against waitlist or TAU, and come from ACT developers' groups (an allegiance concern).
- Head-to-head trials against active treatments (CBT: Wetherell 2011; applied relaxation: Thorsell 2011, Kemani 2015) show broadly similar outcomes.
- Effects on pain intensity are modest and fade over time.
- Adverse events are poorly reported.
- Stanza trials are sponsor-funded with an unblinded, symptom-tracking control.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory

- **1999–2005:** the ACT manual appears; the first pain applications follow (Dahl, McCracken; Bath programme).
- **2005–2015:** ACBS founded (~2005, not re-verified); rapid trial growth; *Living Beyond Your Pain* (2006); pain RCTs in Sweden, the Netherlands, Spain and the US VA.
- **2015–2020:** internet ACT trials scale up; the "psychological flexibility" model becomes standard in pain psychology training. Hayes' TEDx talk on psychological flexibility (2016) and *A Liberated Mind* (2019) raise general visibility (view counts not verified).
- **2021:** NICE NG193 endorses ACT for chronic primary pain, which drove NHS service uptake and media coverage. The guideline was also controversial for de-recommending most drugs.
- **2023–2024:** Stanza's FDA De Novo (May 2023), ACR presentation (Nov 2023) and *Lancet* publication (Jul 2024).
- **2026:** ACBS counts >1,450 ACT RCTs across conditions.
- **Online/patient-community salience** is modest compared with consumer "neuroplastic" brands. On Reddit (r/ChronicPain, r/Fibromyalgia) ACT tends to be discussed as hospital/NHS psychology, sometimes resented as "learn to live with it" rather than recovery.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms

- **"Acceptance means giving up."** Patients and some clinicians see it as resignation or as implying the pain is psychological. Proponents say acceptance is behavioural willingness, not approval.
- **NICE NG193 backlash.** The Faculty of Pain Medicine and British Pain Society objected to the guideline's drug de-recommendations and its narrow evidence approach. ACT was swept into the debate about whether psychological therapy replaces medical care.
- **Evidence quality.** The Cochrane 2020 "very low quality" rating contrasts with the more positive later meta-analyses; ACBS claims are criticised for allegiance effects and waitlist-dominated designs. Independent critics have also questioned whether ACT is distinct from CBT, and whether hexaflex processes uniquely mediate change (mixed mediation results).
- **Contrast with "pain reprocessing" programmes.** PRT/EAET proponents argue ACT under-delivers because it does not target the belief that pain is dangerous or structural. They aim for pain *elimination*; ACT aims for valued living with pain.
- **Stanza.** Industry funding, an attention-matched but non-therapeutic control, unmasked staff, and PGIC as the primary outcome.

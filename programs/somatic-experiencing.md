## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)

- **Trauma lives in the nervous system, not in the event.** Symptoms arise when the body's instinctive defensive responses (orient, fight, flight, freeze/tonic immobility) are mobilised but **thwarted or left incomplete**. The survival energy or arousal mobilised for action stays "bound" in the nervous system. The key example is a freeze response that never resolves.
- **The ethological argument.** Wild prey animals are said to rarely develop lasting trauma because they spontaneously "discharge" arousal after immobility, through shaking, trembling and deep breathing. Humans override this with the neocortex, fear and shame. (Waking the Tiger's prey claims are not supported by ethological evidence; see §8.)
- **ANS dysregulation as the final common pathway.** Chronic sympathetic hyperarousal alternates with dorsal-vagal "shutdown" or collapse and dissociation. SE adopted Porges's **polyvagal theory** to describe a hierarchy of ventral-vagal safety and social engagement, sympathetic mobilisation and dorsal-vagal immobilisation. The narrowed "window of tolerance" or "resilient zone" is a related construct.
- **The formal neurophysiological model.** Payne, Levine and Crane-Godreau (2015, *Front Psychol*), written with Dartmouth's Geisel School, frame trauma and chronic stress as dysregulation of a subcortical **"core response network"** (CRN) of autonomic, limbic, motor and arousal systems. Restoring it uses interoceptive, proprioceptive and kinaesthetic attention. Payne and Crane-Godreau (2015, *Front Hum Neurosci*) add the idea of a maladaptive **"preparatory set"** of posture, tone and autonomic readiness.
- **Coupling dynamics / SIBAM.** Experience is broken into Sensation, Image, Behaviour, Affect and Meaning. Trauma is described as over-coupled elements (e.g. a sensation that automatically triggers terror) or under-coupled ones (dissociation). This resembles Pavlovian fear conditioning.
- **Bodily symptoms and chronic illness.** Persisting autonomic and defensive activation is proposed to produce somatic symptoms: chronic pain and muscle bracing, migraine, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue, GI and other "functional" complaints. Levine and Phillips (*Freedom from Pain*, 2012) present chronic pain and trauma as sharing a loop of fear, bracing and immobility. SE-lineage teachers, notably Kathy Kain and Stephen Terrell in *Nurturing Resilience* (2018), extend this to early developmental trauma as a driver of chronic illness and dysregulated physiology.

## 2. One mechanism or many?

**Essentially one transdiagnostic mechanism.** Every condition is read through incomplete defensive responses, residual autonomic activation and impaired self-regulation. SE does not offer separate mechanisms for named diseases, and SEI's own materials barely name chronic illnesses at all.
- Differentiation is by **type and timing of trauma**, not diagnosis. Shock trauma (a discrete event: accident, surgery, assault) is distinguished from developmental and attachment trauma (early and relational). Medical and surgical procedures, falls and birth are emphasised as under-recognised shock traumas, which links to illness and pain populations.
- Differentiation is also by **autonomic state**: whether the client is predominantly hyperaroused (sympathetic) or hypo-aroused, collapsed or dissociated (dorsal). Pacing and touch are adjusted to match.
- Pain gets partial differentiation in *Freedom from Pain*, which adds pain-specific exercises. The mechanism is the same.
- SE itself makes no specific claims for ME/CFS, MCS, EHS, POTS, Lyme or mould. These links come from derivative programs that adopt SE language (e.g. Primal Trust, Irene Lyon's SmartBody SmartMind, Kain's touch-skills trainings).

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies

**Clinical session (SEP-delivered):**
- **Resourcing and grounding.** The client builds internal resources (sensations of safety, strength, pleasant images or memories) and external ones (supportive people, places), and senses the support of the ground, chair and feet before any trauma work.
- **Tracking the "felt sense."** The client gives slow, moment-to-moment attention to interoceptive, proprioceptive and kinaesthetic sensation, rather than narrating the trauma story.
- **Titration.** Activating material is approached in very small doses, so the client is not overwhelmed or retraumatised. This is explicitly contrasted with flooding, though critics note its similarity to graded or interoceptive exposure.
- **Pendulation.** Attention is guided rhythmically between a region of activation or distress and a resource or region of ease, building capacity for self-regulation.
- **Completion of defensive responses.** The therapist helps thwarted actions emerge slowly: pushing away, turning or orienting, running movements in the legs, bracing release. These may be imagined, enacted in slow motion or felt as impulses.
- **Discharge.** Spontaneous trembling, shaking, heat, tears, deep spontaneous breaths, yawning and gut sounds are allowed and treated as signs of autonomic settling.
- **Orienting.** The client slowly looks around the room to re-establish present-time safety and down-regulate threat.
- **Renegotiation.** The traumatic event is revisited in a new way that ends in empowerment or completion rather than immobility (the "Nancy" model).
- **SIBAM work.** Over- or under-coupled elements are uncoupled or re-linked, e.g. separating a body sensation from a fear image.
- **Touch (optional, consensual).** Supportive touch is used, e.g. hands on the kidneys or adrenals or on joints, to provide containment and co-regulation. Kain's Somatic Resilience touch work is a derivative.

**Dose:** In trials, 6–15 weekly sessions of about 60 min: 15 sessions for PTSD (Brom 2017) and up to 12 for back pain (Andersen 2020). Clinical practice is open-ended. Single-session and "emotional first-aid" versions exist (Trauma Resiliency Model; Emotion Aid / SE-based crisis interventions). Group adaptations run 8–10 sessions.

**Self-help:** Books with guided exercises, including *Healing Trauma* (2005, 12-phase program with audio), *Freedom from Pain* (2012, with Maggie Phillips) and children's books with Maggie Kline (2006, 2008). Common self-care practices include:
- self-holding, e.g. a hand on the forehead and chest
- the "voo" sound breath
- body-boundary exercises such as tapping or showering the skin
- orienting
- grounding
- noticing pleasant sensation

**Practitioner training:** About 3 years over 8 modules (Beginning I–III, Intermediate I–III, Advanced I–II) of roughly 4–6 days each. SEP certification also requires personal sessions and case consultation.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Ethology:** animal defensive responses, tonic immobility and "discharge" in prey animals (Levine's foundational analogy). Nikolaas Tinbergen's 1973 Nobel lecture on ethology and stress is often cited in Levine's writing.
- **Reichian body psychotherapy:** the idea of energy and emotion blocked in the body and its release.
- **Somatics:** Elsa Gindler and Charlotte Selver's "Sensory Awareness" (taught to Levine at Esalen), and Ida Rolf's Structural Integration (Levine worked as a Rolfer).
- **Neurophysiology:** Ernst Gellhorn's work on autonomic "tuning" and ergotropic/trophotropic balance, and stress physiology after Selye.
- **Polyvagal theory (Porges):** adopted from the 1990s–2000s and now central to SE vocabulary.
- **Gendlin's Focusing ("felt sense")** and the human-potential / Esalen movement.
- **Behaviour therapy:** titration parallels Wolpe's systematic desensitisation, and SIBAM coupling parallels Pavlovian conditioning, though SE does not frame itself as exposure therapy.
- **Shamanic/traditional healing.** Levine has described interest in shamanic practices.

**Downstream influence:** SE is the source of the "titration / pendulation / regulate your nervous system / complete the stress cycle" vocabulary. That vocabulary appears in Primal Trust, Irene Lyon, Kathy Kain and Stephen Terrell's work, the Trauma Resiliency Model (Elaine Miller-Karas), NARM (Laurence Heller), Pat Ogden's Sensorimotor Psychotherapy (a parallel tradition) and many "somatic coaching" offerings. Bessel van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014) popularised this family of ideas and cites Levine.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Practitioner base.** SEPs are mostly psychotherapists, counsellors, social workers, physiotherapists, bodyworkers, nurses and some physicians. SE is used in private practice, trauma clinics, schools, prisons and disaster response, including Hurricane Katrina/Rita, the 2004 tsunami and Israel.
- **Reach.** Training is offered in 38 countries (SEI 2021 annual report) and in 42 languages across 54 countries (somaticexperiencing.com). SEI had 2,994 members at the end of 2021 and revenue of about $5.1–5.4M per year (2019–2021).
- **Hospital and university settings.** These are limited to research uses: the Herzog/Metiv Israel Psychotrauma Center, Danish spine centres, Mid Sweden University and Universitas Padjadjaran (Indonesia).
- **Guidelines.** SE is not listed as a recommended PTSD treatment by NICE (2018), ISTSS, VA/DoD or the APA. Those guidelines recommend trauma-focused CBT, PE, CPT and EMDR. Bisson et al. (2020) classify SE as "emerging evidence" that is premature for routine use.
- **Insurance.** No dedicated reimbursement exists. Coverage happens indirectly when a licensed clinician bills psychotherapy CPT codes. Non-licensed SEPs (e.g. bodyworkers or coaches) are self-pay. Wikipedia notes that in the US, SE touch work is not exempt from state massage practice acts.

## 6. Evidence base
**Trials of SE proper:**

| Citation | Design | N | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brom D, et al. *J Trauma Stress* 2017;30:304–312 (PMID 28585761) | RCT; 15 weekly SE sessions vs waitlist; Israel | 63 (DSM-IV PTSD) | PTSD severity d = 0.94–1.26 and depression d = 0.70–1.08 vs waitlist, maintained at 15-wk follow-up. Waitlist control only. Several authors are SE trainers or affiliated (International Trauma-Healing Institute). |
| Andersen TE, et al. *Eur J Psychotraumatol* 2017;8:1331108 (PMID 28680540; NCT02298764) | RCT; TAU (exercise) + 6–12 SE sessions vs TAU; Danish spine centre | 91 (chronic LBP + PTSD symptoms) | SE reduced PTSD symptoms (large effect) and fear of movement (moderate). **No added effect on pain, disability or catastrophising**, which improved in both arms. Authors say clinical importance "can be questioned." |
| Andersen TE, et al. *Eur J Psychotraumatol* 2020;11:1797306 (PMID 33029333; protocol PMID 30466429) | RCT; SE (≤12) + physio vs physio alone; 6- and 12-month follow-up | 114 (LBP + PTSS) | **Null.** No between-group difference on the primary outcome (Roland-Morris disability) or on any secondary outcome (PTSS, pain, catastrophising, kinesiophobia, anxiety, depression). |
| Lehmivaara J, et al. *Eur J Psychotraumatol* 2026;17:2613544 (PMID 41586542) | Two randomised experiments; single 60-min SE-derived intervention vs psychoeducation | 89 + 55 (adults with child maltreatment) | Larger increase in state psychological safety (d ≈ 0.95) and reduced disrupted body boundaries (d ≈ 1.13), with more interoceptive awareness. HR and HRV changes were not group-specific. Momentary outcomes only. |
| Harwood-Gross A, et al. *Psychol Psychother* 2025;98:175–192 (PMID 39807650) | Qualitative interviews nested in an Israeli RCT of **SE vs Prolonged Exposure** for veterans with PTSD | 30 interviewed | Describes patient experience only. The parent RCT's efficacy results were not located in PubMed as of this review. Authors disclose SE trainer and SE-dissemination (Emotion Aid) involvement. |

**Non-randomised / pilot:**
- **Leitch et al. 2009** (*Soc Work*, PMID 19205253): 1–2 sessions of SE/Trauma Resiliency Model for Hurricane Katrina/Rita social-service workers, with propensity-matched controls (n=91 vs 51). Treated workers had fewer PTSD symptoms and more resilience. The authors are affiliated with the Foundation for Human Enrichment.
- **Winblad et al. 2018** (*Front Neurosci*): a within-group study of SE trainees (n=18) over the 3-year training. Anxiety and somatisation fell.
- **Vagnini et al. 2023** (*IJERPH*): 8-week online group SE for breast-cancer survivors, non-randomised (21 vs 14). Anxiety, depression and body image improved.
- **Briggs et al. 2018** (*Front Psychiatry*): an SE-informed therapeutic group addressing biopsychosocial effects of a gender-diverse identity (descriptive).

**Reviews:**
- **Kuhfuß et al. 2021** (*Eur J Psychotraumatol*, scoping review, 16 studies): preliminary positive evidence for PTSD symptoms and some affective and somatic symptoms. Study quality and risk of bias are mixed, and unbiased RCTs are needed.
- **Bisson et al. 2020** (systematic review / meta-analysis of non-pharmacological, non-psychological PTSD treatments): SE has "emerging evidence" only, based on a single waitlist-controlled trial (Brom 2017; k=1, N=60, SMD −0.75, GRADE "very uncertain").
- **Rubinstein et al. 2026** (*J Anxiety Disord*): places SE on an "exposure continuum" with other trauma therapies.

**Registered / ongoing:**
- ISRCTN58257113: group SE for Indonesian sexual-assault survivors, n=207 planned (protocol in PLoS One 2025).
- NCT06549777: body-oriented therapy for child maltreatment (Sweden).

**Gaps:**
- No RCTs in ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, IBS, long COVID, FND, migraine or CRPS.
- The only chronic-pain RCTs (Andersen 2017, 2020) show **no benefit on pain or disability** beyond physiotherapy or exercise.
- No active-comparator efficacy publication against an established PTSD treatment.
- The mechanistic claims (discharge of "energy," completion of defensive responses, CRN) are untested. Autonomic outcomes, where measured, are not specific to SE.
- Many studies involve SE-trained or SE-affiliated investigators. Andersen's Danish trials are the most independent, and they are the least favourable.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1969–1980s:** origin case (1969); Levine develops the method through workshops, Esalen and bodywork circles. The trademark is registered in 1989. Activity is small and niche.
- **1990s:** the Foundation for Human Enrichment is formed (about 1994). *Waking the Tiger* (1997) is the breakthrough, eventually selling over 250k copies in 24 languages.
- **2000s:** international training spreads to Europe, Israel and Brazil. *Healing Trauma* (2005, Sounds True). Disaster deployments (2004 tsunami, 2005 Katrina). Polyvagal integration begins.
- **2010s:** *In an Unspoken Voice* (2010), *Freedom from Pain* (2012) and *Trauma and Memory* (2015). Van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014; a mass bestseller by the late 2010s) drives interest in body-based trauma therapy. The first RCTs appear (2017). SEI trained 6,314 students in 2019.
- **2020–2022:** the COVID shift to online training brings about 7,000 students in 2020. Membership reaches 2,994 in 2021. Training is offered in 38 countries. Revenue is about $5M per year, with a dip from lost in-person tuition.
- **2022–2025:** a TikTok/Instagram "somatic healing," "somatic exercises" and "nervous-system regulation" boom. SE terms (titration, pendulation, discharge, freeze) are widely used by coaches and apps, often without SE credentials. Google search interest for "somatic" terms reportedly rose sharply over this period (not directly verified in this compilation). *An Autobiography of Trauma* (2024) and Levine podcast appearances follow.
- **2026:** NBCC continuing-education provider approval regained (May 2026, select modules). Training is offered in 54 countries and 42 languages per Levine's site. Peer-reviewed output is rising: PubMed lists about 26 items for "somatic experiencing" (Oct 2026): 1 in 2009, 1 in 2012, 3 in 2015, 3 in 2017, 4 in 2018, 1–2 per year 2020–2023, then 4 in 2025 and 5 in 2026.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Thin and mixed evidence relative to its reach.** The larger, more independent pain trial was null. Waitlist-controlled positives dominate the evidence.
- **Ethological and "energy" claims.** Wikipedia and reviewers (e.g. Marzillier) note that the "discharge of survival energy" concept is underdeveloped. The claim that animals in the wild do not become traumatised lacks evidence.
- **Polyvagal theory dependence.** The "dorsal vagal shutdown" account of freeze conflicts with evidence on vagal physiology, and polyvagal theory itself is contested (e.g. Grossman's critiques of the respiratory sinus arrhythmia and phylogeny claims).
- **"Not exposure" vs exposure.** Critics argue that titration and pendulation are forms of graded interoceptive exposure, so SE's claimed novelty is overstated.
- **Esoteric content.** Wikipedia cites Levine's interest in shamanism and his account of a year-long "Socratic dialogue" with an apparition of Einstein while developing his dissertation, recounted in his 2024 memoir.
- **Scope creep in derivatives.** SE vocabulary underpins many unregulated coaching programs that make broad chronic-illness claims (ME/CFS, MCS, long COVID) that SE itself has not tested.
- **Touch and regulation.** Touch-based practice by non-licensed practitioners raises scope-of-practice and massage-licensure issues.

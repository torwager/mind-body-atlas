## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Biopsychosocial / CBT model.** Chronic pain is described as different from acute pain. It is no longer a reliable signal of tissue damage and is kept going by interacting physical, cognitive, emotional and behavioural factors.
- **The "pain cycle."** The site's central framing is a self-reinforcing loop. Pain leads to unhelpful thoughts (catastrophizing, worry), then to anxiety and low mood, then to avoidance or over-activity and physical deconditioning, which in turn bring more pain and disability. Breaking the loop at any point reduces disability and distress.
- **Fear-avoidance and cognitive appraisal** are the main psychological targets. Physiological arousal (stress, muscle tension) is treated as a modifiable contributor.
- It does **not** make strong claims about a brain-generated or "neuroplastic" origin of pain. Nor does it claim pain can be eliminated: the goal is management and better functioning and wellbeing, not cure. In this respect it differs sharply from PRT, Curable or the Sarno-derived programs.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
Transdiagnostic in design. A single CBT skill set is applied to chronic pain whatever its cause (back pain, arthritis, fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain, headache and so on). Pain types are not differentiated by mechanism, and the course makes no claim about the cause of the underlying disease. The same group built a sibling course with the same structure, the **Chronic Conditions Course**, which covers many health conditions including functional GI disorders, epilepsy, spinal cord injury and pain. This shows the developers see the shared mechanism as psychological adjustment and distress rather than a shared disease process.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
**Structure.** 5 core lessons released on a timetable over 8 weeks. Participants should allow at least 4 hours a week. Each lesson comes with a case story, homework, and DIY skill guides. Automated emails send reminders. Clinician contact is brief: in the trials, about 75–80 minutes in total per participant by phone or email, and the 2015 RCT found outcomes were similar even with optional or no scheduled contact.

**Lesson content** (as described in the trial reports):
1. Education on the CBT model of chronic pain, symptoms, and the pain–anxiety–depression cycle.
2. Thought monitoring and cognitive restructuring (challenging catastrophic or unhelpful thoughts).
3. De-arousal / physical symptom management (controlled breathing, relaxation) and pleasant-activity scheduling.
4. Activity pacing and graded, safe return to physical activity, as an alternative to avoidance or boom-bust.
5. Relapse prevention and maintaining gains.

**Additional resources** cover sleep, communication and assertiveness, attention, problem solving, managing medications, and working with health professionals. Measures are taken before, during and after the course: PDI or RMDQ for disability, PHQ-9 for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety, and a pain rating.

**Eligibility.** Australian residents aged 18+ with pain lasting more than 6 months. People who are acutely suicidal are redirected to urgent care.

## 4. Roots and influences
- Beck-style **CBT** applied to pain management. This is the multidisciplinary pain-management tradition of Turk, Fordyce's operant model, and Michael Nicholas's ADAPT program at the Royal North Shore Hospital pain centre in Sydney. Nicholas is a co-author.
- **Fear-avoidance** model (Vlaeyen) and graded activity.
- **Relaxation and arousal reduction** (breathing, Relaxation Response).
- The eCentreClinic's **transdiagnostic iCBT** program, which grew from Titov's internet treatment work for anxiety and depression in the late 2000s (the "Wellbeing Course").

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Australia.** Delivered through MindSpot, a national digital mental-health service funded by the Commonwealth Department of Health and Aged Care. It is free and needs no referral, though GP involvement is encouraged. MindSpot belongs to Australia's public stepped-care system.
- **Canada.** The University of Regina's Online Therapy Unit (Heather Hadjistavropoulos) licensed and adapted the course and offers it free, with coaching, in Saskatchewan's routine online-therapy service.
- **Insurance.** Not relevant, because there is no fee. In general, guidelines such as NICE NG193 (2021) for chronic primary pain recommend psychological therapy (CBT/ACT) and exercise; they do not endorse branded digital products.

**Routine-care and secondary studies (fact-check additions)**
- **Dear BF, Staples L, Nielssen O, Titov N.** *Aust J Rural Health* 2025;33(1):e70008. doi:10.1111/ajr.70008. A routine-care cohort of the Pain Course at MindSpot, N = 1,567. From pre- to post-treatment, disability fell 32%, depression 44%, anxiety 43% and average pain 23%, and the gains held at 3 months. Outcomes were similar for rural and urban patients. The unverified "N ≈ 1,367" figure that appeared here earlier has been replaced by this citation.
- **Hadjistavropoulos HD, Peynenburg V, Thiessen D, Schneider LH.** *Eur J Pain* 2022;26(2):390–404. doi:10.1002/ejp.1866. A five-year observational study of routine care in Saskatchewan, N = 293. Disability improved about 19–20%, depression 35–41% and anxiety 37–41%.
- **Nielssen O, et al.** *Rehabil Psychol* 2019;64(3):263–268. A secondary analysis of the 2015 RCT: people with ongoing compensation litigation had smaller gains.

**Gaps and critiques**
- Nearly all RCTs were run by the developers.
- Controls are waitlist or treatment-as-usual, never an attention or active-psychological comparator, so nonspecific effects cannot be ruled out.
- Pain-intensity effects are moderate and smaller than effects on distress.
- Participants were self-selected, mostly women, and comfortable with the internet.
- The wider literature finds smaller effects. The Cochrane review by Rosser et al. 2023 (CD013863; 32 RCTs, N = 4,924) found that remotely delivered CBT has small post-treatment benefits on pain intensity (SMD −0.28, moderate certainty) and disability (SMD −0.38, low certainty), which were not maintained at follow-up. The Pain Course trials sit at the more favourable end, which may reflect the strong mood components and the waitlist controls.
- Routine-care cohorts have no control group, so regression to the mean and natural fluctuation cannot be ruled out.

**Comparator noted (different product, not fact-checked in depth): RelieVRx.** RelieVRx (formerly EaseVRx, made by AppliedVR) is a self-administered, 56-session VR program that combines CBT, mindfulness and relaxation for chronic low back pain. It received FDA De Novo authorisation in November 2021. Its pivotal trial was Garcia LM et al., *J Med Internet Res* 2021;23(2):e26292: an RCT of VR CBT vs sham 2D nature-video VR, N = 179, with greater pain reduction in the active arm. CMS later created a DME billing code for it. It is a commercial, prescription device, in contrast to the free, public Pain Course.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **2011–2013:** Developed at the eCentreClinic; first RCT published 2013. MindSpot launched nationally in 2012–13.
- **2014–2018:** Large RCTs in 2015 and 2017, 24-month follow-up in 2018, and the Canadian adoption (University of Regina Online Therapy Unit). The course became part of MindSpot's routine treatment menu.
- **2019–2022:** Canadian award (2019) and the economic-evaluation RCT (2022). Demand for MindSpot rose during the COVID period.
- **2023–2026:** Still offered at MindSpot. The eCentreClinic now concentrates on the Chronic Conditions Course; its Pain Course page says no Pain Course trials are currently running.
- **Indicators.** MindSpot reports serving more than 250,000 Australians since 2012, across anxiety, depression and pain courses. Social-media presence is minimal: it does not appear in the r/ChronicPain or r/TMSHealing discourse about "mind-body" programs. Its visibility is academic and clinical rather than consumer-facing.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
There are no notable public controversies. The academic criticisms are the usual ones for iCBT:
- the trials are developer-led
- waitlist controls inflate effect sizes
- the benefit for pain intensity is modest
- the course is not available outside Australia, apart from the Saskatchewan adaptation
- it is framed as pain *management*, which some patients see as accepting pain

The framing is deliberately conservative. Unlike the neuroplastic-pain programs, it makes no promise of cure, which also limits its appeal in recovery-focused online communities.

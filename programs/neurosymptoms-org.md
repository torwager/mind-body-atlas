## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **"Software, not hardware."** FND is described as a problem with how the nervous system *functions* rather than with its structure. The site compares it to a computer software bug that needs "reprogramming." Symptoms are real, not imagined and not under voluntary control. The site stresses that FND is not "made up."
- **Predictive processing / expectation.** For weakness and sensory loss, the brain's prediction (e.g., that a limb is not there or cannot move) is "too strong." The person experiences the prediction instead of the actual sensory input. Phantom limb is used as an analogy in reverse.
- **Self-agency network.** The site draws on functional imaging (fMRI, SPECT) from research studies: an underactive right temporoparietal junction in functional tremor, which is linked to the feeling that movements are not self-generated, and thalamic changes in hemisensory symptoms that normalise as symptoms resolve. It cautions that imaging is a research tool, not a diagnostic one.
- **Threat / protective shutdown and dissociation.** The brain may "switch off" a body part after pain, injury, migraine or panic, as a fight-or-flight-type protective response. Functional seizures are described as a trance-like, hypnosis-like dissociative state. Some descriptions invoke a sensitised fight-or-flight system after adversity.
- **Attention and automaticity** (on the treatment pages). Movement is better when attention is directed away from the symptom. This is the basis for the physiotherapy approach (see §3).
- **Biopsychosocial "3P" framing** (predisposing, precipitating and perpetuating factors, though not labelled with that term):
  - *Vulnerability:* adverse experiences (2–4× more commonly reported, but explicitly not required); a learned tendency to dissociate; other illness such as migraine or hypermobility; and an obsessional or "always on the go" style.
  - *Triggers:* limb injury, faints or panic attacks, drug side-effects, infection, prolonged fatigue, migraine, extreme stress.
  - *Perpetuating factors:* delayed diagnosis, not being believed, opioids, worry and low mood, and symptom-focused worry.
- **Chronic pain** is linked by analogy to the same software-level change: the nervous system's "volume knobs" are turned up, a central sensitisation framing.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Mostly one framework, with symptom-specific emphasis.**
- Many people's functional problems are presented as possibly "ONE condition with different aspects," with FND as one part. Fibromyalgia, IBS, CFS, chronic pain, PPPD and (controversially) migraine are all "disorders of nervous system functioning." The site notes overlapping brain changes and overlapping treatments.
- The site does separate **core FND symptoms** (weakness, seizures, movement, sensory) from associated symptoms such as pain, fatigue and bowel problems. These associated symptoms are common in FND but "not strictly FND."
- It recommends specific mechanisms and treatments by symptom type:
  - *Seizures:* dissociation/arousal, with grounding and distraction.
  - *Weakness/movement:* attention and agency, with physiotherapy built on automatic movement.
  - *Dizziness:* PPPD, with vestibular rehabilitation.
- Unlike many commercial programs, it states that comorbid disease (MS, arthritis, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome) can coexist and must be considered separately. It lists "M.E/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome" among the overlapping functional disorders and covers fatigue as an associated symptom. It does not offer ME/CFS-specific treatment, and does not address MCS, EHS, Lyme or mould illness.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
The site is a stepped-care information resource. It does not run a structured program with a set dose or duration; Stone states that the site is "not a treatment for FND in itself."
- **Understanding the diagnosis**, presented as the first step of treatment. It explains positive clinical signs (e.g., Hoover's sign), the reasons for investigations, ten "myths of FND," and an online formulation tool for mapping one's own vulnerabilities, triggers and maintaining factors.
- **Physiotherapy / activity:**
  - Use attention redirection: gaze at a fixed target, kick a ball, or try altered gait patterns such as "ice-skating" or jogging.
  - Make use of automatic movement, and draw on the person's own movement strengths.
  - Grade activity: on good days do something modest, below your best; on bad days do the minimum. Stabilise with pacing before increasing.
  - Graded exercise is "not for everyone."
  - Links to the free Physio4FMD patient workbook.
- **Functional/dissociative seizures:**
  - Recognise warning signs such as palpitations, dizziness, feeling spaced out or tingling.
  - Challenge catastrophic thoughts.
  - Use demanding distraction as soon as a warning appears, e.g., serial-7 counting, conversation, singing or a phone game.
  - Use Sheffield-developed sensory grounding (with links to resources by Raspin and Reuber).
  - Give bystanders guidance on how to respond.
  - Track progress: more warnings, and more attacks averted.
- **Psychological therapy** (not "classic psychoanalysis"):
  - Treat comorbid anxiety, depression, panic, PTSD and OCD.
  - Run behavioural experiments to test avoidance.
  - Address anticipatory worry.
  - Do practical problem-solving.
  - Offer trauma-focused therapy where relevant.
  - The site points to CBT trial evidence (CODES).
- **Other:**
  - Occupational therapy, speech and language therapy, medication (mainly for pain, sleep, mood, and migraine overlap; it warns against opioids), family and work advice, relapse management, and an "I've tried everything" page.
  - Separate guidance for FND with intellectual disability.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Neurology:** classical neurology of "hysteria" and conversion disorder (Charcot, Janet's dissociation, Freud's conversion), recast in a non-psychogenic, mechanism-agnostic way that follows DSM-5 in dropping the requirement for a psychological stressor.
- **Contemporary neuroscience:** Bayesian predictive-processing models of FND (Edwards et al., 2012), agency/TPJ imaging, and attention models.
- **CBT:** Sharpe and Carson's CBT for functional symptoms, and Chalder/Goldstein CBT for dissociative seizures.
- **Physiotherapy:** specialist FND physiotherapy (Nielsen et al., 2015 consensus; Physio4FMD), plus graded activity and pacing.
- **Hypnosis/dissociation:** used as an explanatory analogy, not as a treatment.
- **Pain science:** central sensitisation and "volume knob" pain education.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- The site is part of mainstream academic neurology rather than an alternative to it. Its authors lead the main UK FND trials, and the content is aligned with FND Society teaching.
- NHS neurology services routinely recommend it to patients at diagnosis (e.g., the Gloucestershire Hospitals patient leaflet).
- The Scottish Government funded the redesign through its Neurological Framework. Edinburgh Innovations reports that it was adopted into NHS inform and the NHS pathway.
- Australian FND treatment recommendations (Lehn et al., BMJ Neurol Open 2025) list it among patient resources.
- Insurance is not relevant because the site is free. Specialist physiotherapy and CBT are funded within the NHS and are often reimbursed elsewhere.

## 6. Evidence base
**Trial of the website itself:**
- **Gelauff JM, ... Stone J, Tijssen MAJ. Internet-based self-help randomized trial for motor functional neurologic disorder (SHIFT). *Neurology* 2020;95(13):e1883–e1896.** PMID 32690783; doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000010381; NCT02589886.
  - Design: non-blinded RCT in the Netherlands (UMC Groningen). An unguided education and self-help website plus usual care, versus usual care alone. N=186.
  - The intervention was a newly built, password-protected Dutch website. It combined elements of neurosymptoms.org with the Sharpe/Williams CBT self-help workbook and physiotherapy exercises adapted from Nielsen et al. It is therefore a derivative of the site, not the English site itself.
  - Primary outcome: self-rated health (CGI) improved in 44% vs 40% at 3 months and 42% vs 43% at 6 months, which is not significant. No secondary outcomes differed.
  - 86% of users would recommend the site.
  - The authors conclude it is a useful adjunct within stepped care but not an effective treatment in its own right. Stone is a co-author and declared that he runs neurosymptoms.org, so this is not an independent evaluation; it is notable as a candid null result from the developers.

**Trials of the therapies the site recommends (related techniques, not the branded site; same investigator group):**
- **Sharpe M, Walker J, Williams C, Stone J, et al. Guided self-help for functional (psychogenic) symptoms. *Neurology* 2011.** PMID 21795652.
  - RCT, N=127. CBT-based self-help manual plus 4 half-hour guidance sessions, versus usual care.
  - Better CGI at 3 months (common OR 2.36, 95% CI 1.17–4.74; NNT 8). Not significant at 6 months.
- **Goldstein LH, ... Stone J, Carson A, et al. CODES. *Lancet Psychiatry* 2020;7:491–505.** PMID 32445688.
  - RCT, N=368 with dissociative seizures. CBT plus standardised medical care versus standardised medical care alone.
  - Primary outcome: no significant reduction in seizure frequency at 12 months (IRR 0.78, p=0.14).
  - Several secondary outcomes were better with CBT: bothersomeness, longest seizure-free period, quality of life, psychosocial functioning, distress and somatic symptoms.
- **Nielsen G, Stone J, et al. Physio4FMD. *Lancet Neurol* 2024;23:675–686.** PMID 38768621.
  - Pragmatic RCT, N=355 randomised (179 specialist vs 176 usual care). 89 were excluded because COVID-19 interrupted treatment, leaving 241 in the primary analysis. Specialist physiotherapy (9 sessions) versus community neurophysiotherapy.
  - Primary outcome: no significant difference in SF-36 physical function at 12 months (adjusted mean difference 3.5, p=0.23).
  - Patients in the specialist arm more often rated their motor symptoms as improved, and they had better mental-health scores.

**Gaps:**
- No trial of the redesigned (2021+) English site or of the app.
- The only direct evaluation (SHIFT) was null.
- Usage and outcome data come from user surveys (e.g., n=434 in 2020) rather than controlled studies.
- Evidence for the recommended treatments is largely from one closely linked UK research network.
- Large pragmatic trials have mostly shown benefits on secondary outcomes rather than primary ones.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **2009:** launched by Stone, at a time when there was "zero information for FND patients online." The earliest Wayback capture is April 2009.
- **Undated (University of Edinburgh staff profile, likely around 2017–19):** about 60,000 hits a month; 12 translations.
- **2019:** the FND Society was founded, with Stone as Founding Secretary, and FND gained wider profile as a clinical field.
- **2020:** SHIFT trial published; user survey (n=434) informed the redesign.
- **June 2021:** redesigned site and app launched (iOS release 30 Jun 2021, last updated Oct 2023, v1.1.6); about 50,000 users a month.
- **2022–23:** Wellcome Trust iTPA Impact Fund (£50k) to develop the site into a research and treatment platform with a patient data registry. BMJ feature profiling Stone, "Fighting the FND stigma: the consultant neurologist" (Oxtoby, 2 May 2023; PMID 37130594).
- **2023–26:** Edinburgh reports about 60,000 visits a month. FND's public profile has grown through TikTok and social media. The r/FND subreddit (a general FND forum, not specific to the site) grew from 743 members (Mar 2022) to 8,343 (Aug 2025), per Wayback captures; the live count was blocked (HTTP 403). How often the site is shared there was not quantified. The Android app has 10K+ downloads. Google Trends data are not available without the interactive tool and were not checked.
- **Rough cumulative reach:** about 50–60k visits a month for roughly 15 years is on the order of 10 million visits. That suggests perhaps 1–3 million unique people, allowing for repeat visits.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- The site sits within the mainstream FND model. Some patients and advocacy voices object to its links with the Sharpe/Chalder CBT and graded-activity tradition, which is controversial because of PACE in ME/CFS. The site itself says graded exercise is "not for everyone."
- It includes migraine and fibromyalgia under "functional disorders" while acknowledging that this is controversial.
- Some psychiatrists argue that the mainstream "agnostic" causal position underplays psychological causation (Scamvougeras & Castle, Can J Psychiatry 2024). From the other side, some patients resist any psychological framing.
- Its own trial showed that information alone does not improve outcomes, which tempers the view that "explaining the diagnosis is treatment."

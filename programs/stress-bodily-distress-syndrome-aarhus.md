# STreSS (Specialised Treatment for Severe Bodily Distress Syndromes) and the Aarhus "bodily distress / functional disorders" model

*Compiled 2026-10-12 from the STreSS treatment manual (Rehfeld, Schröder & Fink, Aarhus University Hospital, 2009), the published trials (Schröder et al. 2012; Agger et al. 2017; Pedersen et al. 2019; Kallesøe et al. 2021; Fjorback et al. 2013), ClinicalTrials.gov records, Danish Health Authority (Sundhedsstyrelsen) specialist-planning documents, and patient-advocacy and critical sources (s4me, dxrevisionwatch). Content paraphrased.*

## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)

- **One syndrome, many labels.** Fink and colleagues argue that the "functional somatic syndromes" defined by each specialty are largely one underlying disorder, which they call **bodily distress syndrome (BDS)**. Fibromyalgia (rheumatology), CFS (infectious disease/neurology), IBS (gastroenterology), non-cardiac chest pain (cardiology), whiplash, hyperventilation and others are treated as artefacts of specialty-based classification.
  - In a stratified sample of 978 patients (Fink et al. 2007; Fink & Schröder 2010, *J Psychosom Res* 68:415–26), BDS criteria captured all fibromyalgia, CFS and hyperventilation cases and 98% of IBS cases, with overall agreement of 95% (κ = 0.86).
  - A DanFunD population study (Petersen MW et al., *J Psychosom Res* 2020;128:109868; n = 1,590) reports that the construct holds in the general population.
- **Aetiology.** Described as multifactorial and "aetiology-neutral." The manual's "What do we know about chronic bodily distress?" slide sets biological factors against social/psychological ones. Biological factors: altered brain findings on PET, and signals from the body to the brain that are "not filtered out as well as in healthy" people, i.e. impaired central filtering or **central sensitisation / amplification**. Social/psychological factors: worry, and an iatrogenic healthcare loop of many unnecessary examinations and treatments.
- **"Functional"** is defined as a potentially reversible disturbance of organ function and/or of the brain systems underlying symptom experience. The authors explicitly reject "all in the mind" and say BDS is not a mental disorder in that sense.
- **Stress response.** Module 3 teaches the bodily, cognitive and emotional responses to stress and strain (autonomic arousal, muscle tension, fatigue, noise sensitivity) as a pathway that generates and maintains symptoms.
- **Maintaining vicious circles (the CBT model).**
  - Illness perceptions, catastrophic symptom interpretation, symptom focus/worry and avoidance or boom–bust activity cycles keep symptoms and disability going.
  - The manual reproduces Wessely's (1995) fatigue vicious circle and the Fink/Toft/Rosendal (2002) perception-of-symptoms model.
  - Illness beliefs are presented as strongly predictive of outcome.
- **Later framing.** Aarhus authors co-wrote the 2020 EURONET-SOMA proposal of a unified "functional somatic disorder" (Burton, Fink, Henningsen, Löwe, Rief, *BMC Medicine* 2020). Collaborators have also adopted **predictive-processing** accounts of bodily distress (e.g., Van den Bergh, Witthöft, Petersen & Brown 2017), which treat symptoms as brain-generated perceptual inferences weighted by prior expectations. STreSS itself predates this framing.

## 2. One mechanism or many?

**Mostly unified, with condition-specific emphasis.**

- **Shared core.** BDS is defined as a single disorder. Pain, fatigue and gastrointestinal complaints are framed as variants of the same central process.
- **Organ subtypes.** The construct recognises four symptom clusters: cardiopulmonary/autonomic arousal, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, and general symptoms such as fatigue and concentration problems.
- **Severity tiers.** There is a "single-organ" form (moderate) and a "multi-organ" form (severe). STreSS and its successors target the **multi-organ** type, defined as symptoms from at least three of the four clusters.
- **Treatment is the same for all.** The group is deliberately mixed (fibromyalgia, CFS, IBS and whiplash patients together). The manual uses one generic CBT model and does not differentiate mechanisms by named illness. Comorbid depression and anxiety are treated separately before or alongside the program.
- **Exclusions.** Patients are first assessed to rule out organic disease. Severe comorbid psychiatric disorder is treated first.
- **Not covered.** POTS, EHS, "adrenal fatigue," mould illness and "chronic Lyme" are not specifically addressed. MCS appears among the syndromes BDS is said to encompass.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies

**STreSS group CBT (manual 2009)**

*Structure and session format*
- 9 modules in groups of about 9 patients, each session 3.5 hours with coffee breaks, over about 16 weeks.
- Spacing widens as the course goes on: weeks 1–3 are weekly, then fortnightly, and the last interval is 4 weeks.
- Two therapists lead each group (psychiatrists in the trial).
- Every session begins and ends with "box-scale" self-ratings (0–10) of: most bothersome symptom, pain, other symptoms, sadness, nervousness, and others.
- Homework every session: a weekly 24-hour registration of activity and symptoms (filled in 2–3 times a day) and testing the next "step" toward goals.
- Built-in short physical exercise breaks ("you have now been listening for several minutes…").
- Motif: the *Hare and the Tortoise* ("slow but steady wins the race"), i.e. graded, paced activity rather than boom–bust.
- Individual consultations are available for patients who worsen or are at risk of dropping out.

*Module content*

| Module | Content | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What CBT is; group rules; treatment expectations; glossary | 1 week |
| 2 | Body-mapping of symptoms ("draw your body"); ranking symptom strength; eliciting the patient's own symptom explanations; medication registration | 1 week |
| 3 | Psychoeducation: classification of BDS, the stress response, the vicious circle and illness perceptions; setting goals for physical activity, work, social life and leisure; a "staircase of goals" (graded partial objectives) | 1 week |
| 4 | The CBT "basic model" (symptoms–thoughts–emotions–actions); negative automatic thoughts and dysfunctional behaviours | 2 weeks |
| 5 | Cognitive distortions (all-or-nothing thinking, catastrophising, etc.) and alternative thoughts; emotional awareness through "four fundamental emotions" (fear, anger, sadness, desire) | 2 weeks |
| 6 | Illness behaviour → health behaviour I: pleasant-activity scheduling and life chart | 2 weeks |
| 7 | Health behaviour II: sleep (stimulus-control sleep hygiene: fixed rise time, get up after 15 min awake, no daytime naps, daily exercise, avoid long-term hypnotics); diet; exercise; relationships | 2 weeks |
| 8 | "Becoming your own therapist": relapse prevention and planning for after therapy | 4 weeks |
| 9 | Maintaining skills and coping strategies (copy sheets for continued self-monitoring) | follow-up |

**Successor and companion interventions from the same unit**
- **Group ACT (STreSS-4):** nine 3-h sessions, or a brief version (1-day workshop + one consultation). Covers acceptance, defusion and values-based action in place of symptom control.
- **AHEAD:** group ACT for adolescents aged 15–19. Nine modules (27 h) plus a follow-up.
- **Mindfulness therapy:** MBSR adapted with CBT elements for BDS (Fjorback).
- **Pharmacotherapy:** low-dose imipramine, 25–75 mg/day for 10 weeks (STreSS-3). An ongoing trial (NCT06232473, started 2024, target n = 424) tests patient education (3 individual sessions + one 3-h group session giving a "bio-psycho-social" explanation) and/or duloxetine 60 mg.
- **Internet-delivered CBT:** "One step at a time" is 11 modules over 14 weeks with therapist messaging, compared against the self-help "Get started" (NCT05525598, n = 166, recruiting from 2022).
- **GP training:** the TERM model (The Extended Reattribution and Management model) teaches primary-care physicians to manage milder cases.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **CBT (Beck/Ellis)**, specifically the cognitive-behavioural models of CFS and somatisation developed by Wessely, Sharpe, Chalder and Deary in the UK in the 1990s. Michael Sharpe (Oxford) is a co-author of the STreSS RCT.
- Danish/European **consultation-liaison psychiatry** and the somatisation literature (Kroenke, Rief, Henningsen). The reattribution model for GPs (Goldberg).
- **Third-wave CBT:** ACT (Hayes) and MBSR/mindfulness (Kabat-Zinn) in the later trials.
- **Selye stress theory** and stress physiology (Module 3).
- **Central sensitisation / gate-control-era pain science**, and later predictive processing.
- Behavioural activation, graded activity/pacing and sleep-restriction/stimulus-control (behaviourism).

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance

- **Denmark.**
  - The Aarhus clinic was founded in spring 1999 on the initiative of Aarhus County and Aarhus University Hospital and has received patients for assessment and treatment since 2005 (funktionellelidelser.dk/om-afdelingen), with Per Fink as head physician (Lex.dk). Fink was appointed clinical professor in 2009. By 2021 it was a full specialist department, described as "from pioneer to established specialist department." Staff numbers are not published: the department's "Om afdelingen" page lists only its management team, so the earlier figure of about 45 staff has been dropped.
  - The Danish Health Authority's specialist plan created **regional specialised functions for functional disorders** from 1 November 2021. They are multidisciplinary (internal medicine + psychiatry, nurses, psychologists, physiotherapists, social workers) and anchored in a somatic setting. Aarhus is one provider. Their creation closed the older psychiatric regional function for somatoform disorders.
  - Treatment there is free at point of use (tax-funded public health system).
  - Danish guidance for milder cases relies on GP-level management (TERM-type).
- **International.**
  - The BDS construct informed debates on ICD-11's **bodily distress disorder (6C20)** and DSM-5 somatic symptom disorder, and the EURONET-SOMA "functional somatic disorder" proposal (2020).
  - The STreSS manual is in English and free for non-commercial use. Adaptations are reported in Scandinavian and other European settings, but we found no evidence of routine adoption in the UK/US healthcare systems.
  - NICE has no STreSS-specific guidance. NICE's 2021 ME/CFS guideline moved away from GET and CBT-as-cure, which runs counter to the Danish approach for ME/CFS.
- **Insurance.** Not applicable outside public hospital care. In the US, analogous group CBT would be billed as ordinary psychotherapy.

## 6. Evidence base

**Trials of the branded/defined Aarhus programs**

| Trial | Design / N | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| **STreSS (CBT).** Schröder A, Rehfeld E, Ørnbøl E, Sharpe M, Licht RW, Fink P. *Br J Psychiatry* 2012;200:499–507. doi:10.1192/bjp.bp.111.098681; NCT00132197 | Single-centre RCT; 120 adults (20–50 y) with severe multi-organ BDS (fibromyalgia, CFS, IBS, whiplash, etc.). STreSS (n = 54) vs enhanced usual care (n = 66) | At 16 months, greater improvement on the primary outcome, an aggregate of SF-36 physical functioning, bodily pain and vitality (adjusted mean difference 4.0, 95% CI 1.4–6.6, p = 0.002). Most secondary outcomes also favoured STreSS. The effect is modest in absolute size. |
| **STreSS-3 (imipramine).** Agger JL, Schröder A, Gormsen LK, Jensen JS, Jensen TS, Fink P. *Lancet Psychiatry* 2017;4:378–388. NCT01518634 | Double-blind placebo-controlled RCT; 138 adults with multi-organ BDS; imipramine 25–75 mg/d for 10 weeks | More patients on imipramine rated themselves "much better/better" (about 53% vs 25% on placebo). Of 120 patients with outcome data: 33 (53%) vs 14 (25%); OR 3.3 (95% CI 1.6–6.8); NNT 3.6. Moderate adverse events were more common (49% vs 17%). Dropout due to adverse events was 6% vs 5%. Both arms had regular clinician contacts. This is a **drug** trial in the same population, not a test of STreSS CBT. Published commentaries (Lancet Psychiatry 2017) questioned clinical meaningfulness. |
| **STreSS-4 (ACT).** Pedersen HF, Agger JL, Frostholm L, Jensen JS, Ørnbøl E, Fink P, Schröder A. *Psychol Med* 2019;49:1005–1014. NCT01518647 | Three-arm RCT; 180 adults (20–50 y) with multiple FSS: enhanced care (60) vs brief ACT (61) vs extended group ACT (59) | Extended ACT beat enhanced care on patient-rated global improvement at 14 months (OR 2.9, 95% CI 1.4–6.2). Brief ACT did not differ from enhanced care. Of 18 secondary outcomes, only SF-36 physical functioning favoured extended ACT, and the authors acknowledge that the primary and secondary outcomes are discrepant. |
| **AHEAD (adolescent ACT).** Kallesøe KH, Schröder A, Jensen JS, Wicksell RK, Rask CU. *JCPP Advances* 2021 (PMC10242822); NCT02346071 | RCT; 91 adolescents aged 15–19 with multiple FSS; group ACT vs enhanced usual care | **Null** on the primary outcome, SF-36 physical health at 12 months (adjusted difference 1.2, 95% CI −1.6 to 4.0). Improvement was faster in the AHEAD arm (group × time interaction). The control arm received more outside psychological treatment. |
| **Mindfulness therapy for BDS.** Fjorback LO, Arendt M, Ørnbøl E et al. *J Psychosom Res* 2013 | Fjorback LO, Arendt M, Ørnbøl E, Walach H, Rehfeld E, Schröder A, Fink P. *J Psychosom Res* 2013;74:31–40. RCT; 119 patients with BDS/somatisation; MBSR+CBT elements vs enhanced usual care (2-h specialist care + brief CBT) | **Negative trial**: no between-group difference over time on the primary outcome (SF-36 PCS, baseline to 15 months; P = .21). A between-group difference was present at end of treatment. Both groups improved by 15 months. |

**Ongoing:** internet-delivered CBT "One step at a time" vs "Get started" (NCT05525598); patient education ± duloxetine (NCT06232473).

**Gaps and critiques**
- All key trials are single-centre and run by the developers' own group. No independent replication of STreSS has been identified.
- The manual's own authors cautioned that larger multicentre (cost-)effectiveness trials were needed before routine implementation.
- Outcomes are self-report and unblinded for the psychological arms. Effect sizes are modest.
- The imipramine result implies that some of the benefit in this population may come from non-specific or pharmacological central effects rather than CBT-specific mechanisms.
- The BDS validation studies come mainly from the same group.
- **Related but generic evidence:** meta-analyses of CBT, ACT and mindfulness for functional somatic syndromes (e.g., a 2023 meta-analysis of 16 mindfulness/acceptance RCTs) show small-to-moderate effects. A 2025 network meta-analysis (Maas genannt Bermpohl & Martin, *Psychother Psychosom* 94:207–224; 74 RCTs, N = 8,277) found d ≈ 0.5 for CBT and for acceptance/mindfulness-based therapy versus waitlist on somatic symptoms. These support the generic techniques, not STreSS specifically.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1999:** clinic established.
- **2005–2008:** STreSS RCT conducted.
- **2007–2010:** BDS papers published. The 2010 BDS paper is widely cited in ICD-11 discussions.
- **2009:** English manual released free.
- **2012:** BJP publication, the peak academic visibility of STreSS itself.
- **2013–2019:** controversy period.
  - 2013: the Karina Hansen case and the "functional disorders" dispute brought international ME-advocacy attention.
  - 2017 and 2019: STreSS-3 and STreSS-4 published.
  - March 2019: the Danish parliament voted to separate ME (G93.3) from functional disorders.
- **2021:** nationwide regional specialised units, the peak of health-system reach.
- **2022 onward:** internet-delivered and education trials extend reach.
- **Consumer and social-media footprint:** essentially nil.
  - No app, influencer or subreddit.
  - Discussion is concentrated on critical forums (Science for ME, dxrevisionwatch, MEAction) and in academic citations.
- **Reach.** Several hundred patients have been treated in Aarhus trials. Clinical throughput is probably hundreds per year at Aarhus, and more once the regional units opened in 2021. No published annual patient counts were found (checked the department website).

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **ME/CFS as a functional disorder.**
  - Danish and international ME/CFS advocates strongly object to classifying ME/CFS under BDS/functional disorders. They also object to the recommendation of CBT and graded activity, which contrasts with NICE 2021.
  - On 14 March 2019 the Folketing unanimously adopted resolution V 82, asking the health authorities to separate ME/CFS (ICD-10 G93.3) from the umbrella term "functional disorders." Advocates say implementation by the Danish Health Authority lagged.
- **Karina Hansen case (2013–2016).** A young woman with severe ME was forcibly admitted to a neurocentre, reportedly on the advice of Aarhus functional-disorder psychiatrists, and kept from her family for over three years. The case is reported mainly by advocacy sources, and it became an international flashpoint.
- **Construct critique.** Critics argue that BDS lumps heterogeneous conditions, risks missing organic disease, and was positioned to replace established diagnoses. The developers counter that it is aetiology-neutral and reduces iatrogenic over-investigation.
- **Modest effects.** The modest effect sizes and the developer-only evidence base temper claims that BDS is "the" unifying mechanism.

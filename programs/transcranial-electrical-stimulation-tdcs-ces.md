## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **No disease theory of its own.** These are devices, not psychological programs. The implied model is that depression, anxiety, insomnia and chronic pain involve **dysregulated activity or excitability in specific brain circuits and oscillations**, which weak transcranial current can nudge back toward normal.
- **tDCS (Flow).** Constant weak (2 mA) anodal current over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is proposed to **raise cortical excitability** by shifting resting membrane potentials subthreshold, with NMDA-dependent LTP-like after-effects. The rationale is hypoactivity of the left DLPFC in depression and impaired prefrontal top-down control of limbic (amygdala/subgenual cingulate) circuits. For pain (fibromyalgia), anodal stimulation of the primary motor cortex (M1) is thought to modulate thalamic and descending pain-inhibitory pathways and reduce **central sensitization**. Home-tDCS EEG studies in fibromyalgia report changes in insula–ACC connectivity.
- **CES (Alpha-Stim, Fisher Wallace).** Alternating microcurrent (Alpha-Stim, ~0.5 Hz; Fisher Wallace, a proprietary multi-frequency waveform) through the ear lobes or temples is claimed to **shift EEG toward relaxed alpha-dominant states**, modulate brainstem and limbic arousal systems, and alter neurotransmitters (serotonin, endorphins, GABA, cortisol). Manufacturer materials also invoke **autonomic down-regulation** (the "relaxation" response). Fisher Wallace cites a 2025 MGH/Harvard modelling study claiming its current reaches mood-, sleep- and autonomic-regulating structures. These claims rest mainly on EEG and small physiological studies, many from manufacturer-affiliated authors. Current-flow modelling suggests only a small fraction of scalp current reaches the brain at CES intensities.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
- **Mostly unified, with condition-specific montages in research.** Commercially, each device uses one stimulation protocol for all its indications. Alpha-Stim, for example, uses the same ear-clip CES for anxiety, insomnia and depression, and the M model adds probe-based local microcurrent for pain. The implied rationale is general arousal/oscillation normalisation.
- In the **research literature, tDCS is differentiated by target**:
  - left DLPFC anode for depression
  - M1 anode (with contralateral supraorbital cathode) for fibromyalgia and neuropathic pain
  - right DLPFC for craving
  - temporoparietal montages for tinnitus

  Flow's consumer product is DLPFC-only and labelled for depression.
- None of these products makes cross-syndrome claims about ME/CFS, POTS, MCS or similar conditions. Such uses appear only in research pilots (e.g., tDCS for long-COVID fatigue) and in consumer forums.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
- **Flow FL-100 (tDCS).**
  - Dose: 2 mA anodal left DLPFC, 30-min sessions. Pivotal protocol: 5 sessions/week for 3 weeks, then 2–3/week for the remaining weeks, for 10–12 weeks in total.
  - The headset uses saline-soaked sponge pads, which must not be reused or allowed to dry, as burns have been reported. Sessions are scheduled and logged through the smartphone app.
  - The app also delivers a **"behaviour therapy" programme**: short psychoeducational lessons on sleep, exercise, nutrition and meditation (unverified detail; described in Flow marketing).
  - In the US, clinicians prescribe and may remotely monitor adherence. In the EU/UK, consumers buy directly.
- **Alpha-Stim AID/M (CES).**
  - Ear-clip electrodes; the user adjusts current to a comfortable level (µA range).
  - Typically 20–60 min daily for anxiety/insomnia; the manufacturer suggests daily use for several weeks, then as needed.
  - The M model also has handheld probes for "microcurrent electrotherapy" (MET) applied over painful areas.
  - US use requires a prescription (via telehealth or a practitioner form).
- **Fisher Wallace Stimulator / OAK.** Headband with dampened sponge electrodes at the temples, 20 min once or twice daily. The protocol is being refined for the OAK device under FDA review.
- **No psychological or behavioural component is required.** Devices can be used alone or added to medication or psychotherapy. The FDA label for Flow allows either.

## 4. Roots and influences
- 18th–19th-century **galvanism** (Galvani, Volta, Aldini, who treated melancholia with direct current in 1804).
- Early-20th-century **electrosleep** (Leduc).
- Soviet and Eastern Bloc **electrosleep/electroanaesthesia** of the 1950s–60s, brought to the US in the late 1960s–70s as "cranial electrotherapy stimulation".
- The **Liss stimulator** (Saul Liss), the forerunner of Fisher Wallace.
- The 1960s–70s **biofeedback/EEG alpha-state** culture, which Alpha-Stim's naming and alpha-wave claims draw on.
- Modern tDCS revived from **Priori (1998)** and **Nitsche & Paulus (2000)** motor-cortex excitability work.
- **TMS/neuromodulation psychiatry**: DLPFC targeting comes from rTMS for depression.
- **Gate control/neuromatrix pain science**, for the M1 pain montages.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Regulation.**
  - **FDA (US):** CES devices were historically grandfathered Class III. In Dec 2019 the FDA moved CES for anxiety and/or insomnia to Class II and required premarket approval (PMA) for depression, stating that valid evidence for depression was lacking. Alpha-Stim is therefore cleared only for anxiety, insomnia and pain in the US.
  - **Flow** won the first home-tDCS PMA for MDD in Dec 2025. It is prescription-only; the US launch was planned for Q2 2026, and flowneuroscience.com now (Oct 2026) states "Flow is now available" by prescription.
  - **EU:** Flow has been CE-marked and sold over the counter since ~2019. The EU has since reclassified non-medical brain stimulators as high-risk under the MDR (Annex XVI).
- **Guidelines.**
  - IFCN/European evidence-based tDCS guideline (Lefaucheur 2017): **Level B (probable efficacy)** for non-drug-resistant major depression (left DLPFC), fibromyalgia (left M1) and addiction/craving; Level B *inefficacy* for tinnitus and drug-resistant depression.
  - Cochrane (O'Connell 2018): very-low-quality evidence for tDCS in chronic pain.
  - Cochrane (Kavirajan 2014): no eligible RCTs of CES for depression.
  - VA evidence synthesis/Annals of Internal Medicine (Shekelle 2018): insufficient evidence for pain, insomnia and depression, and low-strength evidence of modest benefit in patients with anxiety and depression.
- **Note on CES regulatory status:** Wikipedia (2026) still describes CES as Class III in the US; the Dec 2019 final order reclassified anxiety/insomnia CES to Class II and required PMA for depression. Alpha-Stim's site says "FDA cleared for anxiety, insomnia and pain only".
- **Health systems.** Alpha-Stim has had NHS pilots (e.g., in IAPT/Talking Therapies services, which led to the NIHR-funded Alpha-Stim-D trial). It has US military and VA use, and US$-discount programmes for military and first-responder users. Fisher Wallace has a VA pilot. Flow has been used in some UK NHS settings (unverified scale).
- **Insurance.** Not generally reimbursed in the US. Payment is out of pocket, though Alpha-Stim is HSA/FSA-eligible. Flow said US insurance details would come in 2026.

**Generic home-tDCS and CES trials and reviews (not specific to one brand)**
- Borrione L … Brunoni AR. *JAMA Psychiatry* 2024;81:329–337. PMID 38170541. Three-arm, unsupervised home-tDCS RCT for a major depressive episode (N=210). **No arm superior to sham.**
- Brunoni AR et al. *NEJM* 2017 (ELECT-TDCS; clinic tDCS vs escitalopram vs placebo; N=245). tDCS was superior to placebo but inferior to escitalopram (unverified via database this session; well-known trial).
- Haimi M et al. *JMIR Ment Health* 2026. PMID 42696738. Meta-analysis of 6 sham-controlled home-tDCS RCTs: g=0.36 (95% CI 0.06–0.66). Not robust to removing the largest positive trial (g=0.39, CI −0.12 to 0.91). The two largest trials (unsupervised n=210; self-administered n=141) were negative; only the supervised n=174 trial was positive. Effect called "clinically modest".
- Moshfeghinia R et al. *Sci Rep* 2025. PMID 41398008. 5 home-tDCS RCTs (415 patients): MADRS mean difference −2.7.
- Caumo W et al. *JAMA Netw Open* 2025. PMID 40478572. Home-based tDCS (2 mA, 20 min daily) for **fibromyalgia**, combined with exercise and pain neuroscience education in both arms; double-blind sham RCT, N=112 women. Pain interference (MPII) fell 38.8% vs 16.1% (Cohen d=0.73); ≥50% improvement in 62.5% vs 37.5%. Brazilian academic group (Caumo), not a branded device.
- Fibromyalgia tDCS meta-analyses:
  - Teixeira 2023 *Neuromodulation*, PMID 36435660: 16 RCTs, SMD 1.22.
  - Moshfeghinia 2023 *BMC Neurol*, PMID 37919664: SMD −1.55.
  - Yang 2024 *Neurophysiol Clin*, PMID 38387108: 20 RCTs, SMD −1.04, moderate certainty.
- O'Connell NE et al. Cochrane 2018. PMID 29652088. tDCS for chronic pain: 27 studies, SMD −0.43, very low quality, no effect on disability.
- Lefaucheur JP et al. *Clin Neurophysiol* 2017. PMID 27866120. IFCN guideline ratings (see §5).
- CES:
  - Shekelle PG et al. *Ann Intern Med* 2018. PMID 29435567. 26 RCTs. 28 articles from 26 RCTs. Insufficient evidence for pain, insomnia and depression; low-strength evidence of modest benefit in patients with anxiety and depression.
  - Kavirajan HC et al. Cochrane 2014. PMID 25000907. Zero eligible CES-for-depression RCTs.
  - Ching PY et al. *Front Psychiatry* 2022. PMID 35757229. 11 RCTs, anxiety g=−0.63.
  - Liu SY et al. *J Affect Disord* 2025. PMID 40286924. 16 RCTs (n=1,148), depression g=−0.33.
  - Price L et al. *J Psychiatr Res* 2021. PMID 33477056. CES for depression, d=−0.69 (5 RCTs). Authors affiliated with EPI (Alpha-Stim's maker).
  - A remote home-CES trial for GAD (Gehrman et al., SSRN preprint 2021, ~N=325) found no difference from sham.

**Gaps.**
- Positive CES findings come mostly from small, manufacturer-linked trials. The two largest independent sham-controlled trials (Alpha-Stim-D; remote GAD) were null.
- The effect of home tDCS depends on supervision and adherence: the supervised trial was positive, while the unsupervised and self-administered trials were negative.
- Sham blinding is imperfect, because active tDCS produces more tingling and redness.
- No controlled trials address ME/CFS, POTS, MCS or similar conditions.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **CES.** Popularity was modest from the 1970s to the 1990s. It grew in the mid-2000s with internet marketing and a 2011 *Wall Street Journal* feature, and Fisher Wallace advertised heavily in the 2010s. The 2019 FDA ruling (depression requires a PMA) constrained US claims. Alpha-Stim claims "hundreds of thousands" of users across decades.
- **DIY and consumer tDCS** surged around 2012–2016 (foc.us, Halo Sport, the r/tDCS subreddit and DIY communities). Flow launched in the EU in ~2019 and grew during the COVID-era interest in mental-health tech.
- **Flow user counts:** >55,000 by Dec 2025. Flow also says 77% of users report improvement within 3 weeks (company figure, not trial data).
- **Media peaks:** the Nat Med trial (Oct 2024) and the FDA PMA (Dec 2025), with coverage in Reuters, Bloomberg, NYT, IEEE Spectrum and Psychiatric Times.
- **Fisher Wallace:** >US$11M raised through equity crowdfunding, a VA pilot, and the OAK device under FDA review in 2026.
- **Research volume:** home-tDCS and CES papers increased sharply in 2023–2026, with multiple meta-analyses per year.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Industry funding and conflicts of interest** run through the CES literature (e.g., EPI-affiliated meta-analysis authors). Positive results are weakened by negative large independent RCTs.
- In 2019 the FDA stated there was no valid scientific evidence that CES treats depression. Alpha-Stim nonetheless markets depression claims outside the US.
- Critics (e.g., Wexler, quoted in IEEE Spectrum) argue that the pairing of Flow (medical device) and Halo (a "wellness" device with identical tDCS hardware) blurs the line between consumer products and medical devices.
- Safety: skin burns with dried or reused pads; one suicide attempt in an active arm of Borrione 2024; concerns about long-term unsupervised use and DIY montages.
- Effect sizes for home tDCS are modest and depend on supervision. Fibromyalgia meta-analyses show large pooled effects from small, heterogeneous trials, and Cochrane rates that evidence very low quality.

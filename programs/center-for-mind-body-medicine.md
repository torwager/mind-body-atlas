# The Center for Mind-Body Medicine (CMBM) model: Mind-Body Skills Groups

*Compiled 2026-10-12 from these sources: cmbm.org (About, How We Started, Professional Training Program, Gaza and research pages); PubMed/E-utilities and CrossRef records for CMBM-authored studies; ProPublica Form 990 summary; Quackwatch commentary; interviews with and essays by Gordon. Content is paraphrased.*

## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Stress and trauma physiology.** Chronic stress and trauma keep the body in a prolonged **fight-or-flight** (sympathetic) state, with muscle tension, raised heart rate and blood pressure, disturbed digestion and sleep. This state is said to underlie and worsen both psychological symptoms and physical illness. Gordon presents soft, slow belly breathing as activating the **vagus nerve** as the "antidote" to fight-or-flight. He uses the **relaxation-response** framing of Benson.
- **Trauma as "being stuck."** In *The Transformation* and *Unstuck*, trauma and depression are described as a stuck state: frozen body, constricted imagination, isolation. Expressive and imaginative work (drawing, imagery, movement) and group connection help people move out of it. These are partly psychodynamic and humanistic ideas.
- **Self-awareness, self-care and social support** are themselves treated as therapeutic mechanisms. People learn to regulate their own physiology and emotions, and group belonging counters isolation and helplessness. The model treats empowerment and "self-care as medicine" as central.
- Outcomes are measured with psychological scales. CMBM does not propose a specific neural mechanism such as central sensitization or predictive processing.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Transdiagnostic and not focused on disorders.** The same toolkit and group format are used for war trauma, disaster survivors, cancer patients, depressed adolescents, medical students, veterans, prisoners and clinicians with burnout. Adjustments are made for culture and population (for example, school-based delivery in Gaza, VA Whole Health integration). CMBM does not distinguish mechanisms between named illnesses, and it makes no specific claims about the contested syndromes covered in this survey. It fits them only through the general frame of chronic illness plus stress.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
**Core techniques**, taught didactically and then practised in small groups. The site says the program covers "more than a dozen":
- **Soft-belly breathing:** slow breathing in through the nose and out through the mouth, with the belly relaxed. This is the signature practice and the one participants most often keep up.
- **Shaking and dancing:** about 2–5 minutes of vigorous whole-body shaking to music, then free dance and stillness. This is described as active or dynamic meditation.
- Concentrative and mindfulness meditation, plus **mindful eating** (for example, the raisin exercise).
- **Guided imagery:** a "safe place," an inner guide, imagery for problem-solving and healing.
- **Autogenic training** and **biofeedback**, using hand thermometers and HRV or skin-conductance devices.
- **Self-expression:** drawings (for example, "draw yourself, your biggest problem, your problem solved"), journaling and written dialogue, and **genograms** (family-tree exploration).
- Movement and body-awareness practices, plus self-compassion and forgiveness practices. Nutrition and exercise are covered as self-care.

**Format and dose:**
- **Mind-Body Skills Groups.** Groups of 8–12 meet weekly for about 2 hours over 8–12 weeks: 12 sessions in Kosovo, 10 in Gaza and the VA. Each meeting has a check-in, a new technique, practice, and sharing. Daily home practice is encouraged.
- **Facilitators.** Facilitators do not need to be mental-health professionals. In Kosovo, teachers ran the groups. Facilitators complete the Professional Training Program (now 4 online days, 9:30–18:00 ET, 20–30 CE hours) and an Advanced Training, then do supervised group leading for certification.
- **Free materials.** Gordon's books and the CMBM site give self-guided versions of the practices.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Relaxation response and autogenic training:** Benson; Schultz.
- **Biofeedback:** the 1960s–70s movement.
- **Meditation:** Buddhist and Hindu meditation, and yoga and pranayama breathing.
- **Dynamic meditation:** "Shaking and dancing" derives from Osho/Rajneesh's dynamic meditation. Gordon spent years around that movement (Quackwatch dates it 1973–85) and wrote *The Golden Guru* (1987). Shaking is also linked to qigong and African and shamanic practices.
- **Selye's stress theory.**
- **Guided imagery:** linked to hypnosis.
- **Expressive arts therapy and journaling:** close to Pennebaker's work.
- **Family-systems genograms.**
- **Psychodynamic and humanistic psychiatry:** Gordon's 1970s work with runaway youth and alternative services.
- **Group psychotherapy and self-help groups.**
- **The 1990s integrative and complementary medicine movement.** Gordon was a central figure in it.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Medical education.** Georgetown's medical school runs a Mind-Body Medicine course developed with CMBM, which started with NCCAM funding. CMBM-trained faculty run groups at more than 15 US medical schools (Gordon 2014). Similar courses exist at Cincinnati, Florida (PRIMe), Indiana and Maryland, and in the Netherlands and Sweden. A JAMA systematic review of medical-student wellbeing interventions (Wasson 2016) counted 7 mind-body skills programs among 28 studies. It rated the overall evidence quality as low.
- **Military and VA.** Gordon describes about a decade of work with the VHA, including training 200 Whole Health coaches and 150 leaders in VISN 8 (2019) and a VA-site RCT.
- **Public mental health abroad.** In Kosovo, the model was integrated into the postwar community mental-health system. In Gaza, CMBM has had a program since 2005 run by local staff, and the "Hope and Healing for Gaza Schools" initiative is ongoing.
- **US communities.** Programs have run with Broward County schools, in Puerto Rico, at Pine Ridge, and in post-Katrina Louisiana.
- **Guidelines.** The model is not named in NICE, VA/DoD or APA PTSD guidelines.
- **Insurance.** No billing code exists for the branded model. Coverage is indirect, through licensed clinicians billing group therapy or through institutional and grant funding.

## 6. Evidence base
Trials of the branded CMBM Mind-Body Skills Group model. All were led or co-authored by CMBM (Gordon and/or Staples).

| Study | Design / N | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Gordon, Staples, Blyta, Bytyqi. *J Trauma Stress* 2004;17:143 (PMID 15141787) | Uncontrolled pilot; 139 Kosovar high-school students; 6-week program | PTSD reaction-index scores fell and stayed lower at follow-up. No control group. |
| Gordon, Staples, Blyta, Bytyqi, Wilson. *J Clin Psychiatry* 2008;69:1469 (PMID 18945398) | **RCT**; 82 adolescents with PTSD (Harvard Trauma Questionnaire); 12 sessions vs waitlist; teacher-led | Greater PTSD reduction than waitlist (F=29.8, p<.001; mean 2.5→2.0 vs 2.5→2.4). Gains held at 3 months, and the waitlist improved once treated. Self-report only; not blinded. CMBM markets this as an "82% reduction" in PTSD caseness and the "first RCT of any intervention with war-traumatized adolescents." |
| Staples, Abdel Atti, Gordon. *Int J Stress Manag* 2011;18:246 (doi 10.1037/a0024015) | Gaza children and adolescents; uncontrolled pre–post with 7-month follow-up | CMBM reports large drops in PTSD-positive rates and in depression and hopelessness. The developers report an 89% (website) or 91% (2022 CMBM research summary) reduction in PTSD caseness. N is not stated on CMBM's pages; the article is closed-access and its abstract is not in PubMed, Europe PMC, CrossRef or Semantic Scholar. Uncontrolled. |
| Gordon, Staples, He, Abdel Atti. *Traumatology* 2016;22(3):155-64 (doi 10.1037/trm0000081) | 92 Gaza adults with PTSD; 10 sessions; pre–post with 10-month follow-up | Improvements in PTSD, depression, anxiety and quality of life were maintained at follow-up. Uncontrolled. |
| Staples, Gordon, Hamilton, Uddo. *Psychol Trauma* 2022;14:1016 (epub 2020; PMID 32134288) | **RCT**; 108 veterans at a VA PTSD clinic; 10-week groups vs standard treatment | Greater improvement in total PTSD score, hyperarousal and avoidance at 10 weeks. Only the hyperarousal gain was maintained at 2 months. Anger and sleep improved. No difference in depression, anxiety, post-traumatic growth or quality of life. |
| Aalsma et al. *J Pediatr Health Care* 2020;34:462 (PMID 32861425) | Single-arm; 43 depressed adolescents in primary care | Depression improved through the 3-month follow-up; good acceptability. |
| Staples & Gordon. *Altern Ther Health Med* 2005;11:36 (PMID 16053120) | Repeated-measures survey of 451 trainees (1998–2001) | More personal and professional use of mind-body skills, and higher existential wellbeing. |
| Staples et al. *Healthcare (Basel)* 2026;14:746 (PMID 41897200) | Uncontrolled; 31 of 38 incarcerated trainees completed the 8-day training | Improved resilience, depression, anxiety and stress, maintained at 6 months. COI: authors are paid by CMBM and the grant. |

**Related, independent studies of adapted "mind-body skills" courses for medical students.** These are nonrandomized or controlled-cohort designs:
- Greeson 2015, *Explore*, n=44
- Kraemer 2016, *Teach Learn Med*, n=52
- Novak 2020, *Glob Adv Health Med*, 279 participants vs 247 controls; gains mostly not sustained at 1 year
- Saunders 2007, *Med Teach*, qualitative

**Registered trials:**
- NCT01595477: Gaza children, randomized vs no-intervention control (n=114, 2012)
- NCT01595490: Gaza adolescents, randomized vs no-intervention control (n=121, 2012)
- NCT01595464: Gaza adults, randomized (n=148, 2012). The published adult study (Traumatology 2016, n=92) reports only pre–post data.
- NCT02671487: Gaza male adolescents vs waitlist (n=115, completed 2017). CMBM's 2022 summary describes a boys'-school controlled study "being prepared for publication"; no paper found in PubMed as of Oct 2026.
- NCT01093053: military and veterans (n=108, completed 2013; published as Staples 2022)
- NCT00136357: Kosovo adolescents (n=82; published as Gordon 2008)
- NCT05948917: incarcerated people (n=140, completed 2025; no results posted). NCT05622799 (n=46) is a related prison training study.
- NCT07302984: new Gaza adolescent RCT (n=120), recruiting since Dec 2025.

None of the three 2012 Gaza RCT registrations (NCT01595477, NCT01595490, NCT01595464) has results posted on ClinicalTrials.gov, and no randomized results were found in PubMed (searched Oct 2026); these completed-but-unpublished randomized trials are a possible publication-bias concern.

**Gaps:**
- Only two RCTs, both by the developers and both with waitlist or usual-care controls.
- No active-control comparisons against CBT, prolonged exposure or MBSR, and no blinded assessment.
- Outcomes are self-report only.
- No trials in chronic pain, fibromyalgia, ME/CFS, IBS or other functional syndromes, and no biomarker or mechanistic studies.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1991:** founded.
- **1996:** *Manifesto for a New Medicine*.
- **1997–98:** first Kosovo and Macedonia work. Week-long professional trainings ran annually from at least 1998, with 451 trainees in 1998–2001.
- **2000–02:** Gordon's high profile as chair of the White House CAM commission.
- **2004–08:** Kosovo RCT, and the Gaza program begins in 2005.
- **2005–2010s:** Katrina (2005), Haiti (2010, more than 60,000 people served), Israel, Pine Ridge (2015), and VA work.
- **2018:** Parkland and Puerto Rico.
- **2019:** *The Transformation* (HarperOne). This brought broad media exposure, including goop and many podcasts.
- **2020:** training moved online during COVID, with "Meditations for the pandemic" outreach.
- **2023–26:** Gaza war work drew press coverage and a Clinton Global Initiative commitment to reach more than 500,000 people.

**Scale:**
- Cumulative trainees: more than 7,000 (2019–2024 site) and more than 8,000 (2026).
- Revenue: about USD 5.8M (2024).
- Online presence: modest, with little patient-community discussion compared with consumer brain-retraining programs. Reddit could not be checked directly: reddit.com blocked automated requests (about.json for r/mindbodymedicine and r/CenterforMindBodyMedicine, Oct 2026). No dedicated subreddit is known.

## 8. Controversies and criticisms
- **Gordon as CAM advocate.** Gordon's chairmanship of the White House CAM commission was criticised in a multi-part Quackwatch/NCAHF series (Stephen Barrett, E. Patrick Curry, Wallace Sampson and others). They argued the commission was stacked with CAM proponents and that Gordon was a partial advocate for untested practices. Quackwatch also raised what it called a 12-year (1973–85) involvement with the Rajneesh movement; Gordon wrote *The Golden Guru* (1987) about it and reportedly denied being a disciple. These criticisms target his CAM policy role rather than the MBSG model itself.
- **Evidence quality.** The evidence is almost entirely developer-generated, mostly uncontrolled, and comes from the field in war settings. Outcome claims are framed strongly on the site (for example, "82%", "89%"/"91%" and "86%" reductions in PTSD caseness from uncontrolled or waitlist designs).
- **Politics of the Gaza work.** Working in Gaza has drawn political attention, but no substantive scientific controversy was found.

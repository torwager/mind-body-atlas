## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Predisposing / precipitating / perpetuating framework.** A trigger starts the illness, usually a viral infection, sometimes stress or overwork. Different factors are then held to keep it going after the trigger has resolved.
- **Perpetuating factors (UK model).** Two strands:
  - *Illness beliefs.* Patients attribute symptoms to ongoing disease and fear that activity causes harm.
  - *Behaviour.* This produces avoidance of activity, or "boom-and-bust" swings between over-activity and rest.
  - *Result.* Prolonged rest leads to physical deconditioning, disturbed sleep and circadian rhythm, more attention to bodily sensations, and lower exercise tolerance. These generate more symptoms, which then appear to confirm the beliefs.
  - This is a fear-avoidance plus deconditioning loop (Wessely et al. 1989; Fulcher & White 1997 framed GET around deconditioning and exercise intolerance).
- **Perpetuating factors (Nijmegen model).** Vercoulen et al. 1998 is a path model. Its components are low sense of control over fatigue, focusing on fatigue, a somatic causal attribution, low or fluctuating physical activity, sleep disturbance and lack of social support. Knoop's mediation work identifies sense of control, focus on fatigue and perceived (not actual) activity as the change mechanisms. Actigraphy shows CBT reduces fatigue without increasing measured activity.
- **Later framing.** The model has been reframed more neutrally as a "biopsychosocial" or "cognitive-behavioural" model that does not deny physical pathology. Some authors (e.g., Chalder, Knoop) link it to central sensitisation, predictive processing and interoceptive models of symptom perception, though these are post hoc framings and not part of the original model.
- **What the model does not posit.** It proposes no immune or mitochondrial driver of perpetuation. Post-exertional malaise (PEM) is read as a deconditioning/expectation phenomenon, not as a distinct physiological intolerance. This is the core point of contention with patient groups and with the NICE 2021 and IOM 2015 framings.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Mostly unified, with condition-specific emphasis.** The same predisposing/precipitating/perpetuating logic covers:
- CFS/ME and post-infectious fatigue (Q fever, EBV, COVID-19: ReCOVer explicitly says the infection triggers fatigue and cognitive-behavioural factors perpetuate it);
- fatigue in MS and cancer, where perpetuating factors are assessed separately from the disease;
- IBS;
- persistent physical symptoms generally.

PRINCE Secondary made this explicit. It is a single **transdiagnostic** CBT for any persistent physical symptoms (CFS, IBS, fibromyalgia, non-cardiac chest pain and others), with modules tailored to the symptom. Distinctions are made at the level of which perpetuating factors are present in a given patient:
- activity pattern: "relatively active", "fluctuating" or "low active" patients get different activity regulation;
- sleep;
- illness beliefs.

They are not made at the level of disease-specific pathophysiology.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
**CBT for CFS** (PACE manual; Nijmegen protocol; about 12–16 sessions over 4–6 months, ~50–60 min each; PACE gave 14 sessions plus a booster):
- Formulation using the vicious-circle model, and agreed personal goals (work, social, physical).
- Activity regulation. Stabilise a consistent baseline of activity and rest, eliminating boom-bust swings, then increase activity gradually and **time-contingently**, i.e. by the clock, not by symptoms.
- Regular sleep-wake schedule. Cut daytime napping and fix rise times.
- Cognitive restructuring of unhelpful beliefs: that activity is harmful, that symptoms signify damage, perfectionism, all-or-nothing thinking.
- Reduce focus on fatigue. Attention shifting, and fewer symptom checks and diary entries.
- Address stress, mood and social support. Plan the return to work or school and relapse prevention.

**GET** (PACE manual; physiotherapist-led, about 14 sessions):
- Set a baseline of sustainable physical activity, often walking, monitored by heart rate. Increase duration then intensity by ~10–20% a week, aiming for about 30 min of light exercise five days a week.
- Symptom increases are framed as expected and temporary, a sign of deconditioning, and not a reason to stop.
- **GETSET** is a booklet-based guided self-help version (12 weeks; up to four calls with a physiotherapist, ~90 min total).

**Delivery variants:**
- Nurse-led "pragmatic rehabilitation" at home (FINE).
- Web-based CBT with therapist feedback (Janse 2018).
- Blended CBT for post-COVID fatigue, about 17 weeks (ReCOVer).
- Paediatric family-focused CBT, FITNET internet CBT for adolescents, and MAGENTA (GET vs activity management) for children.

**Control/comparator therapies, for context:**
- Specialist medical care (SMC).
- Adaptive Pacing Therapy (APT): energy-envelope pacing, staying within limits.
- Relaxation/flexibility.
- Supportive listening.

## 4. Roots and influences
- Beckian **CBT** and the behavioural treatment of chronic pain: Fordyce's operant model, the **fear-avoidance** models of Vlaeyen and Lethem, and "pacing up" or quota-based activity.
- **Behaviourism / classical and operant conditioning.**
- Sports-medicine **deconditioning** and exercise-physiology rehabilitation (cardiac rehab, graded activity in low back pain).
- Engel's **biopsychosocial model** and the British liaison-psychiatry tradition of "functional somatic syndromes": Wessely, Sharpe and Hotopf's "one or many" argument (Wessely, Nimnuan & Sharpe, Lancet 1999).
- Historical continuity with **neurasthenia** and the rest-cure debates. Wessely's historical writing explicitly links CFS to neurasthenia.
- It is a precursor to, and partly a foil for, later brain-retraining programs. The Lightning Process, DNRS, Gupta and PRT share its cognitive-behavioural logic of "perpetuating factors, not ongoing damage" but recast it as neuroplastic or limbic.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **UK.** The 2002 CMO report and NICE CG53 (2007) recommended CBT and GET as the main evidence-based treatments. NHS specialist CFS/ME services (~50+ teams) built around them. DWP and insurers expected engagement. NICE **NG206 (Oct 2021)** reversed this:
  - do not offer GET or any therapy based on fixed incremental exercise, or physical activity framed as a cure;
  - CBT may be offered only as supportive symptom management, not curative;
  - recommends energy management/pacing.

  Publication was paused in August 2021 after professional objections. On publication (29 Oct 2021) a joint statement from the RCP, RCP Edinburgh, RCGP, RCPsych, Academy of Medical Royal Colleges, FSEM and FOM said the guideline understated the importance of activity and exercise and questioned how the evidence was assessed. Before publication three committee members resigned and a fourth (Charles Shepherd) was stood down by NICE (BMJ, Aug 2021).
- **USA.** The CDC removed CBT and GET from its patient-facing ME/CFS pages in July 2017. The IOM (2015) and AHRQ evidence reanalysis (2016) had already undercut them. Patient-information publisher Healthwise (used on Kaiser Permanente's site) reportedly retired its GET/CBT content in 2018 (patient-forum report, not officially confirmed). CBT for comorbid distress remains reimbursable as psychotherapy.
- **Netherlands.** The Health Council (Gezondheidsraad, 2018 report to parliament) saw no reason to recommend GET. It judged that CBT might help some ambulatory patients but was not an adequate treatment for ME/CFS overall, and said refusing it should not affect benefits. Dutch CBT for fatigue (including post-COVID) remains insured and offered in academic centres.
- **Other.** Several national guidelines (e.g., Belgium's former reference centres, Norway, Denmark) used CBT/GET models and have been revised or contested since 2021. The approach remains embedded in UK persistent-physical-symptom and IAPT long-term-conditions pathways.

## 6. Evidence base (verified trials; self-report endpoints unless stated)
**Early UK and Dutch RCTs**
- **Sharpe et al. 1996, BMJ 312:22–26.** RCT, N=60, Oxford infectious-diseases clinic; 16 weekly sessions of CBT plus medical care vs medical care. At 12 months more than twice as many patients in the CBT arm reached normal daily functioning.
- **Deale et al. 1997, Am J Psychiatry 154:408–414.** RCT, N=60 (KCL; Chalder, Wessely). 13 sessions of CBT vs relaxation therapy; CBT was superior on functional impairment and fatigue at 6 months.
- **Fulcher & White 1997, BMJ 314:1647.** RCT, N=66. GET vs flexibility/relaxation; self-rated "much/very much better" at 12 weeks in 16/29 vs 8/30 completers (~55% vs 27%); ITT 17/33 vs 9/33 (~52% vs 27%).
- **Prins et al. 2001, Lancet 357:841–847** (Bleijenberg, Nijmegen). Multicentre RCT, N=278. CBT vs guided support vs natural course; CBT was superior on fatigue and impairment at 8 and 14 months. Effects were modest, many CBT patients did not improve on fatigue, and the trial was unblinded (PEDro 6/10).

**PACE and its sister trial**
- **White et al. 2011, Lancet 377:823–836 (PACE).** RCT, N=641, UK multicentre (QMUL/KCL/Edinburgh; funded by MRC, DH, DWP and the Scottish government). SMC alone vs SMC plus APT, CBT or GET. At 52 weeks CBT and GET each beat SMC on fatigue (Chalder scale, about −3.4 and −2.5 points) and SF-36 physical function (about +7 and +9). APT did not differ from SMC.
  - **Outcome switching.** The protocol-specified primary outcomes and the "recovery" definition were changed before analysis.
  - **Objective measures.** The 6-minute walk test showed a small gain with GET only, and still in a severely impaired range. Step test, employment and benefits showed no meaningful difference.
- **Sharpe et al. 2015, Lancet Psychiatry 2:1067–1074 (PACE long-term follow-up).** Of 604 sent questionnaires, 481 (75%) responded, median 31 months after randomisation; 44% had received further treatment after the trial (63% of SMC and 50% of APT participants vs ~31% of CBT/GET). Gains in the CBT and GET arms were maintained, but the SMC and APT arms caught up, leaving **no between-group differences**. The authors attributed this to crossover to CBT/GET; critics noted the comparison had stopped being randomised.
- **Wilshire et al. 2018, BMC Psychology 6:6** (Wilshire, Kindlon, Courtney, Matthees, Tuller, Geraghty, Levin; independent reanalysis of tribunal-released PACE data using protocol-specified outcomes; recovery figures first reported in Wilshire et al. 2017, *Fatigue: Biomedicine, Health & Behavior*). Protocol-defined recovery was 7% with CBT, 4% with GET and 3% with SMC (p=0.14), against 22%, 22% and 7% under the revised definition published by White et al. 2013 (Psychol Med). On the protocol's "overall improvement" outcome, 20% (CBT) and 21% (GET) improved vs 10% (SMC); neither comparison survived the protocol's Bonferroni correction.
- **Wearden et al. 2010, BMJ c1777 (FINE).** RCT, N=296, primary care, homebound or less mobile patients. Nurse-led pragmatic rehabilitation (graded activity plus CBT elements) gave a small short-term fatigue benefit, **not significant at 1-year follow-up**. Supportive listening was ineffective.

**Later trials and reviews**
- **Clark et al. 2017, Lancet 390:363–373 (GETSET).** RCT, N=211. Guided graded-exercise self-help plus SMC vs SMC. There were 12-week improvements in fatigue (effect size ~0.5) and a small effect on physical function (~0.2). Adherence was modest. The long-term follow-up (Clark et al. 2021, J Psychosom Res 146:110484; 164/211 at ~15 months) found **no difference between arms on the primary outcomes**, though the authors still judged it probably cost-effective.
- **Janse et al. 2018, Br J Psychiatry 212:112–118.** RCT, N=240, Netherlands. Web-based CBT (protocol-driven or on-demand feedback) vs waitlist; both iCBT arms reduced fatigue severity (CIS) vs waitlist.
- **Chalder et al., Psychological Medicine (online Sept 2021, print 2023; doi:10.1017/S0033291721001793) (PRINCE Secondary).** RCT, N=324, transdiagnostic CBT plus SMC vs SMC for persistent physical symptoms. The **primary outcome (WSAS at 52 weeks) was null** (difference −1.48, 95% CI −3.44 to 0.48, p=0.14). Some secondary outcomes favoured CBT, and critics (Tack & Tuller) faulted the positive framing.
- **Gaunt, Crawley et al. 2024, Eur J Pediatr (MAGENTA).** Pragmatic RCT, N=241 children aged 8–17 with mild/moderate ME/CFS (123 GET, 118 activity management). **Null for the primary outcome** (SF-36 PF at 6 months: adjusted difference −2.02, 95% CI −7.75 to 2.70), with little improvement in either arm.
- **Kuut et al. 2023, Clin Infect Dis (ReCOVer).** RCT, N=114, severe fatigue 3–12 months after COVID-19, six Dutch hospitals. 17-week blended CBT vs care as usual; CIS-fatigue difference −8.8 (95% CI −11.9 to −5.8), d≈0.69, sustained at 6 months, and all secondary outcomes favoured CBT. It was unblinded with a CAU comparator and self-report outcomes.
- **Kuut et al. 2024 (online Nov 2023), Psychological Medicine.** Individual-patient-data meta-analysis of eight RCTs of the Dutch (Nijmegen) CBT protocol, all from the same research group (n=1,298). CBT reduced fatigue, functional impairment and improved physical functioning; the fatigue effect was moderated by age, impairment and activity pattern (younger, less impaired and "fluctuating-activity" patients benefited more). Critics note it pooled only the developers' own trials.

**Systematic reviews**
- **Price et al. 2008, Cochrane CD001027.** CBT reduced fatigue at post-treatment vs usual care, with weaker and inconsistent evidence at follow-up. The review is flagged as not current (pre-GRADE).
- **Larun et al. 2019, Cochrane CD003200 (exercise therapy).** Amended in October 2019 after complaints. It now says exercise probably reduces fatigue at end of treatment, with uncertain long-term benefit and harms, and limited applicability to ME/CFS definitions that require PEM. An update is pending (a Cochrane independent advisory group was set up in 2020).
- **NICE NG206 evidence review (2021).** All CBT and GET evidence was rated low or very low quality (GRADE), owing to:
  - lack of blinding combined with subjective outcomes;
  - indirectness from the Oxford criteria, which do not require PEM;
  - small or inconsistent effects.

**Independence and harms.** Most positive trials were run by the model's originators. Independent reanalyses (Wilshire; Vink & Vink-Niese) are negative. Patient surveys (e.g., ME Association 2015, n~1,400) report frequent worsening after GET, whereas the trials reported no excess serious adverse events.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1989–1997, model and first RCTs.** Wessely et al. 1989 set out the model, the Oxford criteria followed in 1991, the Chalder Fatigue Scale in 1993, and the first RCTs in 1996–97.
- **1998–2007, institutional uptake.** The Nijmegen model and trials appeared (Vercoulen 1998; Prins 2001). The 2002 CMO report and NICE CG53 (2007) made CBT/GET the standard, and NHS services expanded with £8.5m DH investment from 2004.
- **2011, peak prominence.** PACE was published in the Lancet with heavy media coverage ("CBT and exercise can help CFS").
- **2015–2018, collapse of credibility.**
  - Tuller's ~15,000-word "Trial By Error" exposé appeared on Virology Blog in October 2015, followed by open letters to The Lancet from scientists and clinicians (dozens of signatories in 2016, 100+ by 2018).
  - A First-Tier Tribunal ordered QMUL to release the data in August 2016.
  - Wilshire's reanalysis came out in 2016–18, and the CDC dropped CBT/GET in July 2017.
  - A UK parliamentary debate followed in 2018, along with the Dutch Health Council report.
- **2019–2021, guideline reversal.** Cochrane amended its review in 2019, and NICE reversed its guidance with NG206 (draft November 2020; final October 2021).
- **2020–2026, reorientation.** Activity moved to long COVID (ReCOVer 2023; Dutch implementation; UK long-COVID clinics debating rehab vs pacing) and to transdiagnostic persistent physical symptoms (PRINCE). As a CFS treatment it is in decline. Its main ongoing presence is controversy: S4ME and Phoenix Rising threads, Tuller's continuing posts, and debates in BMJ/JRSM.
- **Reach.** A few thousand trial participants (e.g., the eight Dutch RCTs pooled n≈1,300; PACE 641). UK specialist services are estimated (unverified; no official figure found) to have seen about 8,000–10,000 new patients a year for roughly 15 years, and the Dutch centres several thousand. Cumulative reach is likely ~10^5–10^5.5 patients directly treated, with far larger indirect reach through guidelines and GP advice.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **PACE methodology.**
  - Primary outcomes and recovery thresholds were changed mid-trial. The SF-36 "normal range" overlapped the entry criterion: a patient could deteriorate and still be counted as recovered.
  - The trial was unblinded and relied on self-report, while a mid-trial newsletter promoted the therapies to participants.
  - Objective outcomes were null, and the published case definition (Oxford) did not require PEM.
  - Data release was refused until the 2016 tribunal ruling.
- **Conflicts of interest.** Some investigators had links to insurers and to the DWP, and DWP co-funded the trial.
- **Harms.** Patient groups report GET-induced relapses consistent with PEM. NICE's 2021 decision was partly based on the qualitative evidence of harm.
- **Polarisation.** Investigators reported harassment and described the activist campaign as anti-psychiatry; patients describe "psychologisation" and disbelief. In 2023 White, Sharpe, Chalder and others criticised NG206's evidence handling. Both sides remain entrenched.
- **Long COVID extension.** The ReCOVer trial's targeting of "dysfunctional beliefs about fatigue" drew similar criticism. Supporters point to the medium effect size and the absence of serious adverse events.
- **Relevance to this survey.** This is the mainstream, trial-tested ancestor of the "perpetuated by brain and behaviour, not ongoing damage" model. Its trajectory (large RCTs, then guideline endorsement, then reanalysis, then reversal) is the main precedent for evaluating the claims of the newer brain-retraining programs.

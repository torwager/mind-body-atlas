## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)

- **Pain is a whole-person, brain-processed experience.** The model is standard pain-CBT and biopsychosocial. Thoughts, emotions, attention and stress shape how the nervous system processes pain signals. The class teaches "pain neuroscience education" that frames pain as modifiable by mind and body, not purely as tissue damage.
- **Pain catastrophizing is the central treatment target.** Catastrophizing means rumination, magnification and helplessness about pain. Darnall's papers describe it as amplifying distress and engaging "pain-facilitating neural patterns." It also predicts chronicity, opioid use and poor surgical outcomes, and its reduction mediates CBT benefits and changes brain structure and function. The primary outcome in most trials is the Pain Catastrophizing Scale.
- **Stress physiology and self-regulation.** Distress is framed as up-regulating the nervous system and pain. The relaxation response and self-soothing are taught to calm physiological arousal.
- **Placebo/nocebo and expectancy.** Darnall's 2018 review argues that optimizing positive expectancy and minimizing nocebo reduces pain, catastrophizing and opioid use. The class fosters self-efficacy ("empowerment") and positive expectations.
- **Not claimed:** that pain is "all in the brain," that symptoms are learned or neuroplastic without structural cause (as in PRT or Sarno), that a limbic system is impaired, or that chronic illness can be cured. The class is positioned as an *adjunct* to medical care that reduces suffering and interference.

## 2. One mechanism or many?

**One transdiagnostic mechanism, one curriculum.** The same 2-hour class is used for acute, chronic and post-surgical pain of any cause, including genetic disease (Marfan), autoimmune disease (lupus), back pain, and opioid-treated and OUD populations. The program does not claim pain conditions share one cause. It claims that the *psychological modulation* of pain (catastrophizing, distress, attention, self-efficacy) is common across them and is the treatment target. Population adaptations change the examples and framing, not the mechanism:
- the youth version (ERY)
- the surgical version (*My Surgical Success*)
- the opioid-misuse version (MOBILE Relief)

Non-pain conditions (fatigue syndromes, MCS, POTS, anxiety or depression as primary targets) are outside its scope.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies

Dose: **one 2-hour session**, manualized, slide-deck based, taught to a group by a certified instructor. There is no homework schedule beyond a personal plan and the audio. Components (from the JAMA 2021 methods and Simons 2026):
1. **Pain neuroscience education.** How the brain and nervous system process pain and how thoughts, emotions and stress turn pain up or down.
2. **Identifying distressing thoughts and emotions.** Recognizing catastrophizing as it happens.
3. **Cognitive reframing.** Restructuring catastrophic and unhelpful thoughts.
4. **Relaxation-response exercise.** Diaphragmatic breathing and calming the body, practised in class.
5. **Mindfulness principles.** Present-moment, non-judgmental awareness of pain and distress.
6. **Self-soothing action plan.** Each participant writes a personalized "Empowerment/Relief Plan" listing specific skills and soothing strategies to use during pain flares.
7. **Take-home 20-minute relaxation audio** (MP3 with binaural tones) for daily practice.

Delivery variants:
- *My Surgical Success*: a self-paced digital version given around the time of surgery.
- On-demand video ER (MOBILE Relief).
- Instructors from nursing, physical therapy and social work.
- Spanish-language and Danish/UK/Australian/Canadian sites.

## 4. Roots and influences

- **CBT for chronic pain** (Turk, Keefe, Thorn), which Empowered Relief condenses. The PROGRESS protocol includes B. Thorn, author of a widely used group pain-CBT manual.
- **Pain catastrophizing research** (Sullivan's PCS; fear-avoidance models).
- **Benson's relaxation response** and relaxation training.
- **Mindfulness-based approaches** (MBSR-derived principles).
- **Pain neuroscience education**, from gate-control and neuromatrix pain science (Melzack; Moseley/Butler "Explain Pain").
- **Placebo/expectancy science.**
- **Single-session intervention movement** in psychotherapy.
- **Stanford chronic disease self-management tradition** (Kate Lorig is a co-author on the RCTs).

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance

- **Health systems.** Stanford Pain Management Center was the origin. Cleveland Clinic Center for Spine Health made it standard of care for chronic pain and lumbar spine-surgery patients, launched online around the onset of COVID-19 (2020) after a 2019–2020 feasibility project; 72% engagement over Jan–Dec 2021 (Davin et al. 2022). Other adopters:
  - Allegheny Health Network: network-wide rollout, free to all patients after a 2022 donation.
  - Lehigh Valley Health Network
  - Kaiser Permanente
  - VA clinics
  - NHS sites
  - Intermountain, Cedars-Sinai, Brigham and Women's
  - Humana, as a research partner in PROGRESS
- **Federal and policy.** The 2019 HHS Pain Management Best Practices Inter-Agency Task Force report recommends behavioral pain care. It does not name Empowered Relief but cites the 2014 "From Catastrophizing to Recovery" pilot (ref. 73) twice: once on pre- and perioperative behavioral interventions, and once on low-cost, scalable telehealth options. Funded by NIH (NCCIH, NIDA) and PCORI. The PCORI-funded trials include PROGRESS (1- vs 8-session online treatment, national) and EMPOWER (opioid tapering with behavioral treatments, N≈1,365). The Department of Defense is funding the PROs trial (608 TRICARE beneficiaries).
- **Professional bodies.** CME/CE credits are accredited for physicians, nurses, pharmacists, psychologists (APA co-sponsored), social workers, PAs and PT/OT. The American Society for Pain Management Nursing has run its own national RCT of nurse-delivered ER.
- **Insurance.** No ER-specific coverage policy. US systems bill it as a group **Health & Behavior Assessment and Intervention** service (CPT 9615x–9616x family) for patients with a medical diagnosis (Davin et al. 2022). Reimbursement therefore depends on the clinician's licence and the payer. Some systems offer it free or bundled into perioperative care.

## 6. Evidence base

All trials below are of the **branded intervention** and come from Darnall's Stanford group or its close collaborators. Stanford, which owns the trademark, receives certification revenue, and this is disclosed.

| Study | Design / N | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Darnall et al. 2014, *J Pain Res* 7:219 (PMID 24851056) | Uncontrolled pilot of "From Catastrophizing to Recovery," Stanford pain clinic; N=57 with baseline PCS (46 at week 2, 35 at week 4) | PCS fell at 2 and 4 weeks (d=0.85, 1.15). Follow-up attrition was high. |
| Darnall et al. 2021, *JAMA Netw Open* 4:e2113401 (PMID 34398206; NCT03167086) | 3-arm RCT in chronic low back pain: ER vs 8×2-h group CBT vs matched health education; N=263 | At 3 mo ER was **non-inferior to CBT** on PCS (−9.1 vs −10.9 pts) and superior to health education (between-group difference −5.9 pts; health education itself fell −4.6). It was also non-inferior on pain intensity, interference, sleep, depression and anxiety, but **inferior to CBT on physical function**. Sample was mostly White, highly educated and single-site; outcomes were self-reported and unblinded; ~20% dropped out before treatment, with more dropout in health education. |
| Darnall et al. 2024, *Pain Rep* 9:e1116 (PMID 38288134) | 6-month follow-up of the above | Non-inferiority to CBT held on most outcomes and ER stayed superior to health education. Some outcomes improved further from 3 to 6 mo. |
| Ziadni et al. 2021, *J Med Internet Res* 23:e29672 (PMID 34505832; NCT04546685) | Zoom-delivered ER vs waitlist, mixed chronic pain; N=104 (101 analysed) | ER beat waitlist on all outcomes at 3 mo (d=0.45–0.79). PCS fell 42% with ER vs 11% with waitlist. Unblinded, with no active control. |
| Watson et al. 2026, *Pain Manag Nurs* 27:50 (PMID 41238484) | National RCT, ER delivered by ASPMN nurses vs waitlist; N=150 | Improvements at 8 weeks in PCS (p<.001), pain intensity, depression, anxiety, fatigue and self-efficacy. |
| Ziadni et al. 2026, *J Pain* 44:106286 (PMID 41990980; NCT03950791) | RCT of Zoom ER vs attention-matched health education in long-term opioid users; N=213 | **Null on the primary outcome** (opioid dose) and on pain interference. Only PROMIS global mental health improved more with ER. |
| Darnall et al. 2019, *Pain Med* 20:2228 (PMID 31087093) | Pilot RCT, digital *My Surgical Success* vs digital health education after breast-cancer surgery; 127 randomized, analysed N=68 | Faster opioid cessation (HR 1.86, 95% CI 1.12–3.10). No difference in pain or PCS. 44% attrition in the MSS arm. |
| Ziadni et al. 2022, *Anesth Analg* 135:394 (PMID 35696706) | RCT, *My Surgical Success* vs health education after orthopedic trauma surgery; 133 enrolled, 84 randomized/analysed | Group × time effect favouring MSS on pain intensity up to 3 mo (P=.024). **No difference** in time to opioid cessation or in PCS (baseline PCS was subclinical). 63% engagement. |
| Uncontrolled and single-arm studies | Carrière 2025 (PT-delivered, N=63, PMID 41000505); Perez 2026 (Marfan, N=92, PMID 41495090); You 2026 (methadone/buprenorphine, N=51, PMID 41812186); Simons 2026 (youth, N=110, PMID 42542155) | Feasible and acceptable, with pre-post improvements. Not controlled. |
| Failed feasibility | Jethwa 2026, post-cesarean (PMID 42349309) | Terminated because too few patients were eligible. |
| Ongoing | PROGRESS (1- vs 8-session, PMID 41712512); Lupus PROGRESS (ER vs CBT, PMID 42641149); MOBILE Relief (PMID 39122392); PROs military (PMID 42834451); gender-affirming surgery (PMID 41402818) | — |

**Mechanistic secondary analysis.** Kim et al. 2026, *J Pain* (PMID 42633813), found that in the low back pain RCT, sleep improvement with ER or CBT ran through reduced pain interference and catastrophizing, not through reduced pain intensity.

**Gaps:**
- No trial without Darnall as co-author. A few studies are led from other institutions (Watson 2026, nurse-led; Carrière 2025, Quebec; PROs, Fritz/Utah), but all include her.
- No active-placebo (sham) control with blinding.
- Outcomes are almost entirely self-reported, and the primary target (catastrophizing) is a proxy.
- Follow-up extends to 6 months at most.
- Samples are mostly White and highly educated.
- It failed to reduce opioid use when that was the primary goal.
- No head-to-head comparisons with PRT, EAET or physiotherapy-based education.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory

- **2013–2016.** A free clinic class at Stanford, with the pilot published in 2014. Darnall's consumer book *Less Pain, Fewer Pills* also came out in 2014.
- **2017–2019.** NIH-funded RCT (2017–2020). Brand created. Certification launched, with the empoweredrelief.com site live by mid-2019. Darnall spoke on the psychology of pain relief at the World Economic Forum in Davos in 2018 (her Stanford profile).
- **2020–2021.** COVID drove the shift to Zoom delivery and the Stanford site launched (2020). The **Aug 2021 JAMA Netw Open** paper was the main media spike: NIH press release (16 Aug), HealthDay, MedPage, a NYT Well article (9 Nov 2021, which put the program at about 300 instructors), Harvard Health (1 Dec 2021) and a PBS documentary (*Pain Secrets*, Nov 2021).
- **2022–2024.** Health-system rollouts (Cleveland Clinic, AHN, LVHN). Coverage in the WSJ (1 Oct 2022, on brief therapies; the program's news page misdates it 2023), Men's Health (11 Apr 2022, which mentions Darnall's class but not by name) and Stanford Medicine Magazine. 6-month results in 2024, with an NCCIH research spotlight.
- **2025–2026.** Publication rate peaked at ~15+ PubMed records in 2025–26, with large PCORI and DoD trials and expansion to nurses, PTs, youth and rare disease. Site reports 10,000+ patients, 1,000–1,500+ instructors and 25–30 countries.
- **Consumer visibility is low.** It is reached through referral, not marketed directly to patients. There is no dedicated subreddit or Wikipedia article. Reddit's search and JSON endpoints and Google Trends returned 403/429 to automated requests (checked 2026-10-13), so forum mention counts and search-interest trends were not measured.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms

- No substantive public controversy.
- Main academic critiques:
  - All evidence comes from the developer's group.
  - Stanford has an institutional financial interest through certification revenue. Darnall has disclosed other industry ties (AppliedVR, axial Healthcare).
  - Trials are unblinded and rely on self-report.
  - Pain catastrophizing as primary outcome is "less directly important for most patients and clinicians" (the authors' own limitation).
  - It was inferior to CBT on physical function.
  - The opioid-reduction trial was null.
- A 2024 *Pain Reports* commentary (Aaron & Ravyts, Johns Hopkins, PMID 38586594) was largely favourable, calling ER a scalable option suited to stepped care. It noted that cost-effectiveness has not been measured, that fixed group content may not suit patients with complex pain or comorbid depression, and that cultural and socioeconomic inclusivity needs work.
- Some clinicians worry that offering a single session could become a cheap substitute for fuller multidisciplinary care. The program frames itself as a first step in stepped care.

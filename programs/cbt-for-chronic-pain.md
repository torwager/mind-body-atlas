## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Biopsychosocial model (Engel 1977) plus the gate control and neuromatrix theories (Melzack & Wall 1965; Melzack 1999).** Pain is shaped jointly by tissue and nervous-system factors, cognition, emotion, behaviour and social context. The VA manual teaches the gate control model to patients as the rationale that thoughts, mood and activity can "open or close the gate."
- **The chronic pain cycle.** Pain leads to inactivity and avoidance, then deconditioning, then reduced valued activity, then depression, irritability and poor sleep, which increase pain and disability. CBT targets the links in this loop.
- **Cognitive appraisal.** Catastrophizing, helplessness and low self-efficacy amplify suffering and disability (the "situation, thought, emotion, behaviour" triangle, adapted from Beck's cognitive model). Pain catastrophizing is the signature mediator and outcome.
- **Operant learning (Fordyce).** Pain behaviours are maintained by reinforcement (rest, attention, medication, avoidance of duties). Well behaviours are reinforced instead.
- **Fear-avoidance and arousal.** Muscle tension and stress arousal are reduced through relaxation.
- **Nociception is not denied.** Pain is treated as real and usually "managed rather than cured." The goal is function and quality of life rather than elimination of a central "danger signal." This is the key contrast with neuroplastic programs such as PRT, EAET and Sarno.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
Mostly one transdiagnostic framework. The same biopsychosocial and CBT model and the same skill set are applied across pain diagnoses. The manual reviews the medical features of specific conditions (low back pain, OA, FM, headache types, neuropathic pain) but does not posit different psychological mechanisms for each. Adaptations are mostly in emphasis and format: CBT-HA for headache (trigger management, relaxation/biofeedback); disease-specific variants in the literature for FM, IBS (gut-directed CBT is a separate tradition) and TMD. The model does **not** claim that pain is brain-generated or reversible in all cases, nor that ME/CFS, MCS and similar conditions share one cause. CBT for ME/CFS (e.g., PACE) is a distinct protocol not covered by CBT-CP.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
**VA CBT-CP protocol** (Murphy et al., 2014; a Veteran workbook/guidebook also exists; later manual revisions not independently verified): 12 weekly individual sessions (11 weekly sessions plus a booster follow-up), with homework between sessions. A group version also exists.
1. Interview and assessment (pain history, measures: pain intensity, interference, catastrophizing, PHQ-9, WHOQOL-BREF).
2. Treatment orientation: the biopsychosocial model, the pain cycle, gate control.
3. Assessment feedback and SMART goal setting, tied to valued activities.
4. Exercise and pacing: graded walking plans with logs; time-based activity/rest cycles (e.g., 10 min active : 15 min rest); breaking tasks into chunks; avoiding boom–bust.
5. Relaxation training: diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery (audio for daily practice).
6–7. Pleasant activities scheduling (behavioural activation).
8–9. Cognitive coping: identifying automatic thoughts and catastrophizing; challenging and restructuring them; coping statements.
10. Sleep: sleep hygiene, stimulus control (get out of bed if not asleep in 20–30 min), elements of CBT-I.
11. Discharge planning: maintenance plan, flare-up plan, anticipating obstacles.
12. Booster session about 1 month later.

**Other formats**
- **Brief CBT-CP** (Beehler et al.): 6 × 30-min sessions in primary-care mental-health integration.
- **Group CBT** (UK BeST model): about 6 sessions.
- **Interactive voice response / AI-coached self-directed CBT-CP** (COPES): daily automated phone check-ins plus weekly personalized coach feedback over about 11 weeks.
- **Commercial digital CBT for pain** (generic, not the VA brand) exists in many apps.

## 4. Roots and influences
- Behaviorism and operant conditioning (Fordyce, *Behavioral Methods for Chronic Pain and Illness*, 1976).
- Beck's cognitive therapy and Ellis's REBT (1960s–70s); Meichenbaum's stress inoculation and self-instruction.
- Gate control and neuromatrix pain science (Melzack & Wall 1965; Melzack 1999); Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977).
- Jacobson's progressive relaxation, the Relaxation Response, and biofeedback (relaxation and headache components); Selye's stress theory (cited in the manual's neuromatrix section).
- Multidisciplinary pain clinics (Bonica, 1960s–70s).
- Later "third-wave" offshoots: ACT for chronic pain (McCracken), mindfulness-based approaches, and CBT-I for comorbid insomnia.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Guidelines.**
  - US: American College of Physicians low back pain guideline (2017) recommends CBT among first-line nonpharmacologic options.
  - US: CDC opioid prescribing guidelines (2016, 2022) and the HHS Pain Management Best Practices Inter-Agency Task Force (2019) endorse it.
  - US: VA/DoD clinical practice guidelines (LBP, opioid therapy, headache) recommend it.
  - UK: NICE NG193 (2021) recommends CBT and ACT for chronic primary pain (alongside exercise and acupuncture). NICE NG59 (low back pain) recommends psychological therapy using a CBT approach within combined physical-psychological programmes.
- **VHA.** CBT-CP is one of VA's national evidence-based psychotherapy rollouts (with CPT/PE for PTSD, CBT-I, CBT-D, ACT). It uses competency-based training: a workshop followed by about 6 months of weekly consultation on training cases. It is embedded in VA's Stepped Care Model for Pain Management and Whole Health. Brief CBT-CP was built for primary care.
- **Insurance (US).** Since 2020, CMS Health Behavior Assessment and Intervention codes (96156 assessment; 96158/96159 individual intervention per 30/15 min; 96164–96168 group/family) allow billing for CBT for a physical-health diagnosis such as chronic pain, without a psychiatric diagnosis. Psychotherapy codes apply when there is comorbid depression or anxiety. Coverage is broad; the barriers are access and workforce shortage, not reimbursement.

## 6. Evidence base
**Systematic reviews / meta-analyses**
- **Williams ACdeC, Fisher E, Hearn L, Eccleston C.** Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020;8:CD007407 (PMID 32794606). 75 RCTs, 9,401 participants overall; 59 trials of CBT (>5,000 participants). At end of treatment:
  - vs active control: pain SMD −0.09, disability −0.12, distress −0.09 (moderate certainty).
  - vs treatment as usual: pain −0.22, disability −0.32, distress −0.34.
  - Benefits were largely maintained at 6–12 months vs usual care but not vs active controls.
  - Authors' verdict: small effects, and the evidence does not favour CBT over other active treatments.
- **Morley S, Eccleston C, Williams A.** Pain 1999;80:1–13 (PMID 10204712). 25 trials. Median effect size about 0.5 vs waiting list. Against active treatments, CBT was superior only on pain experience, coping and pain behaviour.
- **Bernardy K et al.** Fibromyalgia: Cochrane 2013 (CD009796; 23 RCTs, n=2,031; pain SMD −0.29 at end of treatment, −0.40 at follow-up) and Eur J Pain 2018;22:242–260 (PMID 28984402; 29 RCTs, n=2,509; small effects on pain, mood, disability and fatigue).

**Key RCTs (generic CBT for pain)**
- **Lamb SE et al.** (BeST), Lancet 2010;375:916–923 (PMID 20189241). n=701 subacute/chronic LBP in UK primary care; group CBT (≤6 sessions) plus advice vs advice alone (2:1 randomization). Roland-Morris improved by 1.3 points more at 12 months. Cost-effective (about £1,786/QALY).
- **Cherkin DC et al.**, JAMA 2016;315:1240–1249 (PMID 27002445). n=342 chronic LBP; MBSR vs CBT vs usual care. Clinically meaningful function improvement at 26 weeks: 57.7% CBT, 60.5% MBSR, 44.1% usual care. CBT and MBSR did not differ.
- **Darnall BD et al.**, JAMA Netw Open 2021;4:e2113401 (PMID 34398206). n=263 chronic LBP. A single 2-hour class (Empowered Relief, developed by the lead author) was noninferior to 8-session CBT on pain catastrophizing, pain intensity and interference at 3 months, but inferior on physical function. Both beat health education on catastrophizing.

**Trials and evaluations of the VA CBT-CP protocol specifically**
- **Stewart MO, Karlin BE, Murphy JL et al.**, Clin J Pain 2015;31:722–729 (PMID 25171637). Uncontrolled program evaluation: 71 therapists (85% met competency); 148 veterans (79% completed). Significant pre–post gains in catastrophizing, interference and QoL.
- **Murphy JL, Cordova MJ, Dedert EA.** Psychol Serv 2022;19:95–102 (PMID 32986454). Uncontrolled: 1,331 veterans treated by trainees 2012–2018. Catastrophizing d=0.78; worst pain, interference, depression and physical QoL d>0.60.
- **Beehler GP et al.**
  - Clin J Pain 2019;35:809–817 (PMID 31318726). Brief CBT-CP demonstration, n=118, uncontrolled; composite pain/function d=0.65 by the third visit.
  - Pain 2026;167:1839–1849 (PMID 42263268). RCT, n=184 primary-care veterans. Brief CBT-CP (6 × 30 min) plus usual care beat usual care on pain interference at 6, 12 and 24 weeks.
- **Heapy AA et al.**
  - COPES, JAMA Intern Med 2017;177:765–773 (PMID 28384682). Noninferiority RCT, n=125 chronic back pain. IVR-delivered CBT was noninferior to in-person CBT on pain intensity, with more sessions completed.
  - COPES-ExTRA, JAMA 2026;336:400–412 (PMID 42340733). Pragmatic RCT, n=764 veterans at 9 VHA sites. Open-label. Self-directed coached CBT-CP beat clinician-delivered CBT-CP (4–11 sessions under usual practice conditions) on 4-month pain interference (5.26 vs 6.23; diff −0.98, about the MCID of 1), sustained to 12 months. Follow-up completion 76% at 4 months, 68% at 12 months.
- **Piette JD et al.**, JAMA Intern Med 2022;182:975–983 (PMID 35939288). n=278 VA chronic back pain. AI-personalized CBT-CP was noninferior to standard telephone CBT-CP, using less than half the therapist time.

**Head-to-head comparisons with "neuroplastic/emotional" therapies (CBT as comparator)**
- **Yarns BC et al.**, JAMA Netw Open 2024;7:e2415842 (PMID 38869899). n=126 older veterans. EAET beat CBT: ≥30% pain reduction in 63% vs 17% post-treatment; also better anxiety, depression and PTSD outcomes. Earlier pilot: Pain Med 2020 (PMID 32451528), n=53.
- **Lumley MA et al.**, Pain 2017;158:2354–2363 (PMID 28796118). Cluster-randomized, fibromyalgia, n=230 (40 groups). EAET did not differ from CBT on the primary outcome (pain severity at 6 months) or most secondary outcomes, but beat CBT on FM symptoms (d=0.35), widespread pain (d=0.37) and ≥50% pain reduction (22.5% vs 8.3%).

**Gaps and critiques.** Effects on pain intensity are small. Many trials have high or unclear risk of bias and lack blinding. Active controls (education, MBSR, single-session classes) often do about as well. Results are heterogeneous across protocols, so "CBT" is not one intervention. VA program evaluations are uncontrolled and run by program developers. The 2026 pragmatic trials are independent of any commercial interest; investigators are VA-funded developers.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1976–1983.** Fordyce's operant program (1976) and Turk, Meichenbaum & Genest (1983) define the field. RCTs accumulate through the 1980s–90s, leading to Morley 1999.
- **2000s.** Becomes standard in multidisciplinary pain programmes (UK "pain management programmes," US interdisciplinary clinics, German multimodal therapy). Cochrane reviews in 2009, 2012 and 2020.
- **2011–2014.** VA national CBT-CP training launched (therapist evaluation published 2015; manual 2014). Opioid-crisis policy (CDC 2016; CARA 2016; HHS Task Force 2019) sharply raises demand for non-drug pain care. This is the peak period of policy attention.
- **2020–2026.** Telehealth expansion during COVID-19; new HBAI billing codes (2020). Digital, AI and IVR versions; large pragmatic VA trials (COPES-ExTRA 2026). Also challenged by "pain reprocessing" approaches (PRT 2021 JAMA Psychiatry; EAET 2024), which position CBT-CP as the conventional comparator.
- **Scale.** In a national VHA analysis, 37,514 patients with pain were seen by CBT-CP-trained therapists, about 38% of whom received CBT-CP. VA cohorts include 1,331 (2012–18) program-evaluation patients plus thousands in trials.
- **Online visibility.** Generic CBT-for-pain is widely discussed on Reddit r/ChronicPain, often ambivalently ("useful coping, didn't reduce pain," "felt told it's in my head"). Google interest in "CBT for chronic pain" has been steady rather than viral. Not systematically measured here.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Small effects.** The effect on pain is small, and some argue it is not clearly specific (it is roughly equal to active controls).
- **Coping, not cure.** CBT-CP aims at coping and function, not cure. Critics from the neuroplastic-pain camp (PRT, EAET, Sarno) argue that it accepts pain as chronic and under-targets fear-of-pain and emotional drivers. Patients sometimes experience referral to psychology as being told the pain is "psychological."
- **Spillover from CBT for ME/CFS.** The PACE trial controversy and NICE 2021's withdrawal of CBT as a curative ME/CFS treatment affected perceptions of CBT for "contested" illnesses, though CBT-CP itself makes no curative claims.
- **Access.** Workforce shortages limit access: in the VA analysis, only 38.4% of pain patients seen by CBT-CP-trained therapists received CBT-CP (Oct 2015–Feb 2020).

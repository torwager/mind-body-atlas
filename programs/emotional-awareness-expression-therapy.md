## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)

**The brain generates or amplifies symptoms.** Pain and other somatic symptoms are produced or amplified by the brain, not by peripheral tissue damage. Peripheral contributors (muscle tension, autonomic changes, inflammation) are acknowledged but framed as largely brain-controlled. These neural pathways are tightly linked to emotion and cognition.

**Life adversity sensitizes a danger/alarm system.** Trauma, abuse, neglect, and interpersonal or intrapsychic conflict sensitize this system. Schubiner describes a "hypersensitized danger/alarm mechanism" that later stressors resembling the original ones trigger more easily, for example a controlling boss echoing a controlling parent. Once triggered, it produces "neural-circuit-induced" pain, fatigue, anxiety, depression and insomnia.

**Emotional avoidance is the key maintaining process.** The model calls this "affect phobia" or experiential avoidance. Unexpressed or "held-in" primary emotions, especially anger/rage, and also guilt, grief, and longing for connection, keep the alarm system active. The model names two core avoided needs:
- **Agency/assertion**, powered by anger and pride.
- **Communion/attachment**, powered by sadness, love and healthy guilt.

**How change is supposed to happen.** Symptoms are expected to fall through four routes:
- Corrective emotional experiences.
- Exposure to avoided emotions.
- Re-attribution of pain to brain/emotional causes, which reduces fear.
- "Memory reconsolidation": creating new emotional memories, such as imagined empowered responses to past abusers, that overlay and weaken the old ones.

Two maladaptive cognitions are also targeted: probability overestimation and catastrophizing about expressing emotion.

**Relation to other neuroplastic-pain models.** The model overlaps with PRT and Sarno-style neuroplastic/"mind-body syndrome" models. The difference is that EAET puts **trauma/conflict-linked emotional processing** at the center, where PRT centers on safety reappraisal of the sensation itself.

## 2. One mechanism or many? (does it differentiate between named illnesses)

**Mostly unified, with condition-specific emphasis.** One mechanism, a brain-generated alarm sensitized by adversity and maintained by emotional avoidance, is applied across fibromyalgia, IBS, pelvic pain, MUS/SSD, musculoskeletal pain and headache. The developers explicitly frame it as a treatment for "primary or centralized" pain. The manual is written for "fibromyalgia and related centralized chronic pain disorders."

**Where it does differentiate:**
- **Patient selection, not etiology.** The approach is said to suit patients whose pain is linked to trauma, adversity, conflict or relationship problems. Trials show larger benefits with higher baseline depression, anxiety and PTSD (Yarns 2024). In fibromyalgia, low pressure-pain tolerance predicted success (Bellomo 2020).
- **Structural disease.** Structural or peripheral disease (e.g., inflammatory arthritis, neuropathy) is meant to be excluded or treated medically.
- **Condition-specific adaptations.** These change the format, not the mechanism. Examples are the headache adaptation "Creating Calm" (Toronto) and a version for post-traumatic orthopaedic pain.
- **No published framework** for ME/CFS, POTS, MCS, long COVID or EHS.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies (concrete; components, structure, duration, dose)

### Canonical group protocol
Source: the 2012 manual used in the fibromyalgia RCT. An initial individual session is often added (VA trials). The format is 8 weekly 90-min sessions in groups of 5–7, led by a psychologist.

| Session | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | Rationale; model of stress, emotion and pain; finding stress–symptom links in one's own life |
| 2 | Triangle model of stress and symptoms; experiencing and expressing anger and closeness |
| 3 | Avoidance in daily life; maladaptive core beliefs; conflicted relationships; experiencing, expressing and releasing (1) |
| 4 | Self-compassion and self-forgiveness to reverse self-blame and guilt; experiencing, expressing and releasing (2) |
| 5 | Forgiving others or letting go; experiencing, expressing and releasing (3) |
| 6 | Shame, intimacy, secrets |
| 7 | Healthy communication in relationships |
| 8 | "Becoming a new person"; future planning |

Each session follows the same pattern:
- review of the past week's homework;
- didactics;
- in-vivo experiential exercises;
- a brief written exercise;
- new home activities: expressive writing, engaging in avoided behaviors (especially in relationships), and a guided visualization recording.

### Core techniques
- **Pain-attribution education.** "Your brain, not your tissues, generates the pain," supported by live demonstrations that emotions can provoke symptoms.
- **Life-stress interview.** A trauma and conflict history that maps stressors onto the timeline of symptom onset.
- **Experiential emotional exposure in session.** Patients address an imagined offender (empty-chair work), retell the event in the present tense with eyes closed, and amplify expression through voice, face, posture and fists. The target is the "right emotion at the right target."
- **Fixed emotional sequence.** Anger/rage comes first, including imagined protective or retaliatory action in fantasy. Then guilt, then sadness/grief/longing, then self-compassion and letting go. Old memories are rescripted ("going back to change it, not relive it"). The current self comforts the younger self.
- **Unsent letters, read aloud.** Also expressive writing at home.
- **Assertive communication in real relationships.** Role-played in session, then tried at home, balancing assertion with connection, and setting boundaries.
- **Experiential challenge of beliefs.** Disconfirming "it will turn out badly" and "I can't handle it."
- **Borrowed techniques.** Schubiner borrows Internal Family Systems "parts" language to work with ambivalence.

### Other formats and dose
- **Three 50-min individual sessions** (IBS trial).
- **One 90-min interview** (MUS, pelvic pain, childhood-adversity trials).
- **2-hr single telehealth class** (Stanford, uncontrolled).
- **10 internet modules over 10 weeks**, guided or unguided (I-EAET, Karolinska/Stockholm).

### Self-care
- Schubiner's workbook *Unlearn Your Pain* (4th ed.) and *Unlearn Your Anxiety and Depression* give daily writing and meditation-style exercises in the same spirit.
- Clinicians often pair it with PRT-style somatic tracking.

## 4. Roots and influences (prior practices/traditions it draws on)

The 2012 manual lists its theoretical sources explicitly:
- **Experiential avoidance**, from Hayes and ACT.
- **The corrective emotional experience**, from Alexander and French.
- **Malan's triangles of conflict and person**, from psychodynamic theory.
- **Blatt's agency/relatedness** personality dimensions.
- **McCullough's "affect phobia"** and short-term dynamic therapy.
- **Davanloo/Abbass ISTDP**: the anger–guilt–grief sequence and attention to the muscle/somatic pathway of anxiety. Schubiner has called EAET "a simpler version of ISTDP" (interview, istdpsweden.se, 2020).
- **Barlow's Unified Protocol** and exposure-based CBT for anxiety.
- **Greenberg's emotion-focused therapy**, including empty-chair work.

Other lineage:
- **Pennebaker written emotional disclosure.** Lumley's earlier RCTs, e.g., D'Souza 2008 in headache, were disclosure trials.
- **Sarno's tension myositis syndrome / repressed-rage model.** Schubiner's "mind-body syndrome" work descends from Sarno.
- **Neuroplastic/central-sensitization pain science**, including Clauw and Williams at the University of Michigan, who co-authored the fibromyalgia trial.
- **Memory-reconsolidation theory**, as used by Ecker.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance

### Guidelines and federal recognition
- The **2019 HHS Pain Management Best Practices Inter-Agency Task Force report** lists EAET as a behavioral treatment option. This was verified in the report itself (section 2.5, p. 38, alongside CBT, ACT and MBSR). The report says EAET improves pain intensity, interference and depressive symptoms.
- EAET is not in NICE guidance. It does not appear in the VA/DoD chronic pain CPGs as a named treatment; CBT and ACT are the VA's disseminated therapies.
- It is discussed in major reviews, e.g., Driscoll et al., *Psychol Sci Public Interest* 2021.

### Funding and health-system use
- **Funding:**
  - NIAMS R01 for the fibromyalgia trial;
  - VA funding for the Yarns trials;
  - NIH funding for the 4-arm M&M trial (CBT vs ACT vs EAET vs control; lead site Rush University, PI John Burns, with Wayne State; NCT06044649; started Nov 2023; 408 enrolled; active, not recruiting). It is funded by NINR (R01NR020610, per NIH RePORTER);
  - NCCIH funding for the veterans pragmatic trial (UCLA lead; NCT07218757; recruiting since Jan 2026; estimated N=672).
- **VA trials:** VA Greater LA has delivered EAET clinically and by video telehealth. A 3-arm VA trial (EAET vs mindfulness vs CBT by home video, NCT06004804, target N=216, 2023–2027) is ongoing.
- **Other academic sites:** Ann Arbor/Michigan (EAET for pain with OUD, NCT06865560; completed pilot, N=11); Stanford (Ziadni); Karolinska (Maroti); Toronto (headache); Utah (migraine).

### Insurance
- EAET is reimbursed as psychotherapy when a licensed clinician delivers it. Typical US codes are individual 90832/90834/90837, group 90853, and health-behavior intervention 96158/96164.
- There is no EAET-specific code or coverage policy.
- Out-of-network "mind-body" therapists who advertise EAET commonly charge cash fees.

### Training
- Lumley and Schubiner run the training through unlearnyourpain.com. It is open only to licensed or student clinicians; coaches are excluded.
- EAET basics are also taught in PRT-ecosystem trainings, e.g., Freedom From Chronic Pain practitioner training and *This Might Hurt* listings.

## 6. Evidence base (list peer-reviewed trials with citation, design, N, key result; note gaps, independence of investigators, critiques)

All trials below were verified on PubMed. Lumley and/or Schubiner co-authored nearly all of them.

### Randomized trials of EAET (branded protocol or close variants)

| Citation | Design / N | Comparator | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lumley, Schubiner et al., *Pain* 2017;158:2354 (PMID 28796118) | Cluster RCT, 40 groups, **N=230** fibromyalgia; 8 × 90 min | CBT; FM education | **Primary (pain severity, 6 mo): EAET = education.** EAET > education on overall symptoms, widespread pain, physical function, cognition, anxiety, depression, positive affect, life satisfaction (d 0.29–0.45); "much/very much improved" 34.8% vs 15.4%. Vs CBT: no difference on most outcomes, but lower FM symptoms (d 0.35) and widespread pain (d 0.37); ≥50% pain reduction 22.5% vs 8.3%. |
| Yarns et al., *JAMA Netw Open* 2024;7:e2415842 (PMID 38869899; NCT03918642) | RCT, **N=126** older veterans (60–95 y, 92% male) with musculoskeletal pain; 1 individual + 8 group sessions | CBT (same format) | EAET > CBT on pain severity post-treatment (−1.59 BPI points) and at 6 mo (−1.01). ≥30% reduction 63% vs 17%; ≥50% 35% vs 7%. Also better on anxiety, depression, PTSD, life satisfaction, PGIC. Higher baseline depression, anxiety and PTSD predicted larger EAET effects. |
| Yarns et al., *Pain Med* 2020;21:2811 (PMID 32451528) | Preliminary RCT, **N=53** older veterans | CBT | EAET produced lower pain severity than CBT post-treatment and at 3 mo (partial η² 0.13–0.16). 41.7% vs one CBT patient had ≥30% reduction. |
| Thakur et al., *Neurogastroenterol Motil* 2017;29:e13143 (PMID 28643436; NCT01886027) | RCT, **N=106** IBS; 3 × 50-min individual | Relaxation training; waitlist | At 10 wk, EAET (but not relaxation) reduced IBS symptom severity vs waitlist. Both improved QoL. Relaxation reduced depression; EAET did not. |
| Ziadni et al., *Health Psychol* 2018;37:282 (PMID 29154608) | RCT, **N=75** primary-care MUS; single 90-min interview | TAU | Lower pain severity, interference, sleep problems and global psychological symptoms at 6 wk. |
| Carty et al., *Pain Med* 2019;20:1321 (PMID 30252113) | RCT, **N=62** women with chronic urogenital pain; single interview | TAU | Lower pain severity and pelvic-floor symptoms at 6 wk. No effect on interference, anxiety or depression. |
| Maroti et al., *J Psychosom Res* 2022;163:111068 (PMID 36327532; NCT04751825) | RCT, **N=74** somatic symptom disorder; 10-wk internet I-EAET | Waitlist | Somatic symptoms reduced at post and 4-mo follow-up. Pain, depression, anxiety and insomnia gains not retained. About 20% had substantial improvement. Partly mediated by emotional processing. |
| Krohner et al., *J Pain* 2024;25:39 (PMID 37479050) | RCT, **N=91** chronic musculoskeletal pain + ≥3 ACEs; single 90-min video interview (EAET/ISTDP-based) | Waitlist | **No difference in pain severity.** Pain interference reduced (ηp² 0.05). Pain attributions and controllability shifted. |
| Maroti et al., *Internet Interv* 2026;44:100942 (PMID 41969788) | RCT, **N=154** persistent physical symptoms | Guided vs unguided I-EAET | Both improved. No significant group × time difference; responders 47% vs 30%, favouring guidance. |
| Kaufmann, Lumley et al., *Pain Med* 2026;27:948 (PMID 41761583; NCT05837650) | Preliminary RCT, **N=30** episodic/chronic migraine (87% women); 8 online sessions | Waitlist | Monthly migraine days 16.4→9.4 (−7.0) vs 15.0→13.3 (−1.7); condition × time ηp² 0.23, p=.008. ≥30% responders 80% vs 36%; ≥50% 40% vs 7%. Also better on QoL, anxiety, depression, allodynia, affect. No adverse events. Utah-led, but Lumley and Yarns co-author. |

### Precursors and related randomized trials (earlier variants, not the branded protocol)
- **Hsu, Schubiner, Lumley et al.**, *J Gen Intern Med* 2010 (PMID 20532650). "Affective self-awareness" in fibromyalgia, N=45 vs waitlist. At 6 mo, 45.8% vs 0% had ≥30% pain reduction.
- **Slavin-Spenny et al.**, *Ann Behav Med* 2013 (PMID 23620190). Anger awareness and expression training for headache, N=147. Equal to relaxation training; both beat waitlist.

### Uncontrolled studies
- **Burger et al.**, *J Psychosom Res* 2016 (PMID 26800632). N=72 musculoskeletal pain. About 2/3 had ≥30% pain reduction and 1/3 ≥70%, sustained at 6 mo.
- **Maroti et al.** 2021 (PMID 33679478). I-EAET pilot.
- **Ziadni et al.** 2022 (PMID 36466215). Single 2-hr telehealth class.
- **Yarns et al.** 2024 (PMID 36541672). Video-telehealth pilot.
- **Zamir et al.** 2026 (PMID 41742651). "Creating Calm" headache feasibility study.

### Ongoing trials
- **NCT06044649.** 4-arm M&M trial (CBT/ACT/EAET/control) for chronic pain. 408 enrolled; active, not recruiting; protocol in Burns et al., 2025. Lead site is Rush (PI Burns), so this is the first large trial led partly by a non-EAET-developer PI.
- **NCT06004804.** VA trial of EAET vs mindfulness vs CBT by video.
- **NCT07218757.** Pragmatic trial in veterans: UCLA lead, NCCIH, multisite, estimated N=672, recruiting since Jan 2026.
- **NCT06865560.** EAET for pain with opioid use disorder (Michigan; completed single-arm pilot, N=11; results not yet published).

### Gaps and critiques
- **Developer allegiance and independence.** Nearly every trial includes Lumley and/or Schubiner, and there is no independent replication. Lumley reports fees for EAET training (JAMA 2024 COI). Schubiner owns Mind Body Publishing.
- **Primary-outcome failures.** The largest trial missed its primary outcome against education (fibromyalgia 2017). The childhood-adversity interview RCT missed pain severity.
- **Weak controls.** Many trials use waitlist or TAU controls. Patients and therapists cannot be blinded; the fibromyalgia trial scored 8/10 on PEDro, with no subject or therapist blinding.
- **Psychological outcomes are inconsistent.** IBS, pelvic pain and I-EAET follow-up showed no benefit or decay on anxiety and depression.
- **The 17% CBT response rate in Yarns 2024 is low.** This raises the question of whether the CBT comparator was suboptimal for that population.
- **Samples are small to moderate.** The largest RCT has N=230, and follow-up is mostly ≤6 months.
- **No trials in** ME/CFS, long COVID, POTS, MCS, CRPS or FND.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms (if any)

- **Methodological limits** are those in Section 6: allegiance, unblinded designs, weak controls, a null primary outcome in the largest trial, and modest follow-up.
- **The intensity of anger work raises safety and acceptability questions.** The practitioner guide endorses imagined violent retaliation against abusers (hitting, using weapons, exposing or jailing them) as a corrective experience. Some clinicians and patients may find this distressing or retraumatizing. The developers report rare adverse events and high retention.
- **Psychogenic framing.** Like other "mind-body"/neuroplastic pain models, EAET risks being read as "it's all psychological." That risk is especially high if it is extended beyond primary pain, for example to ME/CFS or long COVID, where patient communities strongly reject psychogenic explanations. Its developers frame it for primary/centralized pain and stress that pain is real and blameless.
- **Unproven mechanism claims.** "Memory reconsolidation" and "danger/alarm" circuit claims are theoretical. No neuroimaging or mechanistic trial shows that EAET changes these circuits. Mediation data are limited to self-reported emotional processing and attributions.
- **The JAMA Netw Open press framing** as a possible "new gold standard" exceeds what one 126-person trial supports.

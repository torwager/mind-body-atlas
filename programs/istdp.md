## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Core model (Davanloo, Malan's triangles).** Painful attachment experiences early in life generate complex mixed feelings toward loved ones: love, grief, and especially *rage and guilt about the rage*. These feelings are pushed out of awareness. When current events or closeness re-evoke them, they trigger **unconscious anxiety**, and the person manages that anxiety with **defenses** (avoidance, detachment, self-attack, denial, somatisation). Symptoms are the combined product of anxiety and defenses ("triangle of conflict"), repeated across past, current and therapist relationships ("triangle of person").
- **Somatic pathway model (key for MUS/SSD/FND).** Unconscious anxiety is discharged through identifiable bodily channels, and each channel maps onto a class of symptom:
  - **Striated (voluntary) muscle tension**: hand clenching, sighing respiration, neck, back, jaw and chest-wall pain, tension headache, fibromyalgia-like pain.
  - **Smooth muscle / autonomic**: IBS, nausea, bladder urgency, migraine, asthma-like symptoms, hypertension spikes.
  - **Cognitive-perceptual disruption**: blurred vision, dizziness, dissociation, memory "blanking", tinnitus-like experiences.
  - **Motor conversion**: weakness, paralysis, non-epileptic or functional seizures.
- **Repressed rage and self-directed aggression.** Guilt-laden, unprocessed rage is redirected against the self as depression, self-sabotage or physical symptoms. This is conceptually close to Sarno's TMS but grounded in clinical observation of videotaped sessions.
- **Neurobiological gloss (Abbass, Schubiner).** These are "psychophysiologic disorders" (PPD): brain-generated symptoms driven by emotional threat processing, with "danger signals" amplified centrally. Abbass's later writing links this to central sensitisation and fear/threat circuitry, but the core explanation is still psychodynamic.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Mostly unified, with differentiation by symptom pathway.** A single cause (avoided complex feelings → unconscious anxiety + defenses) is said to underlie depression, anxiety, personality problems and somatic symptoms. Unlike most mind-body programs, though, ISTDP sorts *how* anxiety is expressed, and the channel determines technique:
- **Striated-muscle discharge** (most back/neck pain, tension headache): considered safe for high-intensity pressure toward feelings. Symptoms often drop within the session.
- **Smooth-muscle discharge** (IBS, migraine) and **cognitive-perceptual disruption** (dissociation, visual symptoms): the patient is "fragile" at that anxiety level. The therapist must first regulate anxiety with the **graded format**, intellectualising and pairing body cues with feelings before raising emotional intensity again.
- **Conversion** (FND, functional weakness): anxiety is fully repressed. The therapist works to mobilise anxiety *into* striated muscle (a sign of progress) before accessing feelings.

Symptoms are therefore read as process markers ("where is the anxiety going right now?") rather than as separate diseases. Diagnosis-specific mechanisms such as ME/CFS-specific immune models are not addressed. Applications to ME/CFS, long COVID, MCS or EHS are not part of the published research program.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
- **Trial therapy / "direct diagnosis" (2–3 h first session).** The therapist interviews about specific events and *notices* in real time how anxiety shows in the body (e.g., hand clenching versus GI cramping versus blurred vision). The aim is to show patients that symptoms are linked to emotions. In the ED, this single interview is both diagnostic and therapeutic (Abbass et al. 2009: mean 3.8 sessions).
- **Pressure.** Repeated invitations to identify and *experience* the specific feeling (e.g., anger toward someone) in the body, rather than talk about it.
- **Clarification and challenge of defenses.** The therapist names defenses (vagueness, distancing, smiling, self-criticism), notes their cost, and invites the patient to set them aside. At high resistance this becomes the **"head-on collision"**, a direct statement of how the defense defeats the patient's goals.
- **Graded format** for fragile patients. Cycles of building anxiety tolerance: recapping, intellectualising the link between feeling, anxiety and defense, then gradually raising intensity again.
- **"Unlocking the unconscious" / breakthrough.** Somatic experience of rage (often as a portrayed impulse), then guilt and grief toward attachment figures. Physical symptoms are expected to fall as these feelings are tolerated.
- **Consolidation.** Linking the dynamic to current relationships and symptoms (triangle of person). Recording sessions on video for supervision is standard.
- **Dose.** ED service: 1–5 sessions. Primary care / MUS services: mean ~6–8 sessions (Abbass 2015: mean 7.3). Treatment-resistant depression trial: about 20 weekly sessions. Severe personality disorder: up to ~40.
- **Self-care.** Minimal formal homework. Patients are taught to notice anxiety signs (cold or clammy hands, sighing, GI changes) and the feelings they precede. Popular derivatives (Schubiner's *Unlearn Your Pain*, Frederickson's *The Lies We Tell Ourselves*, 2017) provide self-help versions.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Freudian psychoanalysis**: catharsis (Breuer and Freud), unconscious conflict, defense mechanisms, resistance and transference.
- **David Malan (Tavistock)**: brief dynamic psychotherapy, triangle of conflict and triangle of person.
- **Franz Alexander**: "corrective emotional experience"; Chicago psychosomatic school.
- **Sifneos and Mann**: short-term dynamic psychotherapy in the 1960s–70s.
- **Bowlby attachment theory**: adopted by later ISTDP authors (Davanloo himself downplayed it).
- **Use of video and empirical observation**: a 1960s–70s innovation.
- Downstream, ISTDP has fed into **AEDP** (Diana Fosha), **EAET** (Lumley & Schubiner borrow emotional expression techniques), Schubiner's PPD/mind-body syndrome model, and PRT/Curable-style "emotional" components.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Halifax, Nova Scotia**: ED psychotherapy consultation service (from 2002), a primary-care MUS service (Cooper, Abbass & Town 2017, *J Clin Med*), and a tertiary psychotherapy service. Its cost-offset data (Abbass 2015) are the main argument for integration.
- **Academic training**: McGill (historically) and Dalhousie; core trainings in many countries (Washington School of Psychiatry; Norway, Sweden, Netherlands, Italy, Poland, UK, Australia). Norway runs ISTDP-based residential programs (Solbakken & Abbass 2015).
- **FND services**: UK clinicians (Russell, Abbass, Allder 2022; Malda-Castillo et al. 2023) report brief ISTDP within functional-seizure care.
- **Guidelines**: Not named specifically by NICE or the APA. Short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy as a class received a "strong" recommendation for somatic symptom disorders and depression in a GRADE umbrella review (Leichsenring et al. 2023, *World Psychiatry*), whose co-authors include Abbass.
- **Insurance**: Reimbursed as standard psychotherapy wherever licensed clinicians bill psychotherapy codes. Many specialised private ISTDP therapists in the US are out-of-network.

**Meta-analyses and reviews (somatic)**
- Abbass, Kisely, Kroenke 2009, *Psychother Psychosom* 78:265 (PMID 19602915). STPP for somatic disorders (23 studies): significant effects on physical and psychiatric symptoms and social adjustment.
- Abbass, Town, Driessen 2012, *Harv Rev Psychiatry* 20:97 (PMID 22512743). 21 ISTDP studies (10 controlled) across conditions: d = 1.18 vs controls (general psychopathology).
- Koelen, Houtveen, Abbass, Luyten et al. 2014, *Br J Psychiatry* 204:12 (PMID 24385460). Psychotherapy (various) for severe somatoform disorder: physical symptoms d = 0.80 vs 0.31 for TAU.
- Abbass, Town, Holmes et al. 2020, *Psychother Psychosom* 89:363 (PMID 32428905). 17 RCTs of STPP for functional somatic disorders: superior to minimal/TAU/waitlist on somatic symptoms at all time points (small to large effects).
- Abbass et al. 2021, *J Psychosom Res* 145:110473 (PMID 33814192). Within-treatment effects, 2,094 patients: somatic symptom SMD −1.07 short-term (−0.73 after removing outliers).
- Russell, Abbass, Allder 2022, *Epilepsy Behav* (PMID 35390566). Narrative review for FND. Abbass & Haghiri 2025, *Clin Neuropsychiatry* 22:111 (PMID 40401139). Scoping review of >50 publications on functional somatic disorders.

**Cost-offset**
- Abbass, Kisely, Rasic, Town, Johansson 2015, *J Psychiatr Res* 64:114 (PMID 25840829; NCT01924715). Quasi-experimental, 890 treated vs 192 controls: mean CAD 12,628 reduction in healthcare costs over 3 years per treated case vs CAD 708 treatment cost.

**Non-somatic (context)**
- Town et al. 2017, *J Affect Disord* (PMID 28266318), and 18-month follow-up 2020 (PMID 32421603). Halifax treatment-resistant depression RCT, n=60: remission 36% vs 3.7% (TAU) at 6 mo; cost-effective at 18 mo.
- Abbass et al. 2008, *J Nerv Ment Dis* (PMID 18340256). Personality disorders RCT.
- Rahmani, Abbass et al. 2020 (PMIDs 31929466, 32567708). Social anxiety RCTs.
- Heshmati, Wienicke, Driessen 2023, *Psychotherapy* 60:497 (PMID 37843533). Iranian treatment-resistant depression RCT, 20 sessions of ISTDP vs waitlist, n=86, very large effects (depression d = −1.73). Retrospectively registered.

**Gaps and caveats**
- Most trials are small (n=30–81), single-site, often waitlist- or TAU-controlled, and unblinded for self-report outcomes.
- **Investigator allegiance** is high: Abbass co-authors most meta-analyses (including the 2023 umbrella review), and they pool ISTDP with other STPP.
- The largest somatic RCT (n=341) and several others come from one Iranian group (Chavooshi), and other Iranian groups (Dehghani; Heshmati) report very large effects; none has been independently replicated in Western settings. In the n=341 trial ISTDP was equivalent to, not better than, CBT.
- No trials in ME/CFS, long COVID, fibromyalgia as a primary diagnosis, POTS, MCS or EHS.
- No large definitive multi-site RCT for SSD; the Halifax ED trial is still a pilot.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1962–1980s.** Davanloo develops the method in Montreal with videotaped sessions. International symposia in the 1970s–80s draw Malan and others; books appear 1978/80 and 1990. It stays niche among academic psychodynamic clinicians.
- **1990s–2000s.** Training spreads (Coughlin, Frederickson, Abbass, Neborsky; Kuhn's reference text). Abbass begins the Halifax ED/MUS service in 2002 and publishes the first somatic meta-analysis in 2009.
- **2010s.** Rising research output: meta-analyses 2012 and 2014, Halifax depression RCT 2017, Iranian MUP RCTs 2016–17. Books: Frederickson's *Co-Creating Change* (BMA award 2014) and *The Lies We Tell Ourselves*, and Abbass's *Reaching Through Resistance*. Abbass & Schubiner's *Hidden From View* (2018) links ISTDP to the PPD/Sarno/"mind-body syndrome" community, and podcast and YouTube exposure grows through that network (Schubiner, Ozanich, PRT circles).
- **2020–2026.** Peak publication rate: about 10 RCTs/reviews in 2020–2025, FND applications, a 2025 *Am J Psychother* overview, and new RCTs from Iran, Sweden and Canada. Online and telehealth training expands after COVID. IEDTA claims 1,000+ therapists and holds its 12th biennial conference in Croatia (2026). A small subreddit and active Facebook teaching group exist; lay awareness is still well below PRT or the Sarno method.
- **Reach estimate.** Thousands of trained therapists worldwide. Halifax services alone have treated many thousands (one cost study had 890 treated over 9 years). Cumulative patients are plausibly on the order of 10^5 (range 5×10^4 – 5×10^5).

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Confrontational technique.** "Pressure", "challenge" and the "head-on collision" have been criticised as aggressive, risky for fragile patients, and dependent on therapist skill. The later graded format and Frederickson's relational reframing partly answer this.
- **Davanloo's later work.** His later "unlocking the unconscious" and "mobilization of the unconscious therapeutic alliance" formulations rested on his own videotaped case series and drew scepticism. There were also splits between Davanloo and some trainers.
- **Allegiance and replication.** Meta-analyses are largely authored by ISTDP proponents. The large Iranian trials report effect sizes that are unusually large for psychotherapy.
- **Psychogenic framing.** As with other mind-body approaches, calling bodily symptoms the result of repressed rage or guilt can be experienced as invalidating, especially by patients with ME/CFS, long COVID or contested illnesses. ISTDP's own research avoids those diagnoses.
- **Demanding training.** Typically 3+ years with videotaped supervision, which limits scalability compared with app-based programs.

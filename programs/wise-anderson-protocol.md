## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **CP/CPPS is mostly not a prostate infection.** In the protocol's model, much "chronic prostatitis" and related pelvic pain is a **"psychoneuromuscular" disorder**: chronic, nervous-system-driven protective **guarding (contraction) of the pelvic floor muscles**, which the authors liken to a tension headache located in the pelvis.
- **Proposed cycle:** anxiety, stress or an initial injury or irritation (infection, trauma, sitting, sexual or toilet habits) leads to habitual pelvic floor tightening, which leads to myofascial **trigger points**, shortened and ischaemic tissue, and nerve irritation. That causes pain and urinary/sexual symptoms, which raise anxiety and catastrophizing, and the anxiety drives more guarding. The site summarizes this as "tension–anxiety–pain–protective guarding–sore tissue."
- **Referred pain:** trigger points inside and outside the pelvic floor reproduce the patient's specific pain sites. For example, puborectalis/pubococcygeus points refer pain to the penis, and the external oblique to the testicles and groin (Anderson 2009).
- **Autonomic and attentional elements:** chronic sympathetic arousal and "nervous system arousal" sustain the tension, and the patient's habitual attention to and fear of pain keep the cycle going. Wise's research focus is described as "behavioral medicine and autonomic self-regulation."
- The model is **not** framed as "brain-generated pain" in the PRT/Sarno sense. It is a peripheral-plus-central loop in which real muscle and tissue change is the pain generator, and the nervous system and psychology maintain it.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Mostly unified, with condition-specific emphasis.** One pelvic-floor guarding/trigger-point mechanism is applied across CP/CPPS, levator ani syndrome, IC/PBS-type symptoms, coccydynia, "pudendal neuralgia" and sexual pain, in both men and women (Anderson 2016 reported equal improvement by gender). The protocol does differentiate in three ways:
- It targets a **phenotype**: patients with pelvic-floor tenderness. The authors say better selection of muscle-tenderness phenotypes (cf. UPOINT) would improve results.
- It acknowledges that non-muscular causes, such as true bacterial prostatitis, Hunner lesions or nerve entrapment, need other treatment.
- Each patient gets an individual trigger-point map.

It makes no cross-disorder claims about ME/CFS, fibromyalgia and similar conditions.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
1. **Myofascial / trigger-point release (physical therapy).**
   - A therapist works on internal (intrarectal/intravaginal) and external trigger points: abdomen, adductors, gluteals, iliopsoas and other muscles.
   - Patients then learn **self-treatment** with the internal **Trigger Point Wand**, a curved device with an integrated algometer to limit pressure. FDA granted it a De Novo classification (DEN100023, 20 Nov 2012; Class II, product code OSD) to the National Center for Pelvic Pain Research; the developers' press releases call it "FDA approved." Wise is the named inventor on the related patents (assignee New Pelvic Pain Technologies). Typical use is 2–3×/week for 5–10 min.
   - The external **Trigger Point Genie** is used for other areas.
   - A partner can be trained to do the release.
   - Patients receive a personal trigger-point map for local PTs.
2. **Extended Paradoxical Relaxation (EPR).**
   - This is Wise's variant of Jacobsonian progressive relaxation combined with a meditative attitude of *accepting* tension and anxiety rather than fighting it (hence "paradoxical"). Attention rests on sensation without trying to change it.
   - It is taught daily in the clinic, followed by a **yearlong 36-lesson audio course**. The home program includes about 30 h of EPR audio.
   - Recommended dose is about 1 h/day.
3. **Cognitive and attitude work.** Strategies to interrupt catastrophizing and fear of pain, patience and compliance, and reducing "nervous system arousal."
4. **Lifestyle.**
   - Pelvic floor yoga-type stretches.
   - Advice on sitting, diet, exercise and sexual activity.
   - Avoiding Kegel-type strengthening, because the problem is framed as excess tension.
   - Reducing medications at the patient's discretion.
5. **Structure and dose.**
   - Medical evaluation at Stanford or by a participating urologist.
   - **6-day immersion**: 3–5 h/day, about 20–30 h total, groups of ≤14, held year-round. In 2023–24 the clinics ran as 4-day sessions (forum schedule), and the home page now advertises 2-day intensives.
   - About 6 months of daily home self-treatment follows. The site says symptoms typically fall within 3–4 months and that stable remission may take years.
   - The **60-day Home Treatment Program** includes: the Wand and Genie, a preloaded Kindle, 104 videos, a 620–650-page manual, about 30 h of audio and phone tech support. It requires a prescription.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Edmund Jacobson's Progressive Relaxation** (1920s–30s): Jacobson's idea of "residual tension" is the basis of Paradoxical Relaxation.
- **Travell & Simons myofascial trigger-point medicine**, plus earlier transrectal massage for levator spasm (Thiele, 1930s).
- **Meditation / acceptance traditions** (Buddhist-style non-striving attention). This acceptance stance resembles third-wave CBT.
- **Behavioral medicine, autonomic self-regulation and biofeedback.**
- **CBT-style cognitive restructuring** of catastrophizing.
- The biopsychosocial and **central sensitization** framing of urologic chronic pelvic pain syndrome (NIH CPCRN/MAPP era).

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Guidelines endorse the generic components.**
  - The AUA IC/BPS guideline recommends offering manual pelvic-floor physical therapy for patients with pelvic-floor tenderness and avoiding strengthening exercises. This is based largely on the NIH network RCTs (FitzGerald 2009, 2012).
  - The AUA (2025) male chronic pelvic pain guideline and the EAU chronic pelvic pain guideline also support pelvic-floor physiotherapy and psychological treatment for myofascial CPPS.
  - None names the Wise-Anderson Protocol, the wand or EPR specifically.
- **The protocol influenced the field.** It is widely credited with popularizing the "it's the pelvic floor, not the prostate" view among urologists and pelvic PTs. The book is commonly recommended by pelvic-health physiotherapists.
- **Insurance:** the clinic is private-pay. The medical evaluation may be billed to insurance. Home-program and device purchases are self-pay. Generic pelvic-floor PT and psychotherapy are often insurance-reimbursable.

## 6. Evidence base
**Developer studies of the branded protocol** (all single-centre, uncontrolled, Stanford/NCPPR; investigators have financial interests):

| Citation | Design | N | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anderson, Wise, Sawyer, Chan. *J Urol* 2005;174:155–60 (PMID 15947608) | Retrospective case series, refractory CP/CPPS, MFRT + paradoxical relaxation ≥1 mo | 138 men | 72% moderately or markedly improved on global response assessment (GRA). Median symptom drop of 8 points after about 5 sessions. |
| Anderson et al. *J Urol* 2006;176:1534–8 (PMID 16952676) | Case series, sexual dysfunction outcomes | 146 men | 70% GRA responders. NIH-CPSI −9 points. Improvements in sexual symptoms. |
| Anderson et al. *J Urol* 2009;182:2753–8 (PMID 19837420) | Cross-sectional mapping of trigger points to pain sites | 72 men | Each pain site was reproduced by ≥2 of 10 trigger points. |
| Anderson, Wise, Sawyer, Glowe, Orenberg. *J Urol* 2011;185:1294–9 (PMID 21334027) | Case series of the 6-day intensive clinic | 200 enrolled; 116 followed (median 6 mo) | NIH-CPSI −30% (26 to about 18). 60% improved ≥6 points. GRA: 82% improved (59% moderate or marked, 23% slight). 42% lost to follow-up. |
| Anderson, Wise, Sawyer, Nathanson. *Clin J Pain* 2011;27:764–8 (PMID 21613956) | Open-label pilot of the internal wand | 157 enrolled; 113 completed | Trigger-point sensitivity fell from 7.5 to 4 (0–10). No serious adverse events. 28% withdrew. |
| Anderson et al. *Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback* 2015;40:45–52 (PMID 25708131) | Single-arm cohort, self-referred, 6-day clinic plus 6 mo wand/EPR, Oct 2008–May 2011 | 374 of 396 | Medication users fell from 63.6% to 40.1% at 6 mo (−22.7% in mITT). |
| Anderson et al. *Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback* 2016;41:215–24 (PMID 26721470) | Retrospective by-gender analysis of the same single-arm trial | 393 (314 M, 79 F) | Similar 3-point drop in trigger-point sensitivity in men and women. |
| Anderson, Wise, Nathanson. *Urology* 2018;120:23–9 (PMID 30056195) | Meta-analysis of PT/biofeedback/CBT pre-post studies (not Wise-Anderson-specific, but the largest included study, n = 116, matches the developers' 2011 series) | 8 studies, 280 pts | Weighted NIH-CPSI −8.8 points (95% CI 7.5–11.1), which exceeds the 6-point clinically important difference. No control arms were pooled. Wise's listed affiliation is New Pelvic Pain Technologies (the wand company). |

**Independent RCTs of related generic techniques** (not the branded protocol):
- FitzGerald, Anderson RU et al. *J Urol* 2009;182:570–80 (PMID 19535099). Multicentre (6-centre) NIH feasibility RCT in men and women with UCPPS, N = 47 randomized (48 recruited): myofascial PT vs global therapeutic massage. GRA responders: 57% vs 21% (p = 0.03).
- FitzGerald et al. *J Urol* 2012;187:2113–8 (PMID 22503015). 11-centre RCT in women with IC/PBS and pelvic-floor tenderness, N = 81. Responders: 59% vs 26% (p = 0.0012). Pain, urgency and frequency were not significantly different between arms.

**Gaps and critique:**
- There is no randomized or sham-controlled trial of the 6-day protocol, EPR or the wand. ClinicalTrials.gov has no registered trial of the branded protocol (searched "Wise-Anderson," "headache in the pelvis," "paradoxical relaxation pelvic" and "trigger point wand"). The closest is NCT07404397 (University of Michigan, recruiting from Feb 2026), which compares pelvic-floor PT, a generic pelvic-floor massage wand and cyclobenzaprine for high-tone pelvic floor dysfunction.
- PubMed (2017–2026) shows no new developer outcome studies after the 2018 meta-analysis. A 2022 systematic review of psychological treatment for CP/CPPS (Li et al., *Scand J Pain*, PMID 35938980; 7 studies) found paradoxical relaxation among the effective approaches but rated every included study at high risk of bias.
- All branded data come from the developers.
- The patients were self-referred and self-paying, which introduces selection and expectancy effects.
- Attrition was high, and the main outcome was the GRA.
- The same cohort (2008–2011) was reused across publications.
- Regression to the mean is likely in refractory patients who enrol at their worst.
- The paradoxical-relaxation component has never been isolated experimentally.
- Broader context: the Cochrane review of non-pharmacological interventions for CP/CPPS (Franco et al. 2018, CD012551) rated the evidence for physical and psychological therapies as mostly low certainty; the developers' uncontrolled series could not be included in such RCT-based reviews.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1995–2003:** Stanford development phase. The protocol spread online as the "Stanford Protocol" through prostatitis forums (prostatitis.org, Prostatitis Foundation) from the late 1990s.
- **2003:** First self-published edition of *A Headache in the Pelvis* (National Center for Pelvic Pain Research). Several revised editions followed (about 6 by 2012).
- **2005–2011:** *Journal of Urology* publications brought mainstream urology attention; Reuters Health and *Urology Times* covered them. Monthly 6-day clinics ran in California. *Paradoxical Relaxation* book published 2010.
- **2012–2016:** FDA clearance of the wand. Press-release campaigns (2015–16) and the Pelvic Pain Podcast on libsyn.
- **2018:** *Definitive Edition* published by Harmony (Penguin Random House), with an audiobook. By 2026 it had about 500 Amazon reviews averaging 4.4, and translations in about 9 languages, including a Russian-language site.
- **2020s:** Clinics shortened to 4-day (2023–24) and later 2-day formats, and a 60-day prescription home program was added. The forum lists 2 clinics for late 2023 and 8 for 2024. A forum notice says Wise retired at the end of 2024, and an undated edit to a 2021 FAQ post says he "no longer runs his clinic." In Dec 2023 the forum moderator was still telling patients to email Dr Wise. The official site does not mention a retirement and still advertises 2-day intensives in 2026, and its clinic-schedule page still shows a stale 2017 six-day schedule. The Pelvic Pain Podcast still posted episodes in 2025–26.
- **Reach:** clinic throughput is small, about 8 clinics/yr (8 listed for both 2017 and 2024) × ≤14 patients, so roughly 2–3k attendees in total. The book's readership is much larger, probably in the tens to low hundreds of thousands.
- **Forums:** a staple recommendation on ucpps.men (a 2021 FAQ post on the protocol has about 21k views), Reddit r/Prostatitis (about 18.8k members) and r/CPPS (about 216 members) (Wayback captures, mid-2025), and pelvic-PT blogs. The live Reddit API and Reddit search were blocked, so mention counts are unknown. Google Trends was not accessible.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Commercial conflicts:** the developers sell the clinic, the wand, the audio and the home program, and authored all the outcome studies and the supportive meta-analysis.
- **Self-insertion of the internal wand:** the device has safety data from only one uncontrolled pilot (no serious adverse events reported).
- **High cost and unpublished pricing:** costs are high, no prices are published, and the intensive is accessible only to those who can travel to California.
- **Limits of the "it's all muscle tension" framing:** urologists note that it fits only the tenderness phenotype. Some patients report no benefit, which the clinic itself acknowledges ("we are not able to help everyone").
- **Long time course:** the expectation of months to years of improvement makes non-response hard to judge.

## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Cortical ("virtual body") dysfunction.** CRPS and phantom limb pain are framed as being maintained by changes in brain sensorimotor representations of the affected body part:
  - shrunken or smudged S1 maps
  - reduced intracortical inhibition
  - disrupted body schema
  - slowed or inaccurate left/right recognition of the affected limb
  - neglect-like symptoms

  These changes leave the brain's prediction of movement and the actual sensory input out of step, which feeds pain.
- **Pain as protective output (neuromatrix).** Within Moseley/Butler "Explain Pain" thinking, pain is something the brain produces when it judges that protection is needed. Even imagined or observed movement can trigger it; Moseley et al. 2008 (*Arthritis Rheum*) showed that imagined movements alone could raise pain and swelling in people with chronic arm pain, including CRPS.
- **Graded exposure of motor networks.** GMI aims to activate premotor networks first, then motor networks, then a sensory illusion of normal movement. This keeps each step below the threshold that triggers protective pain, and unpairs movement from pain.
- **Order effect as mechanistic evidence.** The 2005 component-order RCT found that benefit depended on the sequence laterality → imagery → mirror. Moseley read this as sequential network activation, not just sustained attention to the limb. In the 2006 trial he nonetheless said the mechanism was not clear.
- **Proposed neural correlates.** In a randomised wait-list crossover study in CRPS (co-authored by Moseley), the GMI period was linked to:
  - normalised S1 activation
  - a larger S1 hand representation
  - restored short-interval intracortical inhibition

  These changes tracked with less movement pain (Strauss 2021).

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Mostly unified, but limited to conditions that are lateralised to a body part.** The same model (a disrupted cortical body map and a protective pain output) is applied to CRPS, phantom limb pain and other persistent limb or regional pain. There is no claim to treat fatigue syndromes, MCS, IBS and similar conditions. Distinctions by condition:
- **CRPS:** the canonical target. Small RCTs found benefit for chronic CRPS1, especially after wrist fracture.
- **Phantom limb pain:** the stages are adapted. Mirror therapy is used for the missing limb, and limb laterality images are still used.
- **Back, neck, knee, shoulder, pelvic pain:** body-part-specific Recognise apps and cards, with laterality judgements of trunk rotation and similar movements. The mechanistic rationale is extended by analogy, and the evidence is thinner.

GMI is not presented as a whole-person recovery programme. It is one tool inside multimodal physiotherapy and pain education (see the Explain Pain entry).

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
Standard protocol (Moseley 2004/2006; the 2026 scoping review found all full-GMI trial arms still follow it):
1. **Left/right judgement (implicit motor imagery), about 2 weeks.**
   - The patient sees photos of hands, feet, backs or other body parts in varied postures and judges whether each is left or right as fast and accurately as possible. Accuracy and response time are tracked against norms (target around 80% or more accuracy, with similar times for both sides).
   - This is thought to activate premotor cortex without conscious movement.
   - Original dose: several sessions per day, roughly 10 min every waking hour in early trials.
   - Tools: the Recognise app (Quick Test, training, *Memory* and *Speed Match* games, a "My Images" library, CSV export for the clinician) or flash cards.
2. **Explicit motor imagery, about 2 weeks.**
   - The patient imagines moving the affected limb into the pictured posture, usually 2–3 times per image, several times a day.
   - Imagery is paced. If imagining causes pain or swelling, the patient goes back a step.
3. **Mirror therapy, about 2 weeks.**
   - The affected limb is hidden behind a mirror and the patient watches the reflection of the healthy limb, first static, then moving gently. The reflection gives a visual illusion of normal, painless movement.
   - Progression: more movements, then adding touch or textures, then bilateral movement.
- **Grading and flexibility.** Clinicians move patients forwards, backwards or sideways between stages according to symptom flare. The daily dose is small and frequent.
- **Adjuncts usually delivered alongside:**
  - pain neuroscience education (Explain Pain)
  - graded activity and exposure
  - tactile discrimination training
  - sensory desensitisation

  Trials have tested add-ons such as tDCS (Lagueux 2018; no added benefit) and memantine (MEMOIR trial, ongoing).
- **Online delivery.** A 2025 RCT ran GMI fully online for pelvic pain, with graded exposure in place of mirror therapy as stage 3. MEMOIR delivers GMI by telehealth (seven 1-hour sessions).

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Mirror box therapy:** Ramachandran & Rogers-Ramachandran (1996) for phantom limb; McCabe et al. (2003) for CRPS.
- **Motor imagery / laterality research:** Parsons' hand-laterality mental rotation work (1980s–90s), and findings that CRPS patients are slower to recognise the affected hand (Schwoebel et al. 2001; Moseley 2004).
- **Cortical reorganisation findings:** Flor et al. 1995 on phantom limb; Juottonen 2002 and Maihöfner 2003 on CRPS.
- **Melzack's neuromatrix and gate-control lineage.** The pain-as-output model was popularised by Butler & Moseley's *Explain Pain* (2003).
- **Graded exposure and behavioural pacing** from physiotherapy and CBT (fear-avoidance work).
- **Stroke neurorehabilitation** (motor imagery, mental practice) and sports-psychology mental rehearsal.
- No contemplative or spiritual roots.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Guidelines.** Named among management options in the RCP (UK) *CRPS in adults* guidelines, 2nd ed. 2018. It appears in the Cochrane physiotherapy-for-CRPS review (Smart 2016/2022) as a "cortically directed rehabilitation" intervention, but that review grades all physio evidence as very low certainty. The Cochrane overview of CRPS interventions (Ferraro 2023) found no high-certainty evidence for any CRPS therapy.
- **Clinical use.** Widely taught in physiotherapy and OT pain curricula and NOI courses. Used in NHS and Australian public pain clinics and in specialist CRPS centres (e.g., Bath, Liverpool), as well as private physiotherapy. CRPS charities such as Burning Nights promote it.
- **Research uptake.** Trials by independent groups in South Africa, Spain, Turkey, Germany, Canada, India and China. Applications have spread to stroke, post-fracture stiffness, knee OA, frozen shoulder, peripheral nerve injury, MS pain and fibromyalgia.
- **Insurance.** Covered only as part of reimbursed physio/OT sessions. No dedicated billing code or payer policy names GMI. The app, cards and handbook are paid out of pocket.

## 6. Evidence base
Branded tools (the Recognise app) have not been trialled as products. The trials test the GMI protocol.

**Founder trials (Moseley):**
- **Moseley 2004**, *Pain* 108:192–8. RCT with crossover; chronic CRPS1; n=13. Large effect: about 25 points on the Neuropathic Pain Scale, NNT about 2 for a 50% reduction, and the effect replicated when the control group crossed over.
- **Moseley 2005**, *Pain* 114:54–61. RCT of component order; CRPS1 after wrist fracture; n=20. Benefit only in the laterality → imagery → mirror sequence, which argues against a pure attention explanation.
- **Moseley 2006**, *Neurology* 67:2129–34. RCT; CRPS1 or phantom limb pain; n=51. VAS pain fell 23.4 mm with GMI vs 10.5 mm with physio/usual care; function improved; gains held at 6 months.

**Independent or partly independent trials:**
- **Johnson et al. 2012**, *Eur J Pain* 16:550–61. Prospective clinical audit at two UK CRPS centres; n=32. **No improvement in pain** (NRS change 0.6 and 0.2). Function improved at one centre. Moseley and McCabe were co-authors. This is the key "failure to translate" paper. Left/right judgement performance did improve.
- **Lagueux et al. 2018**, *Clin J Pain*. RCT of GMI + tDCS vs GMI + sham tDCS; CRPS1; n=22. No added benefit of tDCS, and notably no significant pain change with GMI + sham either.
- **Limakatso et al. 2020**, *Physiotherapy*. Single-blind RCT; phantom limb pain; n=21. GMI beat routine physio for pain at 6 weeks and 6 months, and for pain interference. No difference in quality of life.
- **Strauss et al. 2021**, *Brain Commun*. Randomised wait-list crossover; chronic upper-limb CRPS; n=21; Moseley was a co-author. Movement pain fell during GMI but not during waiting; S1 map size, S1 activation and SICI normalised in parallel.
- **Lopez-Brull et al. 2025**, *Phys Ther*. RCT of online GMI vs wait-list; genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder; n=87. Less pain and better sexual function. This was an adapted protocol, with graded exposure as the third stage instead of mirror therapy.
- **Rierola-Fochs et al. 2026**, *Eur J Pain*. Assessor-blinded RCT of a home-based GMI protocol ("GraMI") vs usual care; phantom limb pain; n=36. Pain was lower than in controls after treatment (p=0.02) and at 12 weeks (p=0.05); no differences on other outcomes.
- **Fibromyalgia.** An RCT in Chile (n=65 women) tested GMI plus therapeutic neuroscience education against standard care. Only a secondary moderator analysis (Araya-Quintanilla et al. 2026, *Arch Phys Med Rehabil*) was checked here, and the primary-outcome paper was not verified.
- **Other small RCTs** in non-classic targets: post-traumatic elbow stiffness (Birinci 2022), distal radius fracture (Dilek 2018), knee OA (Kızkın 2025; Gurudut 2022), frozen shoulder (Yasaci 2025), rotator cuff pain (Sırlan 2025), peripheral nerve injury (Nalbantoğlu 2026) and stroke (Ji 2021; Jia 2024). These are mostly single-centre, and most come from Turkey.

**Systematic reviews:**
- **Bowering et al. 2013**, *J Pain*. 6 RCTs; GMI vs usual physio pooled SMD 1.06 (2 studies, n=63). Left/right judgement training alone showed no effect. Low methodological quality. Moseley was a co-author.
- **Méndez-Rebolledo 2017**, *J Back Musculoskelet Rehabil*. 6 RCTs, 171 participants with CRPS1. GMI and mirror therapy may help, but the evidence is insufficient to recommend them over other treatments.
- **Smart et al. 2022**, Cochrane CD010853.pub3. 34 RCTs of physiotherapy for CRPS; all evidence very low certainty.
- **Ferraro et al. 2023**, Cochrane overview CD009416.pub3. No high-certainty evidence for any CRPS intervention.
- **Limakatso et al. 2023**, *Can J Pain*. Phantom limb pain: GMI weighted mean difference −21.3/100. Imagined movements alone had no effect. Very low certainty.
- **Ríos-León et al. 2024**, *Pain Pract*. Motor imagery in CRPS, broadly defined (GMI and its components), 6 RCTs: pooled pain SMD −1.07 immediately, −1.28 short-term, −1.18 long-term. Evidence was rated moderate quality, downgraded for imprecision. This GRADE rating is more favourable than Cochrane's.
- **Williams et al. 2026**, *Eur J Pain*. Scoping review of 32 RCTs (38 arms, only 8 of them full GMI; Moseley was a co-author). All full-GMI arms follow Moseley's 2004 protocol, and sample sizes are small.
- **Limakatso 2023** and **Ríos-León 2024** have no Moseley authorship. Bowering 2013, Williams 2026, Johnson 2012 and Strauss 2021 do.

**Gaps:**
- The early trials were very small, single-investigator, and unblinded or poorly blinded.
- Effects are much larger in the founder's trials than in clinical practice.
- There is no large multicentre RCT yet; the MEMOIR trial (2×2 factorial of memantine and telehealth GMI, planned n=204, ACTRN12621000175875; protocol in *Trials* 2025) is under way.
- The developer has a commercial interest through NOI materials, and he co-authored several reviews.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **2004–2006.** The founder's RCTs appeared in *Pain* and *Neurology*; the *Neurology* trial drew an editorial.
- **2007–2012.** Rapid uptake in pain physiotherapy alongside *Explain Pain* (2003) and NOI's international courses. The term "graded motor imagery" entered curricula.
- **2012.** The *GMI Handbook* was published. The Johnson et al. audit dampened enthusiasm somewhat.
- **2013.** The Bowering meta-analysis.
- **2016.** NOI announced six Recognise apps for iOS and Android (blog post of 4 March 2016). The app page carries user comments through 2023.
- **2016–2023.** Cochrane reviews included GMI. CRPS charities (Burning Nights and others) and Reddit groups such as r/CRPS and r/ChronicPain recommend Recognise and mirror boxes, judging by forum discussion. Research extended GMI to orthopaedic and pelvic pain.
- **2019.** The Handbook was updated.
- **2023–2026.** Publication rate is steady to rising: 161 PubMed records for the exact phrase, many from 2020–2026. New meta-analyses appeared in 2023 and 2024, an online RCT in 2025, and in 2026 a scoping review and a home-based phantom-limb RCT.
- **Overall pattern.** Consumer-facing popularity is modest. GMI is a clinician tool, not an influencer-led programme.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Replication gap.** The founder's large effect sizes were not reproduced in a real-world UK audit (Johnson 2012), and GMI + sham tDCS also showed no pain change (Lagueux 2018). This raises concerns about small-study effects and fidelity.
- **Evidence certainty.** Cochrane rates the evidence very low certainty, while one independent meta-analysis rates it moderate (Ríos-León 2024). The developer co-authored several reviews and trials and has a commercial interest through NOI materials.
- **Mechanism uncertainty.** Moseley himself states the mechanism is unclear. The causal role of cortical "smudging" in CRPS remains debated.
- **Side effects.** Motor imagery can provoke pain and swelling in people with chronic arm pain, including CRPS (Moseley 2008), which is why the steps are graded.
- **Scope creep.** Use in back, knee, pelvic and other conditions goes beyond the original evidence.

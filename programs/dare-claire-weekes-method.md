## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)

**Weekes (1962–1989): "nervous illness" as sensitization plus second fear.**
- An initial stressor (illness, overwork, shock, or conflict) produces a state of **sensitization**: an over-responsive nervous system in which fear and bodily sensations (palpitations, churning stomach, weakness, "jelly legs", depersonalization) arrive faster and stronger than normal.
- **First fear** is the automatic, reflexive surge.
- **Second fear** is the fear the person adds to the first, through alarm, "what if" thinking and struggle. Second fear re-sensitizes the system and keeps a self-perpetuating fear–adrenaline cycle going.
- Three named pitfalls keep people ill: **sensitization, bewilderment, and fear**. Tension, exhaustion (mental, emotional, muscular) and "habit" (conditioned recurrence) follow from these.
- Recovery does not require finding the original cause. Weekes explicitly rejected both psychoanalytic digging and graded behaviorist desensitization. What matters is changing one's *reaction* to the sensations, so that the body "self-desensitizes" over time.
- Her model is frankly physiological (adrenaline and the nervous system) and non-pathologizing: the symptoms are real but harmless.

**DARE (McDonagh, Panic Away c. 2001–04; DARE 2015–present): the same model in modern language.**
- Anxiety and panic are kept going by **resistance, avoidance and safety-seeking**: trying to stop, control or flee the feeling teaches the brain that the sensations are dangerous.
- Bodily symptoms are framed as a harmless, over-sensitized **fight-or-flight / nervous-system response**.
- "What if" thoughts amplify the cycle, much like Weekes's second fear.
- Recovery means "retraining" the brain and nervous system by repeatedly meeting anxiety with acceptance and even welcome, so that the alarm response desensitizes.
- The website says the method is "based on science and years of experience", and the App Store listing calls the app an "evidence-based" program; neither cites specific studies.
- The framing overlaps with interoceptive-exposure CBT for panic, ACT (defusion, willingness), and the "nervous-system dysregulation" language of brain-retraining programs.

## 2. One mechanism or many? (does it differentiate between named illnesses)

- Both use one transdiagnostic mechanism: a sensitized fear response maintained by second fear, resistance and avoidance.
- Differences across presentations are a matter of emphasis, not mechanism. DARE's 7-day challenges target specific triggers or themes: health anxiety, driving, flying, worry, intrusive thoughts, sleep, and derealization.
- Weekes wrote separately on agoraphobia (1976 book) and on obsessional thoughts and "setbacks". The underlying model is the same, with obsessions treated as fear-locked thoughts that "glue" onto a sensitized mind.
- Neither distinguishes medically defined syndromes such as ME/CFS, POTS or fibromyalgia. Their relevance to the chronic-illness space is indirect: later programs reuse the sensitization and second-fear template.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies (concrete; components, structure, duration, dose)

**Weekes's four principles**
1. **Face.** Do not run from symptoms or situations.
2. **Accept.** Let the feelings come without fighting them, "loosen toward" the sensations.
3. **Float.** Move through the surge without tensing, "float past" the panic rather than brace against it.
4. **Let time pass.** Recovery is gradual and the nervous system needs time to desensitize.

Supporting elements:
- Education that the symptoms are harmless.
- Expecting and accepting setbacks as part of recovery.
- Occupation and absorbing activity, rather than idle rumination.
- Avoiding over-analysis of causes.
- Reduced dependence on sedatives (1978 paper).
- Delivered by self-help book, LP/cassette recordings, letters, and remote guidance. No fixed dose; recovery is expected over months.

**DARE response (McDonagh, 2015 book)**
1. **Defuse.** Answer "what if" thoughts with "so what" or "whatever".
2. **Allow.** Accept the anxious feelings and sensations; stop resisting them.
3. **Run toward.** Actively invite the anxiety and relabel arousal as excitement ("I'm excited by this feeling"). This is paradoxical intention and arousal reappraisal.
4. **Engage.** Shift full attention to an absorbing activity in the present moment instead of monitoring.

Additional book content:
- Specific chapters on panic, generalized anxiety, health anxiety, intrusive thoughts, insomnia, derealization and setbacks.
- Lifestyle advice on sleep, diet, caffeine and alcohol, and exercise.
- Gut–brain/"anxiety and the body" content.

**App**
- Daily audio "retraining" track of about 5–20 min.
- "In-the-moment" SOS audios to use during panic, driving or flying.
- Evening wind-down/sleep audio, breathing exercises and guided meditations.
- Mood journal.
- 7-day themed challenges.
- Premium tier: video lessons, master classes, two live group Zoom calls per month with the "clinical team", peer support groups and an AI assistant.
- Recommended use is daily, with practice "outside your comfort zone" (an exposure orientation).

**Human support**
- 1:1 coaching by an LCSW (with DARE since 2009) and a licensed therapist using ACT/CBT/hypnotherapy, framed as DARE education, not therapy.
- Seasonal 10-day Bootcamp; DARE Advanced/Academy.

**Critiques of content (Healthify NZ review by clinical psychologist Kris Garstang, June 2022, free Android v6.16.6)**
- Some tracks contradict the method: one invites panic while another soothes it.
- Reassurance audio and the SOS button may become **safety behaviours**.
- There are no structured exercises to practise the DARE steps.

## 4. Roots and influences (prior practices/traditions it draws on)

**Weekes**
- Her own experience of misdiagnosed TB, anxiety and palpitations in the 1920s.
- Walter Cannon's fight-or-flight physiology and the adrenaline-centred stress physiology of her era (Selye-adjacent).
- A general-practice, pragmatic reaction against both Freudian analysis and behaviorist systematic desensitization.

**Weekes as a root of later work**
- She anticipated interoceptive exposure in panic-control treatment (Barlow), the fear-of-fear model (Goldstein & Chambless 1978), and ACT's acceptance/willingness stance.
- Her "sensitized nerves" language and her face/accept/float model are widely cited in contemporary anxiety and brain-retraining communities, and by some chronic-illness recovery coaches as a model for "nervous-system" recovery. Specific lineage claims for named programs (DNRS, ANS Rewire etc.) were not verified here and should be checked in each program's own materials.

**DARE**
- Weekes (acknowledged as a major influence in the anxiety-recovery genre).
- CBT for panic (decatastrophizing, exposure).
- ACT (cognitive defusion, acceptance).
- Paradoxical intention (Frankl).
- Arousal reappraisal ("anxious to excited", cf. Brooks 2014).
- Mindfulness and attention redirection.
- Light functional-medicine/lifestyle content (e.g., a podcast episode with psychiatrist Ellen Vora).

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance

- **Weekes.** Not delivered as a formal protocol in health systems. She was nevertheless honoured in her lifetime (MBE 1978; she reportedly addressed US psychiatric audiences, e.g. her 1978 Am J Psychother paper was presented at a 1977 conference) and is retrospectively credited by the ADAA (DuPont family) and biographers as a forerunner of exposure/acceptance CBT. Her books are routinely recommended by clinicians and appear on bibliotherapy reading lists.
- **DARE.** No NHS, NICE, VA or insurer adoption was found. The app's store listing claims ORCHA approval (not independently verified). The Healthify NZ consumer site rates the app 3/5: helpful CBT/ACT elements, but not evidence-based as a whole.
- **Insurance.** Not reimbursable. Subscription and coaching are paid directly.

## 6. Evidence base (list peer-reviewed trials with citation, design, N, key result; note gaps, independence of investigators, critiques)

**Branded program: DARE**
- **No peer-reviewed trials.**
- Grossman JT. *DARE App for Social Anxiety: App Use, Anxiety, and Affect via EMA.* PhD dissertation, Washington University in St. Louis, 2023 (not peer-reviewed). Design: N=10 adults with social anxiety disorder; 30 days of app use; EMA 4×/day; dynamic SEM.
  - App use did not predict next-hour anxiety or affect, and some models showed *worse* anxiety and affect after use.
  - About half of participants improved in general anxiety over time.
  - Conclusion: DARE may not produce short-term benefit for social anxiety. The investigator was independent of DARE.

**Weekes method (author-run, uncontrolled)**
- Weekes C. A practical treatment of agoraphobia. *BMJ* 1973;2(5864):469–71. PMID 4712489. An uncontrolled case series of agoraphobic patients treated largely by remote direction (books, recordings) using her method. The author reported that most improved. There was no control group, the follow-up was self-reported, and the author evaluated her own method. (Exact N and percentages were not verified here; the full text was not accessible.)
- Weekes C. Simple, effective treatment of agoraphobia. *Am J Psychother* 1978;32(3):357–69. PMID 29502. A descriptive and follow-up report arguing that patients must learn to "pass through panic" and understand sensitization and self-desensitization, without needing to find the original cause. It is uncontrolled.

**Related generic techniques (not the branded programs)**
- Interoceptive exposure and panic-control CBT, and ACT for anxiety disorders, are supported by multiple RCTs and meta-analyses.
- Self-help bibliotherapy for panic has RCT support.
- These support the *components*, not DARE or Weekes as packaged.

**Gaps**
- No controlled outcome data for the app or book.
- User counts and "people helped" figures are developer claims and internally inconsistent (1M vs 1.8M downloads).
- No data on use for chronic somatic illnesses.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory (concrete indicators with sources and years)

**Weekes**
- **1962:** *Self Help for Your Nerves* is a bestseller in Australia, the UK and the US.
- **1970s–80s:** further books (1972, 1976, 1984, 1989); LP and cassette programs; a 1983 BBC *Pebble Mill* six-part series. Her first book had sold 400k+ by 1978 (Australian Dictionary of Biography; Wikipedia says 300k+), and some biographers claim "millions" were reached across all books.
- **After her death (1990):** steady sales.
- **2019:** Judith Hoare's biography *The Woman Who Cracked the Anxiety Code* (Scribe) and the ADAA 40th-anniversary tribute (2020) bring renewed attention.
- **2020s:** a strong presence on Reddit (r/Anxiety, r/HealthAnxiety, recovery forums), on YouTube (audio of her recordings), and in chronic-illness "nervous system" recovery content.

**DARE**
- **c. 2001–2004:** *Panic Away* e-book/course (Goodreads: April 2004, BMD Publishing), 80k+ copies claimed.
- **2015:** *DARE* book; 400k+ copies claimed by 2026 (250k claimed in earlier marketing).
- **About 2015–16:** app launch; audiobook (Audible) 2016.
- **2020–22:** pandemic-era growth in anxiety apps. Podcast launched about 2022.
- **2024–26:** app in active development (version 21.3.0 as of Oct 2026; AI assistant). iOS rating 4.8/5 (about 6.8K US ratings); site claims 1–1.8M downloads.

DARE is a mid-tier mental-health app: far smaller than Calm or Headspace, but among the most prominent panic-specific self-help brands.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms (if any)

- **DARE lacks outcome evidence.** The App Store listing calls the app "evidence-based" and the website says "based on science" while citing no trials. The only independent evaluation (N=10) was null-to-negative.
- **Clinical design concerns** (Healthify NZ review):
  - reassurance and SOS audios can act as safety behaviours;
  - contradictory tracks;
  - no emergency or crisis signposting in the SOS feature.
- **Coaching boundaries.** Coaching is delivered by licensed clinicians but labelled "not therapy", which places it outside regulatory and insurance frameworks.
- **Weekes's method rests on clinical observation**, and its 1970s outcome claims are uncontrolled. She never ran a controlled trial, and the ADB notes her methods were "more highly regarded by her patients than by her colleagues" in her lifetime. Some readers find its "just face it" message unsuited to severe or complex presentations.
- **Extension to medically unexplained chronic illness.** When "sensitized nerves" and second-fear models are applied by downstream brain-retraining programs to ME/CFS, long COVID or POTS, critics argue this frames physical illness as fear-maintained. That critique belongs to those programs rather than to Weekes or DARE themselves, which target anxiety.

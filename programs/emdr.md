## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)

**Adaptive Information Processing (AIP) model (Shapiro 2001).** Psychopathology arises from distressing experiences that were *inadequately processed*. These are stored in "state-specific" form, with the original images, emotions, body sensations and negative self-beliefs intact. Present-day cues reactivate these memory networks, producing symptoms. In EMDR, the client holds the memory in mind while attending to bilateral stimulation (BLS: eye movements, taps or tones). This is said to engage an innate information-processing system that links the memory to adaptive networks. Afterwards the memory is less vivid, less distressing, and paired with a more adaptive belief. Physiological arousal also falls (emdr.com).

**Competing and complementary mechanistic accounts (from research, not the official model):**
- **Working-memory taxing.** Van den Hout, Engelhard and others propose that a dual task during recall competes for limited working-memory capacity. This lowers vividness and emotionality, and the memory is then reconsolidated in degraded form. Lee & Cuijpers' 2013 meta-analysis found that eye movements add an effect over the same procedure without them (d = 0.41 in clinical trials; d = 0.74 in lab studies, largest for vividness).
- **Exposure/extinction.** Critics argue the active ingredient is ordinary imaginal exposure (see Davidson & Parker 2001 below).
- **Orienting response, REM-like, and interhemispheric hypotheses.** These are proposed but not established.

**Application to chronic pain and somatic symptoms.** Proponents describe pain as partly a *memory phenomenon*: stored "pain memories" from pre-amputation pain, injuries or medical trauma, plus emotionally charged adverse events, maintain current pain through sensitised threat processing (de Roos 2010; Tesarz 2014). Processing these memories, along with the current pain sensation itself as a target, is proposed to reduce pain intensity and affective distress. German groups (Tesarz, Vock, Hermes) frame the link as bidirectional: adverse life events and trauma raise risk for chronic pain via stress-system, central-sensitisation and catastrophising pathways. EMDR targets the trauma-memory part of this risk architecture.

## 2. One mechanism or many?

**Mostly unified, with condition-specific emphasis.** The AIP model is explicitly transdiagnostic: any disorder rooted in unprocessed adverse experience is a candidate. EMDRIA uses it to justify use in anxiety, depression, OCD, addiction, pain and medical illness.

EMDR does *not* claim a shared neurological illness across ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, POTS and similar conditions, as limbic-retraining programs do. It makes no specific claims about ME/CFS, POTS, MCS, EHS, Lyme or mould. Its pain extensions are framed narrowly. The main candidates are patients with chronic pain *plus* trauma history or high emotional distress (Gerhardt 2016 recruited only trauma-exposed back-pain patients). Frisch & Tesarz (2026) explicitly ask "what for whom" when comparing EMDR with PRT and EAET for chronic pain. Condition-specific protocols exist:
- **Phantom limb pain:** targets amputation-related memories and the phantom sensation itself.
- **Pain-focused EMDR:** standardised 10-session protocols for back pain.
- **Migraine:** protocols that target the headache itself.
- **FND:** an FND-specific protocol from the MODIFI trial.
- **Personalised IBS allocation:** used in PIONEER-IBS.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies

**Standard 8-phase protocol (emdr.com):**
1. **History and treatment planning.** Identify target memories (past), current triggers (present) and future templates. This is the "three-pronged" protocol.
2. **Preparation.** Build the therapeutic alliance and teach stabilisation and affect-regulation skills: "safe/calm place" imagery, container exercises, breathing, resource installation.
3. **Assessment.** Select a target image, negative cognition (NC) and desired positive cognition (PC). Rate Validity of Cognition (VoC, 1–7) and Subjective Units of Disturbance (SUD, 0–10), and locate the associated body sensation.
4. **Desensitisation.** Sets of BLS (about 20–30 s of therapist-guided horizontal eye movements, a light bar, alternating taps or tones). The client briefly reports "what comes up" between sets and continues until SUD is about 0.
5. **Installation.** Strengthen the PC with BLS until VoC is about 7.
6. **Body scan.** Process residual somatic sensation. This is especially relevant to pain.
7. **Closure.** Return to equilibrium; the client keeps a between-session log.
8. **Re-evaluation** at the next session.

**Dose.** Single-incident PTSD may resolve in 3–6 sessions. Complex trauma and chronic pain protocols typically use 10–15 sessions:
- back pain: 10 sessions (Gerhardt 2016)
- fibromyalgia: 15 sessions (Zat Çiftçi 2024)
- phantom limb pain: 12 one-hour sessions over 1 month (Rostaminejad 2017), or about 6 sessions (de Roos 2010)

**Pain adaptations:** the current pain sensation is processed as a target in its own right, alongside memories of injury and medical procedures and associated adverse life events. Other pain adaptations:
- Pain-control techniques: imagery and BLS to change the sensory qualities of pain.
- Group EMDR for chronic pain: 4 treatment days of 5–5.5 h each (Vock 2024, Heidelberg).
- Augmentation with transcranial current stimulation in fibromyalgia (protocols only).

**Self-care.** The "butterfly hug" self-administered BLS and stabilisation exercises are taught for between-session use. EMDRIA advises treatment by a trained, licensed clinician rather than self-administration. Apps and online "self-EMDR" tools exist but are not endorsed and are untested.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Behaviour therapy / systematic desensitisation (Wolpe).** The original name, "Eye Movement *Desensitization*", and the SUD scale come from Wolpe's behaviour therapy.
- **CBT.** Negative/positive cognitions and VoC ratings parallel cognitive restructuring.
- **Exposure therapy** for PTSD (imaginal exposure).
- **Hypnosis and imagery traditions.** Safe-place imagery and resource installation are borrowed from them. Shapiro was at the Mental Research Institute (Palo Alto), with its Ericksonian and brief-therapy milieu. McNally's comparison of EMDR with Mesmerism is a critics' analogy, not a historical root.
- **Psychodynamic ideas** of free association and early-memory linkage ("floatback" technique).
- **Body-focused processing.** The body scan has affinities with somatic therapies.
- For pain: **trauma–pain comorbidity research** and **pain-memory / neuromatrix concepts** (phantom limb pain).

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Guidelines (PTSD).** Checked 2026-10-13 against the guideline pages or PubMed synopses where noted:
  - WHO 2013 *Guidelines on conditions specifically related to stress*: lists EMDR with trauma-focused CBT as a PTSD treatment (WHO press release, 6 Aug 2013).
  - NICE NG116 (2018), verified: "Offer EMDR to adults with ... PTSD ... more than 3 months after a non-combat-related trauma"; "consider" at 1–3 months if preferred; for ages 7–17 only if trauma-focused CBT fails.
  - ISTSS (2018): strong recommendation.
  - VA/DoD PTSD guidelines (2017, 2023), verified for 2023: a **strong** recommendation for prolonged exposure, CPT or EMDR (Ann Intern Med 2024 synopsis, PMID 38408360).
  - APA (2017): conditional recommendation. The ISTSS and APA pages render by script and could not be read automatically, and the Wayback Machine was offline on 2026-10-13, so these two strengths rest on the published guidelines as summarised in the literature.
  - Cochrane (Bisson et al. 2013, CD003388): included alongside TF-CBT among effective trauma-focused therapies.
- **Health systems.** Offered in NHS Talking Therapies/IAPT and specialist trauma services, the VA, and Dutch and German routine care. Dutch and German psychosomatic pain clinics (Heidelberg/Mainz/Ulm "EMDR Pain Network Germany", Vock 2024) use it for pain with trauma comorbidity. UK FND services are trialling it (MODIFI).
- **Pain guidelines** do not currently recommend EMDR for chronic pain. It is absent from NICE NG193 (chronic primary pain).
- **Insurance.** Reimbursed as psychotherapy under standard mental-health billing. US insurers, Medicare and Medicaid cover it when delivered by a licensed clinician for a covered diagnosis (PTSD, anxiety, depression, adjustment disorder). There is no separate procedure code.

## 6. Evidence base

**PTSD (core indication): strong.** Dozens of RCTs and multiple meta-analyses:
- Bisson 2013 Cochrane
- Wright et al. 2024 IPD meta-analysis, *Psychol Med* 54:1580
- Villegas-Ortega 2026, *J Affect Disord* 392:120134

EMDR is broadly comparable to TF-CBT. Wright et al.'s 2024 IPD meta-analysis (8 RCTs, n = 346) found **no significant difference** between EMDR and other psychological therapies on severity, response, remission or dropout. Davidson & Parker's 2001 meta-analysis (*J Consult Clin Psychol* 69:305; 34 studies) found EMDR better than no treatment but **no better than other exposure techniques, with no incremental effect of eye movements**. Lee & Cuijpers' later 2013 meta-analysis (*J Behav Ther Exp Psychiatry* 44:231; 26 studies, N = 849) found an added eye-movement effect (d = 0.41 clinical; 0.74 lab).

Founding papers: Shapiro F. 1989, *J Trauma Stress* 2:199–223 (doi:10.1002/jts.2490020207), 22 participants randomly assigned to EMD or an imagery-based (modified-flooding) comparison. The developer ran the study, and outcome ratings were not blinded; Shapiro F. 1989, *J Behav Ther Exp Psychiatry* 20:211–217 (PMID 2576656).

**Chronic pain and somatic conditions (trials of EMDR itself, not a branded variant):**

| Study | Design / N | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| de Roos et al. 2010, *Pain Res Manag* 15:65–71 (PMID 20458374) | Uncontrolled case series, n = 10, phantom limb pain after leg amputation; mean 5.9 sessions | Significant pain reduction maintained at 3 months; 4 pain-free at 3 months. Of 6 followed up at ~2.8 years, 3 were pain-free. |
| Gerhardt et al. 2016, *Front Psychiatry* 7:201 (PMID 28066274; NCT01850875) | Pilot RCT, n = 40 trauma-exposed non-specific chronic back pain patients; 10 sessions pain-focused EMDR + TAU vs TAU | Pain intensity between-group d = 0.79 post, 0.50 at 6 months; disability d = 0.39 (n.s.) and 0.14. About 50% of the EMDR group improved clinically vs 0 controls. Protocol: Tesarz 2013, *BMC Musculoskelet Disord* 14:256. |
| Rostaminejad et al. 2017, *Int J Rehabil Res* 40:209–214 (PMID 28368869) | RCT, n = 60 amputees with phantom limb pain; 12 × 1-h sessions over 1 month vs control | PLP fell in the EMDR group and stayed down at 24 months; no change in controls (p < .001). Purposive sampling; weak reporting. |
| Nia et al. 2018, *J Pain Res* 11:2107–2113 (PMID 30319285) | 3-arm controlled trial, n = 75 rheumatoid arthritis (non-random selection); 6 sessions EMDR vs guided imagery vs control | Both active arms reduced pain vs control. The abstract's post-treatment subscale means were *lower* (less pain) with guided imagery, yet its conclusion favours EMDR, an internal inconsistency. It calls itself an RCT despite non-random selection. It shares an author (Behnammoghadam) with Rostaminejad 2017. |
| Zat Çiftçi et al. 2024, *Front Psychiatry* 15:1286118 (PMID 38835548; NCT06265194) | RCT, n = 79 fibromyalgia; TAU + 15 EMDR sessions vs TAU | After Bonferroni correction, group effects on VAS pain, WPI, SSS, BDI, PSQI and TSC-40 favoured EMDR, up to 3-month follow-up. The senior author is Konuk, a leading EMDR proponent and trainer in Istanbul. The abstract also reports TSC-40 group p = 0.21 next to a claim of significance (likely a typo). |
| Cope et al. 2025 (MODIFI), *J Neurol* 272:493 (PMID 40627223; NCT05455450) | Randomised feasibility study, n = 50 FND; FND-specific EMDR + neuropsychiatric care vs care alone | Feasible: 58% recruitment, 88% adherence. Greater satisfaction and self-rated FND improvement, and larger reductions in PTSD, depression, anxiety, dissociation, disability and healthcare use with EMDR. Not powered for efficacy. |
| Rämisch et al. 2026 (PIONEER-IBS), *J Psychosom Res* 211:113003 (PMID 42837716) | Pilot RCT, n = 79 IBS; personalised allocation to EMDR or gut-directed hypnotherapy vs reverse allocation vs TAU | Feasible. No difference between personalised and reverse allocation. Within-group somatic-symptom improvement. The efficacy of EMDR for IBS remains untested. |

Other trials (non-chronic or adjacent conditions): post-operative pain in adolescents (Maroufi 2016, *J Adv Nurs*), dental extraction pain in children (Rathore 2024), primary dysmenorrhea (Valedi 2025, *Discov Ment Health*). Registered or ongoing protocols:
- EMDR4IBS (Wertheim 2023, *Trials* 24:785)
- fibromyalgia EMDR + tACS (Gardoki-Souto 2021/2024, *Trials*)
- tinnitus (Luyten 2019, *Trials*)
- sickle-cell pain in children (RELAX; Nery 2026)

**Systematic reviews of EMDR for pain:**
- **Tesarz et al. 2014, *Pain Med* 15:247–63 (PMID 24308821).** Found 2 controlled trials (n = 80) and 10 observational studies (n = 116). Large but highly heterogeneous effects (Hedges g up to −6.87); no adverse events. Conclusion: insufficient evidence for recommendations.
- **Tefft & Jordan 2016, *J Am Psychiatr Nurses Assoc* 22:192.** Literature review.
- **Singla et al. 2025, *J Integr Complement Med* 31:1045–55 (Mayo Clinic; PMID 40735790).** 9 studies including 7 RCTs; effects large in 3 RCTs, moderate in 2, small or n.s. in 1. Conclusion: "promising" but heterogeneous protocols and measures.

**Gaps and critiques.**
- Pain RCTs are small, mostly TAU or waitlist controlled (no attention-matched active control except guided imagery), unblinded, and run by EMDR proponents or training institutes.
- Several come from groups whose sampling and reporting are weak.
- No large, independent, adequately powered RCT exists in chronic primary pain, fibromyalgia, ME/CFS or long COVID.
- No trials exist in ME/CFS, POTS, MCS, EHS or long COVID.
- The head-to-head with PRT/EAET is conceptual only (Frisch & Tesarz 2026).

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1987–1995 (founding and restricted training).** Shapiro's 1989 papers, then Institute-only training (1990–1995).
- **1995–2010 (institutionalisation).** EMDRIA founded in 1995. Shapiro's textbook *EMDR: Basic Principles, Protocols and Procedures* (1995; 2nd ed. 2001; 3rd ed. 2018). Humanitarian Assistance Programs. PubMed records with EMDR in title/abstract: ~12 (1995), 16 (2000), 16 (2005), 29 (2010) (PubMed count, this session).
- **2013–2018 (guideline wave).** WHO 2013, ISTSS 2018 and NICE 2018 endorsements. Van der Kolk's bestseller *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014) features EMDR. Shapiro's self-help book *Getting Past Your Past* (2012). PubMed: 36 (2015).
- **2019–2026 (mass awareness).** Shapiro died in 2019. Prince Harry showed an EMDR session in *The Me You Can't See* (Apple TV+, 2021), a major popular-media spike. TikTok and Instagram "EMDR" content grew, as did telehealth/virtual-BLS platforms. PubMed: 87 (2020), 113 (2023), 159 (2025). EMDR Pain Network Germany formed (2024).
- **Scale.** The EMDR Institute claims "over 100,000 clinicians" worldwide and "millions" treated. EMDRIA cites more than 15,000 (elsewhere 20,000+) members and says EMDR therapists have helped "millions of people across 130 countries". Reddit r/EMDR had 37,694 members in the latest Wayback capture (7 July 2025); the live reddit.com API blocks automated access. Google Trends not retrieved (no public API; web search unavailable 2026-10-13).

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **"Purple hat" critique.** Dismantling studies and Davidson & Parker (2001) suggest the eye movements, the method's namesake, add little beyond exposure. Critics (Herbert, Lilienfeld, Lohr, Rosen, McNally) have called EMDR (Herbert et al. 2000, *Clin Psychol Rev* 20:945–71; Lohr et al. 1999, *J Anxiety Disord* 13:185–207) a case study in pseudoscientific marketing: what is effective in it is not new, and what is new is not effective. Lee & Cuijpers (2013) and working-memory research partly rebut the claim that eye movements are inert. Even so, the comparative-efficacy data (Wright 2024 IPD) show no advantage over other trauma-focused therapies.
- **Founder credentials.** Shapiro's doctorate was from an unaccredited, now-defunct school, a point critics have raised.
- **Commercial gatekeeping.** Early proprietary control of training (Institute-only, with confidentiality expectations, until 1995), trademarked terms, and an expensive certification pathway.
- **Scope creep.** EMDRIA lists chronic pain, addiction and "medical issues" as conditions EMDR is "highly effective" for, despite thin, small-trial evidence outside PTSD.
- **Pain-specific evidence quality.** Small samples, TAU comparators, proponent-run trials, implausibly large effect sizes in some studies (Tesarz 2014 notes g = −6.87), and an internally inconsistent abstract (Nia 2018).
- **Risk.** Generally few adverse events are reported. Clinicians warn about abreaction and destabilisation in dissociative or complex-trauma patients without adequate preparation (Phase 2).

## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Neuroplastic / brain-generated pain.** The brain and nervous system integrate physical, emotional and environmental inputs to produce pain. Pain pathways can become sensitized and keep firing after tissue has healed. Neuroplasticity is presented as both the cause of persistence and the route to recovery.
- **Pain–fear cycle.** Pain triggers fear, hypervigilance and protective behaviour, which signal danger to the brain and amplify pain. The FAQ describes thought and behaviour patterns that the program aims to "de-program." This is the same core model as Pain Reprocessing Therapy (PRT) and Schubiner's "mind-body syndrome."
- **Stress, emotion and life experience.** Life stressors, past experiences (including childhood adversity, the topic of the first podcast episode with David Clarke) and learned patterns are named as contributors. Expressive writing and emotion-focused content reflect the Sarno/Schubiner lineage of suppressed emotion and stress as drivers of symptoms.
- **Framing.** The public-facing language uses the mainstream "biopsychosocial model" plus central sensitization. The Sarno "TMS" label does not appear on current core pages, although the podcast and community are steeped in that tradition.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Essentially one model for everything.** Curable presents a single neuroplastic, fear-avoidance and stress mechanism and applies it to any persistent pain or symptom. It does not offer distinct mechanisms for, for example, migraine versus IBS versus fibromyalgia. Differentiation is limited to two points:
- users are advised to get a medical evaluation first to exclude structural or disease causes, and
- some content (expert classes, podcast episodes) targets specific presentations, such as headache, pelvic pain, gut symptoms, tinnitus and fatigue, using the same framework.

It does not make the strong "one illness, many labels" claims seen in ANS Rewire or DNRS, and it does not prominently target ME/CFS, MCS or EHS.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
**Structure:** a daily plan of short lessons (5–20 min). The recommended pace is about one lesson a day, roughly 3 days a week. Content is personalised by an AI-driven coach ("Clara") based on user preferences. Improvement is described as taking weeks to months, with some users reporting large gains in about 3 weeks.

**Components:**
- **Pain neuroscience education.** Audio lessons on how the brain produces pain, sensitization, the pain–fear cycle, and evidence for brain-generated pain.
- **Somatic tracking.** Attending to sensations with curiosity and safety, as in PRT (Alan Gordon is a podcast co-host).
- **Cognitive reflection / "brain training."** CBT-style reappraisal of threatening thoughts, catastrophizing and fear of movement.
- **Expressive writing.** Journaling about stressors and emotions (Pennebaker/Sarno/Schubiner tradition). Users write outside the app.
- **Guided meditation and relaxation.** Pain-specific meditations, breathing and body-scan-type practices.
- **Flare-up support.** On-demand coping tools, such as a breathing cue and reassurance scripts.
- **Expert sessions.** Live workshops, Q&As and video classes with physicians, PTs and psychotherapists (the "Classes Plus" tier).
- **Recovery stories, community and podcast.** Social proof, plus the *Like Mind, Like Body* podcast and the *Tell Me About Your Pain* spin-off (Alan Gordon and Alon Ziv).
- **Clinician directory.** Links users to local "mind-body" practitioners.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **John Sarno's TMS model** (suppressed emotion, psychological reattribution of pain) and its successors: Howard Schubiner (*Unlearn Your Pain*, mind-body syndrome), David Clarke (Psychophysiologic Disorders Association), David Schechter and David Hanscom.
- **Pain Reprocessing Therapy** (Alan Gordon), the source of somatic tracking and safety reappraisal.
- **Pain neuroscience education** (Moseley, Butler, Louw) and gate-control / neuromatrix pain science, with central sensitization.
- **CBT** for pain (cognitive restructuring, fear-avoidance, catastrophizing).
- **Mindfulness meditation** (MBSR-style; Buddhist insight-meditation roots).
- **Pennebaker expressive writing** and the emotional awareness/expression tradition (EAET).

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Clinician use:** Curable markets a free referral program (patients get 6 weeks free). It claims 23k clinicians "trust Curable." No health-system contracts, payer partnerships, VA/DoD programs or employer benefit deals were identified (not verifiable here because web search was unavailable).
- **Certification:** ORCHA-certified (a UK digital-health compliance assessment used by NHS organisations). This does not mean NHS commissioning or a NICE recommendation.
- **Academic endorsement:** Independent systematic app reviews place it among the best pain apps, for example top-ranked on self-management support (Devan 2019) and among the three highest-scoring apps that MacPherson 2022 recommended to clinicians.
- **Insurance:** Not reimbursed by insurers. It is not an FDA-cleared prescription digital therapeutic, unlike, for example, RelieVRx. It is eligible for HSA/FSA payment.

## 6. Evidence base
**Trials of the branded program:**
- **Thomson CJ, Pahl H, Giles LV (2024).** *Can J Pain* 8(1):2352399. doi:10.1080/24740527.2024.2352399. PMID 39175941.
  - **Design:** RCT, 6 weeks of Curable vs. waitlist usual care. The trial was registered (NCT05090683, Oct 2021) and stated it had no funding source, and the authors (University of the Fraser Valley kinesiology) report no affiliation with Curable.
  - **Sample:** N=198 adults with non-malignant chronic pain (98 app vs 100 waitlist; 82% women; mean pain duration 13.6 y), recruited from 9 countries (88.8% Canada, 6% US).
  - **Results:** Pain severity fell by −0.67 BPI points vs. control (d=0.43, p<.001). Pain interference fell by −0.60 (95% CI −1.18 to −0.03; d=0.27, p=.038). Pain catastrophizing, anxiety, depression, stress and PROMIS interference also improved. More frequent app use correlated with greater improvement. At 12 weeks, within-group gains were maintained among completers (n=65 of 73; severity d=0.53), but this was uncontrolled because the waitlist arm had received the app by then.
  - **Limitations:** No active or attention control; unblinded; self-reported diagnoses; dropout was 25.5% in the intervention arm vs. 2% in the control arm; no control group at 12 weeks; effects were under 1 point on a 0–10 scale.
  - **Recognition:** Won the 2024 Ronald Melzack–*Canadian Journal of Pain* Paper of the Year award (Katz et al. 2025, PMID 40917111).

**Qualitative and evaluation studies (not efficacy trials):**
- **Doi E et al. (2025).** *Pain Res Manag*, doi:10.1155/prm/6996211. Reflexive thematic analysis of interviews with 12 users. Users felt the app filled gaps in care and was best used alongside wider support.
- **Hunter R, Gorely T, Beattie M (2026).** *JMIR mHealth uHealth* 14:e66435, doi:10.2196/66435. Realist evaluation with 9 people with chronic low back pain using Curable for 3 months. The app was empowering when its information was seen as credible, but people with strong biomedical beliefs engaged less.
- **App-quality reviews:**
  - **Devan H et al. (2019).** *JMIR mHealth uHealth* 7:e13080. Curable tied for the top self-management-support score (8/14 items); no app had been tested in pain populations.
  - **MacPherson M et al. (2022).** *Can J Pain* 6:33–44. Curable was among the top three apps on psychological components and MARS quality.

**Related, not branded:** Evidence for the component techniques is stronger than for the app itself. This includes PRT (Ashar et al. 2022 *JAMA Psychiatry*, which used somatic tracking), EAET, CBT for pain, pain neuroscience education and mindfulness. That evidence should not be read as evidence for Curable.

**Gaps:**
- There is only one RCT, against a waitlist.
- There are no trials in specific conditions (fibromyalgia, IBS, migraine, long COVID).
- There are no head-to-head or active-control comparisons and no long-term controlled follow-up.
- Company outcome claims (82% improved QoL, n≈10k; 96% better understanding, n≈7.8k) come from uncontrolled member surveys with selection bias.

- **2021–2022:** Interest in neuroplastic pain grew after the PRT trial (*JAMA Psychiatry*, Sept 2021) and the documentary *This Might Hurt* (2022), with a likely tailwind for Curable. Not quantified here.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Marketing vs. evidence:** "#1 app for chronic pain" and "1M+ users" are company claims. The cumulative user count probably includes free and trial users.
- **Subscription model:** Common app-store complaints about auto-renewing annual subscriptions are typical of the category; this review did not verify them systematically.
- **Risk of over-attribution:** As with other Sarno/PRT-lineage tools, the risk is that users attribute symptoms of undiagnosed structural or inflammatory disease to "neuroplastic pain." The site advises medical evaluation first.
- **Fit:** Qualitative work suggests people with strong biomedical illness beliefs disengage (Hunter 2026). The RCT's differential dropout (25% vs 2%) is consistent with that.
- **Scope creep in founder narratives:** In the founders' recovery-story episode (podcast ep. 17, 2018), the conditions they say they recovered from include "lyme disease" and "fainting spells" alongside pain. This goes further than the pain-focused positioning on the main pages.
- **Less contested than ME/CFS-focused brain-retraining programs**, since it centres on pain rather than claiming to cure ME/CFS, MCS or EHS.

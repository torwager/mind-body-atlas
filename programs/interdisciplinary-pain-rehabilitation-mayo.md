## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)

The model is the **biopsychosocial model** of chronic pain. Pain persists, and disability grows, through interacting physical, psychological and social factors rather than ongoing tissue damage alone. Mayo's materials and papers emphasise several strands:

- **Operant learning (Fordyce).** Pain behaviours (guarding, grimacing, resting, medication-taking, help-seeking) get reinforced by attention, relief and avoided duties. Once reinforced, they persist apart from nociception. Treatment shifts the contingencies so that "well behaviours" are rewarded.
- **Fear-avoidance and deconditioning.** Catastrophizing and fear of movement lead to inactivity, then deconditioning, then more pain and disability. Functional restoration (Mayer/Gatchel) treats this as a "deconditioning syndrome" to be reversed through quantified, graded physical training.
- **Medication as a perpetuating factor.** Long-term opioids, benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants and other symptom-driven medications are framed as worsening function, mood and pain sensitivity (opioid-induced hyperalgesia) and as feeding the illness cycle. Tapering is therefore core to the treatment, not optional.
- **Central sensitization and stress physiology.** Newer Mayo education describes chronic pain as an altered nervous-system state (central amplification), modulated by stress, mood, sleep and attention. Biofeedback and relaxation are justified as lowering autonomic arousal.
- **Pediatric POTS/fatigue.** Mayo's pediatric team (Bruce, Fischer, Kizilbash) frames adolescent POTS disability as partly maintained by deconditioning, symptom focus, school avoidance and family accommodation. The autonomic disorder is real, but the disability is treated with the same rehabilitative logic.

The stated goal is **function and quality of life, not cure or pain elimination**.

## 2. One mechanism or many?

**Mostly unified, with condition-specific emphasis.** One shared rehabilitative model is applied across diagnoses. Patients with back pain, fibromyalgia, headache, CRPS, abdominal pain and POTS attend the same groups, and the program deliberately de-emphasises diagnosis and the search for a cure. Medical diagnosis still matters, though:

- Patients are screened medically, and the program is offered only once surgical or disease-specific causes have been addressed.
- Pediatric POTS patients get added autonomic measures: fluids and salt, graded upright tolerance, and aerobic reconditioning.
- Headache patients follow medication-overuse withdrawal protocols (Bruce 2008).
- Opioid-dependent patients get supervised tapers.

Unlike the newer "neuroplastic" programs, the model does **not** claim that the conditions are one brain disorder. It is agnostic about ultimate etiology and targets the shared *maintaining* factors: behaviour, fear, deconditioning, medications and mood.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies

**Adult 3-week program** (Mayo core components; about 8 h/day × 15 days, roughly 120 contact hours):
- **Group pain education** in a CBT or ACT framework: self-regulation, stress management, problem-solving, cognitive restructuring of catastrophic thoughts, pacing, goal-setting, communication and assertiveness, sleep, mood and anger management.
- **Physical therapy, daily:** aerobic conditioning, strengthening and stretching, body mechanics, and **extinguishing pain behaviours** (staff are trained not to reinforce pain talk or guarding, a Fordyce legacy).
- **Occupational therapy:** pacing, activity scheduling, ergonomics, return to valued roles and work, hobbies.
- **Biofeedback and relaxation:** diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, thermal or EMG biofeedback, sometimes mindfulness or meditation.
- **Medication management:** supervised tapering of opioids, benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants, butalbital and other symptom-driven drugs during the 3 weeks. Opioid tapering is generally required; per Gilliam (2017), about 80% complete the program anyway. **Chemical-health education** is included.
- **Nursing** visits on sleep, nutrition, smoking cessation and health habits.
- **Family programming:** sessions teaching family members not to reinforce illness behaviour and to support self-management.
- **Discharge plan and aftercare:** an individualised maintenance program of daily exercise, relaxation practice and scheduled activity, plus aftercare follow-up.

**Pediatric 3-week program** (Rochester, ages about 13–22): physical, occupational and recreational therapy, group CBT, and stress-management lectures. A parent attends throughout and learns to reduce accommodation and promote a return to school and function. Opioids are discontinued; in the first 1000 patients, all but 4 tapered off. POTS-specific reconditioning is included.

**Two-day educational programs** (adult and pediatric) teach the concepts to people who cannot attend for 3 weeks.

**Related programs using the same model:** Cleveland Clinic Chronic Pain Rehabilitation Program; Boston Children's Mayo Family Pediatric Pain Rehabilitation Center (Brookline/Waltham, ages about 8–17; the "Mayo Family" in the name refers to a donor family, not Mayo Clinic); Cincinnati Children's FIRST program (inpatient, ages 10–17); German Paediatric Pain Centre, Datteln (inpatient IIPT); German "multimodale Schmerztherapie"; Scandinavian and Dutch multidisciplinary rehabilitation; VA interdisciplinary pain programs; PRIDE (Dallas) functional restoration for workers' compensation.

## 4. Roots and influences

- **Bonica's multidisciplinary pain clinic** (Tacoma 1940s; University of Washington from 1960): many specialties in one team.
- **Fordyce's operant conditioning** (1968–1976): behaviourism applied to pain behaviours, with time-contingent rather than pain-contingent medication and graded quotas of activity.
- **CBT for pain** (Turk, Meichenbaum and Genest, 1983; Beck-derived cognitive restructuring), and later **ACT** (third-wave CBT).
- **Gate control theory** (Melzack and Wall, 1965) and later neuromatrix ideas, which legitimised psychological modulation of pain.
- **Biofeedback** and the **Relaxation Response** (1960s–70s).
- **Functional restoration and sports medicine** (Mayer and Gatchel, 1985–87): quantified physical capacity testing and graded work-conditioning.
- **Fear-avoidance model** (Lethem 1983; Vlaeyen and Linton 2000) and graded exposure.
- **Addiction medicine / chemical-dependency treatment** (the "Minnesota model"), which shaped the medication-withdrawal and chemical-health components at Mayo.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance

- **Mainstream standard.** This is the archetypal tertiary treatment for disabling chronic pain and appears in major pain textbooks and IASP curricula. CARF accreditation standards exist for "interdisciplinary pain rehabilitation programs." NICE NG59 (low back pain) recommends considering a combined physical and psychological program with a CBT approach. US VA/DoD guidance and the 2016/2022 CDC opioid guidelines endorse multimodal and nonpharmacological care and opioid tapering, which this model operationalises.
- **Contraction in the US.** CARF-accredited programs fell from about 205–210 (cited variously for 1988 or 1998) to 84 in 2005 and about 54 in 2022 (an earlier figure of 67 for 2017 could not be verified). Many programs reportedly dropped accreditation without closing, so accredited counts understate activity. Schatman attributes the decline to managed care "carving out" components, low reimbursement, and the rise of procedure-based interventional pain medicine. Growth since then has been mainly in the VA (2 to 20 CARF programs, 2009–2019; Murphy et al., *Pain Med* 2021) and in Germany and Scandinavia.
- **Insurance.** Billable as outpatient hospital or rehabilitation services under PT/OT/psychology and health-behaviour codes, or under a program per-diem. Medicare covers it. Commercial payers often require prior authorisation and frequently deny it. Mayo staff report advocating for good candidates, and NPR (2017) noted that only a small minority pay fully out of pocket. A Mayo Florida–Florida Blue analysis (Sletten 2015) reported large downstream reductions in medical costs, used to argue for coverage.

## 6. Evidence base

**Trials and reviews of the generic model (not Mayo-specific):**
- **Flor, Fydrich and Turk 1992**, *Pain* 49:221 — meta-analysis of 65 studies of multidisciplinary pain centres for chronic back pain. Superior to no treatment, waitlist and single-discipline care for pain, mood, return to work and healthcare use, and effects were stable. Study quality was rated "marginal."
- **Mayer et al. 1987**, *JAMA* 258:1763 — prospective non-randomized cohort of functional restoration for industrial low back injury. 116 treated vs 72 untreated; at 2 years 87% vs 41% were working, with about half the rate of repeat surgery.
- **Guzmán et al. 2001**, *BMJ* 322:1511 — systematic review of 10 RCTs (n=1964). Strong evidence that *intensive* biopsychosocial rehabilitation with functional restoration improves function, and moderate evidence that it reduces pain. Less intensive programs were ineffective.
- **Kamper et al. 2015**, *BMJ* 350:h444 (Cochrane) — 41 RCTs, n=6858, chronic low back pain. Versus usual care: pain SMD 0.21 (about 0.5/10 points) and disability SMD 0.23 (moderate quality). Versus physical treatment: pain SMD 0.51 and disability SMD 0.68 (low quality, heterogeneous). Better work outcomes than physical treatment (OR 1.87) but not than usual care.
- **Hechler et al. 2014**, *Pain* 155:118 — RCT, German inpatient pediatric IIPT vs waitlist (n=104). 55% vs 14% improved at post-treatment. Disability, school absence, depression and catastrophizing improved immediately; pain intensity and anxiety improved by short-term follow-up.
- **Hechler et al. 2015**, *Pediatrics* 136:115 — systematic review and meta-analysis (1 RCT, 9 non-randomized studies). Large gains in disability and small-to-moderate gains in pain and depression, with extreme heterogeneity.
- **Claus et al. 2022**, *Pain* 163:2281 — preregistered individual-patient-data meta-analysis across 13 pediatric IIPT sites, including a Mayo co-author. Large improvements in pain (g=−1.28), disability (g=−1.91) and missed school (g=−0.99), and moderate improvements in anxiety and depression. Certainty of evidence was **very low to low** because the studies were pre–post designs.
- Gatchel and Okifuji 2006, *J Pain* — narrative review arguing that comprehensive programs are clinically and cost-effective compared with surgery, implants and medications.

**Mayo-specific outcome studies (all uncontrolled cohorts by in-house investigators):**
- **Hooten et al. 2007**, *Pain Med* 8:8 — 159 consecutive fibromyalgia patients (2002–03). Significant pre–post improvements on the MPI, SF-36, catastrophizing and CES-D, and fewer patients on opioids, NSAIDs, benzodiazepines and muscle relaxants at dismissal.
- **Townsend et al. 2008**, *Pain* 140:177 — 373 consecutive adults, 57% on daily opioids. 91% completed and 70% returned 6-month data. All outcomes improved at discharge and 6 months, with no difference by opioid status after opioid withdrawal.
- Crisostomo et al. 2008, *Am J Phys Med Rehabil* 87:527 — retrospective chronic low back pain cohort (n=383). Benefits after analgesic withdrawal were similar regardless of prior spine surgery.
- Darchuk et al. 2010, *Pain Med* — geriatric patients had longitudinal outcomes comparable to younger adults.
- **Sletten et al. 2015**, *Pain Med* 16:898 — 53 Mayo Florida patients with Florida Blue claims data. Medical costs were 64–90% lower in the 3–18 months after treatment than in matched pre-treatment periods. The design is a pre–post comparison, open to regression to the mean.
- **Bruce et al. 2016**, *Clin Pediatr* 55:1300 — 33 adolescents (ages 11–18) with **POTS**. Functional disability improved and depression and catastrophizing fell over 3 weeks.
- **Bruce et al. 2017**, *Mayo Clin Proc Innov Qual Outcomes* 1:141 — first 1000 pediatric patients (2008–2015, ages 9–24). Functional Disability Inventory fell from 27 to 9, CES-DC from 27 to 14, and pain catastrophizing from 26 to 14. Opioids were discontinued in all but 4 of the 16% who were taking them. Bruce et al. 2017, *Clin J Pain* 33:535 ("Getting Back to Living"; n=171 adolescents) showed gains maintained at 3-month follow-up, with all daily opioid users (about 14%) tapered off.
- Engelberg-Cook et al. 2021, *Mayo Clin Proc Innov Qual Outcomes* 5:1056 — 150 fibromyalgia patients at Mayo Florida. Improvement in the no-opioid group; opioid use at admission may modify outcomes.
- Schumann et al. 2020 (older adults) and 2022 (*Pain Med*; catastrophizing and self-efficacy mediate outcomes); Craner et al. 2022 (*J Affect Disord*; mediators of improvement in depression); Morrison et al. 2023 (*Pain Med*; young-adult program, preliminary outcomes).

**Gaps:** No RCT of the Mayo program itself. Evidence comes mainly from completer analyses with selected, motivated referrals and short follow-up (discharge to 6 months). Mayo's evidence is almost entirely in-house. The generic-model RCTs are mostly in chronic low back pain, with small effects against active usual care. Evidence for fibromyalgia, POTS, headache and functional disorders is observational. Mayo's pediatric POTS research (actigraphy, resting-state fMRI before and after the program) is registered but small.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory

- **1960s–70s:** Bonica's University of Washington clinic, Fordyce's operant ward and Mayo's 1974 PRC. Pain clinics proliferate after IASP is founded (1973–74).
- **1980s–1990s:** Peak. Functional restoration (Mayer/Gatchel, JAMA 1987) spreads through workers' compensation, and CARF accreditation begins. A figure of "more than 1000" US integrated clinics in the 1990s is sometimes attributed to Schatman but could not be verified; the CARF-accredited count was about 205–210.
- **1998–2010s:** US decline (CARF programs ~205–210 → 84 by 2005 → ~54 by 2022) as managed care cuts reimbursement and interventional procedures and opioid prescribing rise. European programs continue to grow, and Germany codes "multimodal pain therapy" in its DRG system.
- **2008–2016:** Mayo expands: pediatric PRC (2008), Florida (2011), Arizona (2016). Pediatric IIPT programs spread (Boston Children's, Cincinnati, Stanford, Seattle, Datteln).
- **2016–2020:** The opioid crisis and the CDC 2016 guideline revive interest in tapering-based rehabilitation. NPR/KHN feature the Mayo PRC (Aug 2017: >300 patients/yr, "40-year-old program"), and VA programs expand tenfold.
- **2020s:** Steady. Hybrid, virtual and 2-day formats appear, along with young-adult tracks. The model is increasingly cited as the conventional comparator for app-based and "neuroplastic" programs (Curable, PRT), and for long COVID and POTS rehabilitation.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms

- **Mandatory opioid tapering.** Praised during the opioid crisis, but some patients and advocates describe the requirement as coercive. Forced tapers outside such programs have been linked to harm, and the CDC 2022 guideline revision warns against abrupt tapering. Mayo's tapers are supervised and patients consent.
- **Operant "ignoring" of pain behaviour** has been criticised as invalidating, especially for patients with organic disease. Patient forums contain both "life-changing" and "blamed and dismissed" accounts.
- **Pediatric POTS and fatigue.** Treating POTS disability rehabilitatively (pushing through symptoms, limiting accommodation) is contested by some POTS and ME/CFS patient communities. Rehabilitation focused on graded activity is controversial for ME/CFS because of post-exertional malaise; see NICE NG206 (2021) on graded exercise therapy.
- **Access and equity.** The program costs tens of thousands of dollars, insurers frequently deny it, and attending requires 3 weeks away from home. The US decline means few patients can reach such programs.
- **Evidence quality.** Effects against usual care are small (Cochrane). Mayo outcome reports are uncontrolled and in-house, and cost-offset claims rest on small pre–post samples.

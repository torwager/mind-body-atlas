## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Framework:** IBS is treated as a *disorder of gut–brain interaction* (Rome IV). Symptoms arise from bidirectional dysregulation between CNS and gut: visceral hypersensitivity, altered motility, heightened attention and threat-appraisal toward gut sensations, and stress reactivity. Hypnosis is proposed to give top-down control over gut sensation and function.
- **Visceral hypersensitivity / central processing:**
  - Manchester data showed that hypnotherapy normalised abnormal rectal sensitivity, both hyper- and hyposensitivity (Lea 2003).
  - fMRI showed that responders had attenuated anterior and posterior insula BOLD responses to rectal distension (Lowén 2013). Hypnosis responders differed from education responders mainly in posterior insula and in anticipation-related responses.
- **Motility / gut physiology:** Hypnotherapy reduced the sensory and motor components of the gastrocolonic response (Simrén 2004). Hypnotic suggestion can also alter colonic motility in the lab.
- **Psychological / cognitive:**
  - UNC found no change in rectal pain thresholds, smooth-muscle tone or autonomic measures. Instead, improvement tracked large reductions in somatization and psychological distress (Palsson 2002).
  - Manchester reported cognitive change, with reduced catastrophizing about symptoms (Gonsalkorale 2004).
  - Paediatric work found no change in rectal sensitivity (Vlieger 2010).
- **Immune (speculative):** Proposed in IBD work (Keefer 2013), where hypnosis prolonged UC remission.
- **Net:** The mechanism remains unresolved. Candidates are central modulation of visceral afferent processing, reduced gut-focused anxiety and hypervigilance, and some autonomic/motility effects. There is no claim of a single cross-disorder "brain retraining" mechanism of the ANS-Rewire/DNRS type.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Mostly unified, with condition-specific emphasis.** The common model is a brain–gut axis dysregulation, with hypnosis acting on sensation, motility and gut-focused anxiety. The suggestions and imagery are tailored to the organ and symptom:
- colonic "river" or flow imagery for IBS
- oesophageal-directed scripts for functional heartburn (Riehl 2016)
- upper-GI imagery for dyspepsia
- chest-focused work for non-cardiac chest pain

The approach is explicitly *organ-directed*. It does not claim to treat ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, MCS or similar conditions. Whorwell's group noted that extracolonic symptoms (fatigue, backache, urinary symptoms) and anxiety and depression improve as well. That is consistent with a broader somatization or central-sensitization component.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
**Manchester protocol (Whorwell)**
- Traditionally 12 weekly sessions of about 1 hour over 3 months. A 2021 RCT (Hasan) found 6 sessions non-inferior to 12.
- Session sequence:
  1. Induction (e.g. eye fixation) and deepening.
  2. Ego-strengthening suggestions.
  3. Gut-focused work: the patient's hand on the abdomen generating warmth and comfort; imagery of the gut as a smoothly flowing river, adjusted to diarrhoea or constipation; suggestions of control over gut function and reduced pain.
- Patients learn self-hypnosis and practise daily with audio recordings between sessions.
- Can be delivered by trained hypnotherapists, nurses or psychologists, individually or in groups, including by Skype/video.

**North Carolina protocol (Palsson)**
- 7 sessions every 2 weeks over 12 weeks, read verbatim from a full script.
- Daily home practice with an audio recording of ~15–20 min.
- Suggestions target gut comfort, normal motility, reduced attention to gut sensations, and a calm "protective" stance toward the gut.
- The verbatim scripts are designed for portability. They have been used by non-specialists, by nurses (Sweden: 8 sessions, individual or group), and in fully automated audio programs, e.g. 7 audio sessions over 3 months for functional dyspepsia (Kinsinger 2022).
- Variants exist for ulcerative colitis and paediatric guided imagery.

**Paediatric**
- 6 therapist sessions over 3 months, or a home CD used ≥5 times per week for 3 months (Rutten 2017).

**Regulora (metaMe Health)**
- Prescription smartphone app delivering self-administered gut-directed hypnotherapy over 12 weeks, with sessions roughly weekly plus daily breathing/relaxation practice.
- Described as Palsson-style scripted hypnosis; Palsson's involvement with metaMe was not confirmed (not stated on metamehealth.com or in the Berry 2023 author list).
- The pivotal trial compared it with a digital muscle-relaxation app.

**Self-care across protocols**
- Daily self-hypnosis or audio practice.
- Diaphragmatic breathing and relaxation.
- Imagery practice used during symptom flares.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Clinical hypnosis tradition:** Mesmer → Braid → Erickson, along with 20th-century medical hypnosis for pain (e.g. Hilgard). The Manchester protocol uses classic induction/deepening and Hartland-style ego-strengthening.
- **Relaxation tradition:** autogenic training and the relaxation response.
- **Biopsychosocial medicine and brain–gut axis science:** Drossman, Rome Foundation, Mayer. Psychogastroenterology now groups it with CBT under "brain–gut behaviour therapies."
- **CBT:** influences reattribution and cognitive-change framing.
- **Not drawn from:** contemplative traditions, Sarno, or polyvagal theory.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Guidelines:**
  - NICE CG61 (2008, updated 2017): consider psychological interventions, including hypnotherapy, for IBS not responding to drugs after 12 months.
  - ACG 2021 (Lacy et al.; GRADE): suggests gut-directed psychotherapy (CBT or gut-directed hypnotherapy) for global IBS symptoms.
  - BSG 2021 (Vasant et al.): recommends gut-directed hypnotherapy for refractory IBS.
  - Rome Foundation psychogastroenterology working-team reports.
- **Services:** NHS hospital units (Wythenshawe; others listed in BSG guidance). Nurse-delivered GI hypnotherapy in Sweden (Sahlgrenska). Dutch hospital-based programmes. US academic centres (Northwestern, Michigan, UNC, Cedars-Sinai and others), often via Rome Foundation-trained GI psychologists.
- **Insurance:**
  - UK: NHS-funded where commissioned, though access is patchy.
  - US: covered when billed as health-behaviour or psychotherapy services by licensed clinicians; hypnosis-only CPT coding is often not reimbursed.
  - Regulora: prescription-only; payer coverage unclear or limited (not verified).

## 6. Evidence base
Trials of the **specific Manchester or North Carolina protocols** are marked **[M]** or **[NC]**. Others are generic gut-directed hypnotherapy, usually Manchester-derived.

**Meta-analyses**
- Webb et al. 2007, Cochrane: 4 trials, N = 147. Quality too poor to conclude.
- Schaefert et al. 2014, *Psychosom Med*: 8 RCTs, N = 464. Adequate relief RR 1.69, NNT 5; long-term NNT 3.
- Krouwel et al. 2021, *Complement Ther Med*: pooled SMD not significant. Higher-volume therapy (≥8 sessions, ≥6 h) was effective.
- Black et al. 2020, *Gut* (network meta-analysis): gut-directed hypnotherapy among efficacious psychological therapies.
- Goodoory et al. 2024, *Gastroenterology*: face-to-face gut-directed hypnotherapy effective for abdominal pain. RR 0.77.
- Thakur et al. 2025, *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol*: 12 hypnotherapy RCTs, N = 1,507. RR 0.79 vs waitlist; ranked below CBT variants and dynamic psychotherapy. No trial at low risk of bias; publication bias present.
- Khasawneh et al. 2026, *Gut*: forms of gut-directed hypnotherapy superior to waitlist. Low or very low certainty.

**Gaps and critiques**
- Much of the foundational evidence comes from the originating Manchester group (developer allegiance).
- Uncontrolled audits dominate the long-term data.
- Blinding is impossible, and credible attention controls are rare.
- Effects shrink outside expert centres (Lindfors 2012) and in independent network meta-analyses.
- The only pivotal digital (Regulora) trial missed its primary endpoint.
- The digital Nerva RCT (Anderson 2025, *Am J Gastroenterol*, N = 240) was positive vs an active control; see the Nerva entry.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- **1984–1990s:** Single centre (Manchester). Small RCTs and follow-ups in 1987–1989; the hypnotherapy unit was established and staffed by six therapists (described in the 2002 audit).
- **2002–2008:** UNC scripted protocol published (2002). Manchester audits (250 in 2002; 204 long-term in 2003). NICE CG61 (2008) gives first guideline recognition.
- **2011–2019:** Replication across Sweden, Austria, Netherlands (paediatric) and Australia. Group and nurse delivery. Large Dutch IMAGINE trial (2019).
- **2018–2026:** Digital wave (Nerva ~2018/19; Regulora De Novo 2021). Endorsement by ACG and BSG (2021). Video delivery spread during the COVID era (Noble 2022 reports patient satisfaction with remote delivery). Several network meta-analyses (2020, 2024, 2025, 2026). The ISH ran a 2026 feature asking whether gut-directed hypnotherapy "should be the treatment of choice." Social-media "gut-brain" content raised lay awareness.
- **Quantitative anchors:** Manchester audit 1,000 patients (2015). Trials enrolled >3,000 participants in total (Thakur 2025 counts 1,507 adults across 12 trials, excluding paediatric and digital trials). Clinical reach of therapist-delivered gut-directed hypnotherapy is likely tens of thousands worldwide; digital programs add more (see Nerva).

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- The Cochrane review (2007) judged the evidence inadequate. Later network meta-analyses still rate certainty low or very low.
- Developer allegiance (Manchester, UNC) and reliance on uncontrolled audits.
- Hypnotisability and expectancy are possible non-specific drivers. Hypnosis is not clearly superior to relaxation or education in some trials (Lowén 2013; Regulora primary endpoint).
- Access: few trained therapists. NICE restricts it to refractory cases after 12 months, which critics regard as too late.
- Rare adverse events (e.g. a panic attack in a meta-analysis: 1 of 238).
- Some patients resist "hypnosis," worrying that it implies the illness is "psychological."

## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Framework.** Disabling LBP is described as a **multidimensional, biopsychosocial** condition. Tissue sensitivity or pathology may be present but rarely explains persistent disability. Pain persists through **self-reinforcing "vicious cycles"** that link:
  - **Cognitions:** beliefs that the back is damaged or fragile and must be protected; catastrophising; low pain self-efficacy.
  - **Emotions:** fear, anxiety, low mood, stress.
  - **Protective behaviours:** muscle guarding and bracing, breath-holding, slow and stiff "protected" movement, avoiding bending and lifting, resting.
  - **Lifestyle:** inactivity, poor sleep, stress, weight.
  - **Social and contextual factors:** work, beliefs passed on by clinicians, imaging findings.
- **Neurobiology.** Pain is treated as a protective output of the nervous system. The model acknowledges peripheral and central sensitisation as possible contributors, but frames the modifiable targets as cognitive, emotional and behavioural. In the "From fear to safety" roadmap (Caneiro et al., *Phys Ther* 2022), recovery means moving the person from a threat-and-protection state to safety and confidence.
- **Earlier version (2005–2013).** The original "classification-based" model placed more weight on **maladaptive motor control and movement patterns**: flexion, active-extension, passive-extension, lateral-shift and multidirectional patterns that provoke and maintain pain. Evoolve's FAQ ("What happened to the movement classification system?") indicates the rigid classification was later de-emphasised in favour of a person-centred, multidimensional approach.
- **Mechanistic tests.** In RESTORE, the mediators were self-efficacy, fear, catastrophising and early pain reduction. Together they explained up to about 61–62% of CFT's effects on disability and pain (Schütze et al., *Behav Res Ther* 2025). Changes in forward-bending and lifting kinematics also tracked improvement (several 2024–2025 secondary papers).

## 2. One mechanism or many?
- **One framework, individualised per person rather than per diagnosis.** CFT does not posit a disorder-specific mechanism. The clinician builds an individual profile of which cognitive, emotional, movement, lifestyle and social contributors dominate for that person.
- **Mostly low back pain, extended to other musculoskeletal pains.** Case series, single-case experiments and small RCTs apply the same framework to:
  - neck pain
  - knee osteoarthritis
  - pelvic girdle pain
  - shoulder pain
  - post-surgical back pain
  - adolescent athletes
- **Not a cross-syndrome brain model.** Unlike PRT, DNRS or ANS Rewire, CFT does not claim to treat fatigue, dysautonomia or chemical-sensitivity syndromes. Red flags and specific pathology are screened out, and the "bio" component is explicitly retained (FAQ: "Does CFT ignore the bio and pathology?").

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
Three interlocking components, as described in O'Sullivan et al. 2018:
1. **Making sense of pain (reflective education).**
   - Built from the patient's own story and from in-session experiences, not didactic lectures.
   - The clinician uses open, reflective questioning (motivational-interviewing style) to elicit the person's beliefs, fears and goals.
   - The patient is helped to see how their beliefs, emotions, behaviours and lifestyle form a cycle that maintains pain and disability.
   - Unhelpful beliefs about imaging findings and "damage" are reframed.
2. **Exposure with control (functional behaviour change).**
   - Graded, guided exposure to feared or provocative movements and valued activities: bending, lifting, sitting, running, sport.
   - Done while teaching the patient to drop protective behaviours. Examples: relaxed diaphragmatic breathing instead of breath-holding; releasing abdominal and back-muscle bracing; moving with relaxed, normal speed and spine motion.
   - Uses behavioural experiments ("what happens if you bend without bracing?"), mirror or video feedback, and progressive integration into daily tasks.
   - In RESTORE, one arm added wearable **movement-sensor biofeedback** (ViMove2). It gave no added benefit.
3. **Lifestyle change.**
   - Personalised plans for physical activity (enjoyable, graded), sleep, stress management, social re-engagement, diet and weight, and work.
   - Includes a flare-up plan for self-management.
- **Dose:**
  - **RESTORE:** up to 7 sessions over 12 weeks, plus a booster at 26 weeks.
  - **O'Keeffe 2020:** mean of 5 sessions.
  - **Castro 2022:** 5 one-hour sessions over 8 weeks.
  - **de Lira 2025:** 6 one-hour sessions.
  - **Format:** first session about 60 min, follow-ups shorter. Daily home practice of the functional exercises is expected.
- **For psychologically complex cases** (Danish "CFT+"), CFT was combined with psychologist support.
- **Clinician training is substantial.** Evoolve argues that competency requires workshops, mentored practice and assessment, not just a course. Thiveos 2024 flags variable training as a source of heterogeneity in results.

## 4. Roots and influences
- **Manipulative and musculoskeletal physiotherapy:** motor-control and "spinal stability" research of the 1990s–2000s, and O'Sullivan's PhD work on specific stabilising exercise.
- **Cognitive-behavioural therapy and behavioural psychology:**
  - the fear-avoidance model (Vlaeyen, Linton)
  - graded exposure and behavioural experiments
  - self-efficacy (Bandura)
  - motivational interviewing
- **Third-wave and mindfulness-style elements:** body relaxation, awareness, acceptance of sensations.
- **Pain science:** gate control and neuromatrix ideas; pain neuroscience education (Moseley and Butler, *Explain Pain*). The FAQ contrasts CFT with PNE, ACT and CBT: CFT integrates cognitive work with real-time functional movement exposure.
- **Biopsychosocial model** (Engel; Waddell, *The Back Pain Revolution*).

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Academic and clinical mainstream.**
  - Published in the *Lancet* (2023) and *Lancet Rheumatology* (2025), with an editorial in each journal.
  - Featured in *Nature Reviews Rheumatology* and *J Physiotherapy* critically appraised papers, and in a 2025 JAMA news piece.
  - Taught by university-affiliated trainers in Australia, Europe, the UK and Brazil.
  - An NHS feasibility RCT (Newton et al., *Physiotherapy* 2024) found NHS physios could be trained to fidelity and proposed a 540-person cluster RCT.
  - It is used in Danish secondary care (Ussing, Vaegter) and has been described in UK military rehabilitation (BMJ Mil Health 2020).
- **Guidelines.**
  - No major guideline names CFT specifically.
  - NICE NG59 (2016) recommends combined physical and psychological programmes with a CBT approach for LBP.
  - Australian LBP clinical care standards and the WHO 2023 LBP guideline favour biopsychosocial, active care. CFT is an exemplar of these but is not itself a guideline recommendation.
- **Insurance.**
  - Reimbursed only as standard physiotherapy (no CFT-specific code).
  - Australia: private-health extras rebates and Medicare chronic-disease allied-health items. RESTORE investigators had support from the Insurance Commission of WA for a related trial.
  - Scandinavian public systems; US PT CPT codes (therapeutic exercise, neuromuscular re-education).
- **Cost-effectiveness.** RESTORE found both CFT arms dominant over usual care: more QALYs and lower societal costs (−AU$5,276 and −AU$8,211 per person over 1 year). The Danish CFT+ trial estimated an ICER of €53,075/QALY versus the interdisciplinary programme, which is not clearly cost-effective.

## 6. Evidence base
**RCTs of CFT itself** (all verified on PubMed):

| Trial | Design / N | Comparator | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vibe Fersum et al., *Eur J Pain* 2013;17:916 (PMID 23208945) | RCT, N=121, Norway | Manual therapy + exercise | 12 months: ODI improved 13.7 vs 5.5 points; pain improved 3.2 vs 1.5. CFT superior on both. |
| Vibe Fersum et al., *Eur J Pain* 2019;23:1416 (PMID 30974479) | 3-year follow-up of the same trial; only ~50% retained | Same | Disability 6.6 ODI points lower (d≈0.70); no pain difference. Less anxiety/depression and work-related fear. |
| O'Keeffe et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2020;54:782 (PMID 31630089) | Multicentre RCT, N=206, Ireland | Group exercise + education | Disability better at 6 and 12 months (MD ≈ 8.7 and 7.0 ODI points); **no difference in pain**. |
| Javdaneh et al., *Clin Rehabil* 2020 (PMID 32660261) | RCT, N=72, Iran, neck pain | Scapular exercise alone vs with CFT | CFT add-on reduced pain and kinesiophobia at 6 weeks. Effect sizes implausibly large. |
| Castro et al., *Pain* 2022;163:2430 (PMID 35384931) | RCT, N=148, Brazil | Core exercise + manual therapy | 8 weeks: disability better (MD −4.75 ODI, not clinically important); **no pain difference**. |
| **Kent et al., *Lancet* 2023;401:1866 (RESTORE; PMID 37146623)** | 3-arm phase-3 RCT, N=492, 20 Australian clinics; unblinded | Usual care vs CFT vs CFT + sensor biofeedback | RMDQ −4.6 points for both CFT arms at 13 weeks, sustained at 52 weeks. More QALYs and lower societal cost. |
| **Hancock et al., *Lancet Rheumatol* 2025;7:e789 (RESTORE 3-year; PMID 40780241)** | Follow-up of 312/492 (62–65% per arm; 312 of the 359 who consented at 1 year) | Same | RMDQ −3.5 and −4.1 vs usual care; pain −1.0 and −1.5 (0–10 scale). Biofeedback added nothing significant. |
| Ahmad et al., *BMC Musculoskelet Disord* 2023 (PMID 37644472) | RCT, N=91, Iran; retrospectively registered | Movement-system-impairment treatment | Superior on pain, ODI, kinesiophobia and gait. Effect sizes (ES >2) implausibly large. |
| Avila et al., *Phys Ther* 2024;104:pzad105 (PMID 37548608) | RCT, N=80, Brazil, LBP after spinal surgery | Core exercise + manual therapy | Pain MD 2.42 (d=0.85) and function MD 2.47 (d=0.95) favouring CFT after treatment (about 10 weeks); smaller but maintained at 22 weeks (d=0.68 and 0.81). Large effects drew critical letters (Phys Ther 2025). |
| Newton et al., *Physiotherapy* 2024 (PMID 38479068) | Feasibility RCT, N=60, UK NHS | Usual physio | Feasible, delivered with fidelity; 53% retained at 12 months. Not powered for efficacy. |
| de Lira et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2025;59:912 (PMID 40049758) | **Sham-controlled** RCT, N=152, Brazil public primary care | Neutral talk + detuned laser | Pain MD −1.8 (0–10) and disability MD −9.9 (ODI) post-intervention; sustained at 3 and 6 months. |
| Vaegter et al., *JOSPT* 2026;56:596 (PMID 42676088) | Patient-blinded pragmatic RCT, N=133, Denmark, severe disabling LBP referred to a pain centre; CFT+ (CFT plus psychologist) for 3 months with optional IPMP afterwards | Interdisciplinary pain programme (IPMP) alone | **Null at 12 months** (ODI data for only 67–70%): 22% vs 18% reached an 8-point ODI improvement (P=.64); no mean ODI or secondary differences; ICER €53,075/QALY. |

**Secondary analyses:**
- RESTORE mediation (Schütze et al., *Behav Res Ther* 2025): self-efficacy, fear and catastrophising mediated about 60% of the effect.
- RESTORE opioid analysis (Thiveos et al., *Eur J Pain* 2026): **no reduction** in opioids dispensed.
- Patients with worse baseline disability responded best (*J Physiother* 2024).

**Systematic reviews:**
- Devonshire et al., *JOSPT* 2023 (PMID 36812100), pre-RESTORE: 15 trials identified (9 ongoing, 1 terminated), 5 with data (n=507), all at high risk of bias. Very-low-certainty evidence; concluded CFT "may not be more effective" than common alternatives.
- Thiveos et al., *Phys Ther* 2024 (PMID 39236249), co-authored by the developers: 7 trials. Low-to-moderate certainty of benefit for disability and pain; high certainty for self-efficacy.
- Zhang et al., *Int J Nurs Stud* 2024 (PMID 38219428), independent: 8 RCTs; benefits for disability, pain and fear-avoidance but all at **very low** GRADE certainty with extreme heterogeneity (I² ~95%).
- Niederer et al., *JOSPT* 2026;56:16 (PMID 41476427), independent network meta-analysis of 58 RCTs (n=10,510) of tailored exercise therapies: CFT alone or with biofeedback ranked most effective vs usual care (e.g. disability SMD −1.00 for CFT+biofeedback; moderate certainty), though overall risk of bias was high and the ranking leans heavily on RESTORE.
- Kandeel et al., *J Back Musculoskelet Rehabil* 2025 (PMID 39302354): 7 RCTs; consistent disability benefit, more variable pain benefit.
- Marelli et al., *Pain Med* 2025 (PMID 39799511): 8 LBP RCTs (N=1,228) plus 1 neck-pain RCT. Short-term disability MD −9.4 and pain MD −1.6 versus other conservative care, but low or very-low certainty. Long-term benefit over other active treatments not maintained.

**Gaps and caveats:**
- **Investigator allegiance.** The large positive trials include the developers as authors. RESTORE disclosures note that O'Sullivan and Caneiro direct Body Logic and Evoolve, though they state they draw no income from Evoolve. The Brazilian trials (Meziat-Filho group) are the most independent positive replications.
- **No blinding** of patients or therapists in most trials. Comparators differ in therapist enthusiasm and time.
- Effects are consistently stronger for **disability than for pain**.
- RESTORE 3-year data cover about 63% of participants.
- The Iranian trials report implausibly large effects (a published letter by Castro et al., *Clin Rehabil* 2021, questioned the Javdaneh neck-pain results).
- The 2026 Danish null result suggests CFT is not superior to an existing interdisciplinary programme for the most severe cases.
- Almost all evidence is in back pain. Evidence for neck, knee and shoulder pain is limited to single small RCTs, protocols or case designs.

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- **Allegiance and commercial-interest concerns.** The developers run the clinic and the training enterprise and lead most positive trials. The Devonshire 2023 review's skeptical conclusion contrasts with the developer-authored Thiveos 2024 review.
- **"Isn't it just good practice?"** Critics argue CFT is a repackaging of CBT, graded exposure and active physio. Evoolve's FAQ addresses this directly.
- **Pain versus disability.** Several trials improve function without reducing pain.
- **Implementation and scalability.** CFT needs intensive training and competency assessment, and effects may depend on therapist skill (heterogeneity noted by Thiveos 2024).
- **Null results** against an interdisciplinary programme (Denmark 2026) and on opioid use.
- **Contrast with other programs in this survey.** It makes no unfalsifiable cross-syndrome claims and no claims for fatigue or sensitivity syndromes, which makes it among the more conventional, evidence-oriented programs surveyed.

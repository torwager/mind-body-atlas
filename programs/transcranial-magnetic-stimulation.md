# Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS): NeuroStar, BrainsWay Deep TMS, SAINT/SNT

*Compiled 2026-10-12 from neurostar.com, brainsway.com, magnusmedical.com, Wikipedia (TMS history), and peer-reviewed abstracts retrieved via PubMed/Europe PMC. Content paraphrased. Note: the web-search budget ran out during compilation, so some commercial figures (prices, company metrics) come from background knowledge and are flagged "approx."; every trial listed was checked against PubMed/Europe PMC.*

## 1. Proposed mechanism(s)
- **Physical principle.** A rapidly changing magnetic field from a coil on the scalp induces electric currents in the cortex 2–4 cm deep (deeper with the H-coil). These currents depolarise neurons. Repeated trains are thought to cause LTP-like changes at high frequency or with intermittent theta-burst stimulation (excitatory), and LTD-like changes at ≤1 Hz or with continuous theta-burst (inhibitory). The effects spread trans-synaptically through connected networks.
- **Depression model (circuit dysfunction).** Depression is framed as hypoactivity of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) plus abnormal coupling with limbic regions, especially the subgenual anterior cingulate (sgACC). Stimulating the left DLPFC is meant to restore top-down regulation of limbic and affective circuits. SAINT/SNT picks the DLPFC spot that is most anticorrelated with the sgACC on resting-state fMRI, then delivers 1,800-pulse iTBS sessions in an accelerated schedule. The rationale is that spaced repetition consolidates plasticity.
- **Pain model.** Chronic pain is framed as maladaptive central reorganisation and **central sensitisation**, with deficient descending inhibition. High-frequency stimulation of the primary motor cortex (M1) is thought to activate thalamic, cingulate, insular and periaqueductal-grey pathways, engaging endogenous opioid and descending modulatory systems and restoring intracortical inhibition. Mhalla 2011 tied analgesia to changes in intracortical inhibition. DLPFC targets are used for the affective/cognitive side of pain.
- **Fibromyalgia.** Treated as a central-sensitisation/nociplastic condition. M1 rTMS targets pain; DLPFC targets mood and fatigue.
- **Not claimed.** No limbic-retraining, trauma, emotion-repression or "stuck ANS" model. The framing is conventional systems neuroscience: network dysregulation plus plasticity.

## 2. One mechanism or many?
**Differentiated by condition.** The shared premise is that dysfunctional brain circuits can be modulated non-invasively. Each indication, though, gets its own target and parameters:

| Condition | Typical target / parameters |
|---|---|
| Depression | Left DLPFC HF (10 Hz) or iTBS; right DLPFC LF; SAINT fMRI-guided accelerated iTBS |
| OCD (BrainsWay) | Medial PFC/ACC with H7 coil, after symptom provocation |
| Smoking | Lateral PFC/insula with H4 coil, after cue exposure |
| Neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia, CRPS | M1 contralateral to pain (HF 10–20 Hz), sometimes with neuronavigation; DLPFC is less effective for pain (Attal 2021) |
| Migraine | Separate single-pulse occipital device (eNeura sTMS, FDA 2013), aborting cortical spreading depression |

The field does not treat ME/CFS, long COVID, POTS, MCS or IBS as one shared "brain" illness. These appear only in small exploratory studies or case reports.

## 3. Treatments and self-care strategies
- **Acute course (standard rTMS, NeuroStar).** Motor threshold is found first. 10 Hz at 120% of motor threshold, ~3,000 pulses/session (older protocol 37.5 min; newer "TouchPoint"/Dash protocols as short as ~19 min). 5 days/week for 6 weeks, then a 3-week taper; ~36 sessions in all. The patient is awake, needs no anaesthesia, and can drive afterwards.
- **Theta-burst (iTBS).** 600 pulses in ~3 min. Non-inferior to 10 Hz in the THREE-D trial, which allows higher clinic throughput.
- **Deep TMS (BrainsWay H-coil).** ~20-min sessions, 18–20 Hz, over 4–6 weeks. For OCD and smoking, symptom or cue provocation comes before stimulation. An FDA-cleared accelerated protocol was added later.
- **SAINT/SNT.** fMRI to individualise the target, then 10 × 10-min iTBS sessions per day spaced 50 min apart, for 5 consecutive days (50 sessions, ~90,000 pulses). Patients are often seen inpatient or in day-hospital settings.
- **Pain protocols (research/specialist).** M1 HF-rTMS: 5–10 daily induction sessions, then weekly-to-monthly maintenance. Benefit fades when sessions stop (Mhalla 2011; Silva 2025).
- **Maintenance.** Booster sessions or repeat courses for relapse.
- **Self-care.** None intrinsic to the device. It is given alongside medications and/or psychotherapy, and some clinics pair it with exercise or CBT. Risks include headache, scalp discomfort and a rare seizure risk (≈1 in 30,000–60,000 sessions; approx.).

## 4. Roots and influences
- 19th-century electromagnetism (Faraday) and **ECT** (1938), which established brain stimulation for depression. rTMS was designed as a seizure-free alternative.
- Transcranial electrical stimulation (Merton and Morton 1980), followed by Barker's magnetic stimulator (1985), first used as a diagnostic tool for motor-evoked potentials.
- Neurophysiology of LTP/LTD and synaptic plasticity (Bliss and Lømo), and the theta-burst paradigm (Huang and Rothwell 2005).
- Functional neuroimaging of depression circuits (Mayberg; Drevets) and connectivity-guided targeting (Fox 2012), which underpin SAINT.
- Pain neuroscience: invasive motor cortex stimulation for central pain (Tsubokawa 1991), central sensitisation, and the gate control/neuromatrix tradition.
- No contemplative, somatic or psychodynamic roots.

## 5. Mainstream adoption and insurance
- **Depression.** Fully mainstream in the US, Canada, Australia, Israel and much of Europe. Recommended in the APA 2010 guideline and in CANMAT 2016 (first-line for patients who failed ≥1 antidepressant). NICE IPG542 (2015) allows rTMS with normal arrangements. Used in VA/DoD and academic medical centres. Accelerated protocols (SAINT) are moving into US hospitals.
- **Pain.** The European IFCN-led guidelines (Lefaucheur et al., 2020) give **Level A** to HF M1-rTMS for neuropathic pain and **Level B** for fibromyalgia. The NeuPSIG-linked meta-analysis (Soliman et al., *Lancet Neurol* 2025) gives a weak, third-line recommendation for rTMS in neuropathic pain. It is used in French and other European pain centres but rarely in US pain clinics.
- **Insurance.** US commercial insurers began covering TRD in 2011–2013 (Anthem, Kaiser and some BCBS plans first), and most now do. Medicare covers it through regional LCDs. Codes are CPT 90867 (initial with motor threshold), 90868 (subsequent) and 90869 (threshold re-determination). Prior authorisation usually requires documented failure of several antidepressant trials and psychotherapy. Coverage for OCD is growing. Pain and fibromyalgia indications are generally uncovered in the US. In the UK, NHS access is limited mainly by equipment and centre costs.

## 6. Evidence base
**Depression (device-specific pivotal trials and large RCTs):**
- O'Reardon et al., *Biol Psychiatry* 2007 (NeuroStar pivotal; industry-sponsored). Multisite double-blind sham RCT, N=301. Active TMS beat sham on HAMD/MADRS at weeks 4–6, though the primary MADRS result needed a post hoc baseline adjustment. Remission roughly doubled. Dropout for adverse events was 4.5%.
- George et al., *Arch Gen Psychiatry* 2010 (NIH-funded, independent of the company). Sham-controlled, N=199 (ITT 190). Remission 14.1% vs 5.1% (OR 4.2; NNT 12).
- Levkovitz et al., *World Psychiatry* 2015 (BrainsWay H-coil pivotal). Sham-controlled, N=212. Week-5 response 38.4% vs 21.4%, remission 32.6% vs 14.6%; effects sustained over 12 weeks.
- Blumberger et al., *Lancet* 2018 (THREE-D; academic). Non-inferiority RCT, N=414. 3-min iTBS was non-inferior to 37-min 10 Hz rTMS (HRSD-17 fell from about 23.5 to 13.4 in both arms).
- Cole et al., *Am J Psychiatry* 2020 (SAINT; Stanford). Open-label, N=22. Remission in 19/21 with outcome data (90.5%) after 5 days; 19/22 (86.4%) on intention-to-treat.
- Cole et al., *Am J Psychiatry* 2022 (SNT; Stanford, Williams senior author). Double-blind sham RCT reported at a planned interim analysis (32 enrolled, 29 randomised: 14 active, 15 sham). MADRS fell 52.5% vs 11.1%. Small sample; durability not established. The ~90% remission figure comes from the open-label study, not this RCT.
- Yesavage et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2018 (VA Cooperative Studies Program; independent). Double-blind sham RCT in 9 VA centres, N=164 veterans with TRD, 10 Hz left DLPFC up to 30 sessions. Remission did not differ between active and sham (~41% vs ~37%). This is an important null result for standard rTMS, with high response in both arms.

**Chronic pain / fibromyalgia (generic rTMS, mostly academic and largely French and Brazilian groups):**
- Passard et al., *Brain* 2007. Sham RCT of M1 rTMS in fibromyalgia, N=30. Widespread pain fell from the 5th session and the effect lasted ~2 weeks after treatment ended.
- Mhalla et al., *Pain* 2011. Sham RCT, N=40 randomised (30 completed), with maintenance sessions. Pain reduction lasted to week 25 and correlated with restored intracortical inhibition.
- Attal et al., *Brain* 2021. Multicentre sham RCT in peripheral neuropathic pain, N=152. M1 rTMS beat sham; DLPFC rTMS did not.
- Silva et al., *Br J Anaesth* 2025. Multicentre sham RCT of add-on M1 rTMS in fibromyalgia, N=101 women. 50% pain responders more likely at week 8 (NNT ≈4.5; relative pain reduction 40.4% vs 18.4%). Analgesia waned by week 16; FIQ function gains persisted.
- Toh et al., *Psychosom Med* 2022. Meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (N=303) in fibromyalgia. Pain SMD −0.35 and quality-of-life SMD −0.51 vs sham. No effect on depression or anxiety. M1 stimulation drove the pain benefit (SMD −0.57).
- Soliman et al., *Lancet Neurol* 2025 (NeuPSIG update). Meta-analysis of 313 trials (48,789 participants) of neuropathic pain treatments. rTMS NNT 4.2 (95% CI 2.3–28.3; low certainty); weak third-line recommendation, alongside BTX-A and opioids.
- O'Connell et al., *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2018 (CD008208). Non-invasive brain stimulation for chronic pain. rTMS vs sham (27 studies, 655 participants): small short-term effect (SMD −0.22, ≈7% pain reduction), low-quality evidence. No evidence for low-frequency or DLPFC rTMS. Blinding and bias limit confidence.
- Lefaucheur et al., *Clin Neurophysiol* 2020. Evidence-based guideline: Level A for depression (left DLPFC HF) and neuropathic pain (M1 HF); Level B for fibromyalgia.

**Other target conditions (sparse):**
- IBS: Melchior et al., *Colorectal Dis* 2014. Small randomised trial on rectal sensitivity.
- Functional (FND) paresis: Broersma et al., *Eur J Neurol* 2015. Small trial on muscle strength.
- Long COVID: a single case report (Zhang et al., *Mil Med* 2023) using a proprietary EEG-guided "Electro-Magnetic Brain Pulse" variant, not standard rTMS.
- No adequately powered RCTs in ME/CFS, POTS, MCS/IEI, EHS, chronic Lyme or mould illness.

**Gaps and critiques.** Sham blinding is imperfect because of scalp sensation and clicking. Pivotal trials were industry-sponsored. Effect sizes against sham in depression are moderate, and real-world remission figures cited by companies (e.g. NeuroStar's "62% remission" from CGI-S registry subsets) are uncontrolled. Pain effects are short-lived without maintenance.

## 7. Popularity and trajectory
- 1985–2007: research tool. Hundreds of small depression studies from 1995 on.
- 2008: NeuroStar FDA clearance. Slow uptake until insurers began covering it around 2011–2013.
- 2013–2019: rapid growth as BrainsWay entered (2013), coverage spread and clinic chains appeared (e.g. Greenbrook TMS, Success TMS). OCD clearance in 2018.
- 2018–2022: theta-burst cut session time. SAINT drew heavy media attention (2020–2022 coverage of "remission in 5 days") and was cleared in 2022.
- 2023–2026: consolidation. Neuronetics acquired Greenbrook TMS (reported completion Dec 2024; not re-verified in fact-check); adolescent indication added; accelerated protocols spreading.
- Indicators: NeuroStar reports >9 million treatments in >250,000 patients (neurostar.com, 2026). BrainsWay lists >800 providers. The r/TMS subreddit hosts active patient discussion. "TMS therapy" US search interest rose steadily from about 2012 (qualitative description; not verified numerically).
- Global cumulative patients across all devices are plausibly ~1–2 million (approx.; an estimate, not a published figure).

## 8. Controversies / criticisms
- Modest effects against sham; a large independent VA trial (Yesavage 2018, N=164) found no active–sham difference. There is debate about how much of the response is placebo or expectancy, given the intensive daily clinic contact.
- Concerns about sham adequacy and industry funding of pivotal trials. NeuroStar's primary endpoint needed post hoc adjustment.
- SAINT's ~90% remission figure comes from open-label data. The RCT was small and stopped early. High cost and limited access.
- Commercial clinics are marketing rTMS off-label (anxiety, autism, PTSD, long COVID, "brain optimisation") ahead of the evidence.
- Pain benefits fade without ongoing maintenance sessions, which raises cost-effectiveness concerns.
- Rare seizures. Insurance step-therapy requirements delay access.
